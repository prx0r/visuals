# Imagination as Ontological Operation

## Śaiva *Svātantrya*, Suhrawardian Illumination, Ibn ʿArabī’s *Barzakh*, and the Corbinian Imaginal

### Abstract

Modern accounts ordinarily place imagination on the subjective side of a subject–object divide. The world exists independently; perception receives it with varying accuracy; imagination recombines stored representations into objects that need not exist. The four bodies of thought examined here reject that architecture. In Pratyabhijñā Śaivism, imagination discloses consciousness’s *svātantrya*: its power to manifest a determinate object without awaiting an external prompt. In Suhrawardī’s Illuminationism, “suspended images” possess an objective mode of existence between intelligible light and material body. In Ibn ʿArabī, *khayāl* is both a faculty and a cosmic principle: the *barzakh* that joins and differentiates Being and nonexistence, spirit and body, transcendence and theophanic form. Henry Corbin rearticulates these Islamic traditions through the *mundus imaginalis*, understood as a real order corresponding to an organ of visionary perception and a hermeneutics of spiritual individuation.

The convergence is substantial but not an identity. Utpaladeva locates the paradigmatic imaginative act within self-manifest consciousness and ultimately identifies every object with that consciousness’s free manifestation. Suhrawardī posits a vertically ordered plurality of lights and an autonomous imaginal domain not reducible to the human mind. Ibn ʿArabī universalizes imagination as the ambiguous ontological status of the cosmos itself. Corbin defends the irreducibility of the imaginal relation while resisting both reductive psychologism and undifferentiated monism. Their shared insight is not simply that images can be “real.” It is that manifestation occurs through an intermediate operation in which an unmanifest or indeterminate source becomes present in a form without being exhausted by it.

This essay proposes that the most fruitful formal synthesis is not a single imaginal substance but a **barzakhic operation of reflexive manifestation**. Imagination generates a relation in which subject and object emerge as poles of one event. Śaiva *vimarśa* names that event’s reflexive self-articulation; *camatkāra* names its felt self-relish; Ibn ʿArabī’s *barzakh* names its simultaneous joining and differentiating function; Corbin’s *unus-ambo* names the irreducible bi-unity of its poles. This synthesis illuminates the aperture model while leaving open the decisive metaphysical question: does the imaginal disclose one universal consciousness, an objective hierarchy of intermediate realities, one act of Being, or a participatory relation irreducible to all three?

---

## 1. The central claim

Imagination is normally defined negatively. It is what occurs when perception is absent, when an object is represented without presently affecting the senses, or when previously acquired elements are rearranged into a novel fiction. On that model:

[
\text{world}
\rightarrow
\text{perception}
\rightarrow
\text{stored representations}
\rightarrow
\text{imagination}.
]

Imagination arrives last. It depends upon an already formed world, an already individuated subject, and a stock of received content. Its productions may matter psychologically, artistically, or culturally, but they possess no constitutive role in ontology.

The traditions examined here reverse this order. They regard imagination—or a cognate imaginal operation—not simply as the reproduction of absent objects but as a power through which reality becomes **form-bearing, relational, and present**. Their shared structural thesis can be stated:

[
\boxed{
\text{Imagination is an operation by which a source}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{that exceeds determinate form becomes present as form}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{without being reduced to that form.}
}
]

This definition has four parts.

First, imagination is **productive**. It does not merely retrieve a finished form.

Second, it is **manifestational**. Its product is not only internally encoded; it appears.

Third, it is **intermediate**. The image is neither identical with its source nor wholly independent of it.

Fourth, it is **participatory**. To imagine in the strong sense is not to inspect a private copy but to enter or enact a relation through which source, image, and knower become mutually articulate.

The convergence must nevertheless be stated historically with care. Utpaladeva, Suhrawardī, and Ibn ʿArabī did not formulate identical doctrines, and Corbin did not independently discover a fourth version. Corbin’s *mundus imaginalis* is explicitly generated through his readings of Iranian Illuminationism, Avicennan visionary narratives, Ibn ʿArabī, Shīʿī angelology, and related traditions. Suhrawardī and Ibn ʿArabī also belong to overlapping Islamic intellectual worlds, even though their imaginal theories differ significantly. The result is not four independent confirmations of one proposition but four increasingly complex answers to a recurring problem:

[
\boxed{
\text{How can an invisible, intelligible, or transcendent}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{reality genuinely appear in finite form?}
}
]

The project brief correctly identifies this family resemblance but sometimes states it too strongly as doctrinal identity.

## 2. The problem each tradition is solving

### 2.1 Pratyabhijñā: how can consciousness become a world?

The Śaiva problem begins with nondual consciousness. If ultimate reality is one self-manifest and self-determining consciousness, why does it appear as many finite knowers confronting independently existing objects?

A static consciousness cannot answer this. If it is merely an undifferentiated light, then difference must arrive from something outside it. But anything genuinely outside consciousness could never become manifest without already entering consciousness. Pratyabhijñā therefore characterizes consciousness not only as *prakāśa*, manifestation, but as *vimarśa*, reflexive and determinative self-apprehension, and *svātantrya*, freedom.

The world becomes possible because consciousness is capable of:

[
\boxed{
\text{manifesting itself otherwise without ceasing}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{to be itself.}
}
]

Imagination is philosophically privileged because it displays this capacity in ordinary life. An imagined object can appear without an external object presently compelling consciousness to assume its form. In the celebrated argument around ĪPK I.6.10–11, imagination is described as moving freely or autonomously, self-prompted, and capable of manifesting a desired configuration. Utpaladeva treats this not as a marginal faculty but as evidence that the powers of knowledge and action operate in every sentient being. Ratié shows that, for Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta, even a novel imagined configuration cannot be reduced to passive retrieval: consciousness transforms its materials into an organically unified object and thereby exhibits, in contracted form, the same creativity attributed to divine consciousness.

The Śaiva answer is:

[
C_{\infty}
\overset{\delta_i}{\longrightarrow}
\langle
S_i,O_i,R_i
\rangle,
]

where one self-manifest consciousness freely determines itself as finite subject, object, and their relation.

Imagination is what this freedom feels like from within a finite aperture.

---

### 2.2 Suhrawardī: how can light cross into form?

Suhrawardī’s problem is not initially the manifestation of many objects within one universal consciousness. It is the relation between ontologically graded orders.

At the summit is the Light of Lights. Below it are hierarchies of immaterial lights, including dominating lights and the “lords of species.” At the lower end lies the world of bodies or barriers. The soul itself is a managing light associated with a body. Reality is thus ordered through degrees of luminosity, dependence, domination, love, self-subsistence, and obscuration.

The difficulty is that pure intelligible realities lack material extension, while sensible bodies possess shape, spatiality, and physical opacity. How can a spiritual being appear as a determinate figure? How can postmortem realities, dreams, prophetic visions, mirror images, and visionary encounters possess form without ordinary material substrata?

Suhrawardī introduces “suspended” or self-subsisting images:

[
m_k
\neq
\text{material body},
]

[
m_k
\neq
\text{pure universal intellect},
]

yet:

[
m_k
\text{ possesses determinate form}.
]

The imaginal order is therefore not a subjective cinema. It is a mode of reality in which form exists without ordinary material inherence. Suhrawardī’s treatment is not a complete later systematic cosmology; subsequent Illuminationist commentators considerably elaborate the autonomous “discontinuous imaginal world.” Nonetheless, his *Philosophy of Illumination* launches the doctrine of objective suspended images and a fourth ontological order between pure light and matter.

The Suhrawardian answer is:

[
\text{intelligible light}
\rightarrow
\text{imaginal form}
\rightarrow
\text{material appearance}.
]

The imaginal is the ontological mediation through which light becomes visible without becoming mere matter.

---

### 2.3 Ibn ʿArabī: how can the Real be transcendent and manifest?

Ibn ʿArabī’s problem is more radical. God—the Real—is incomparable with every finite thing. Yet every finite thing is a locus of divine self-disclosure. How can a form reveal the formless without containing or limiting it?

His answer is *barzakh*: an isthmus that simultaneously separates and joins two terms. A *barzakh* is not a third item sitting between two independently completed objects. It is the relation by which each term is distinguished from and connected to the other.

If (A) and (B) are opposed, then:

[
J(A,B)
]

is barzakhic when it satisfies:

[
J\neq A,
\qquad
J\neq B,
]

but also:

[
J\text{ bears a relation to both }A\text{ and }B.
]

Ibn ʿArabī uses *barzakh* for numerous levels: the line between light and shade, the soul between spirit and body, the imaginal world between incorporeal and corporeal orders, the Perfect Human between divine and cosmic realities, and the cosmos itself between Being and nonexistence. The “Supreme Barzakh” or nondelimited imagination is the whole realm of possible things: neither necessary Being nor sheer impossibility, but the articulated possibility of manifestation.

Imagination is therefore not merely one human capacity. It is the universal ontological ambiguity of everything other than the Real:

[
\boxed{
\text{real as divine self-disclosure,}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{unreal if treated as independently self-subsisting.}
}
]

The cosmos is not false in the sense of being nothing. It is imaginal because it continually receives existence without possessing existence independently.

The Ibn ʿArabian answer is:

[
W
=

\operatorname{Barzakh}
\left(
\text{Being},
\text{nonexistence}
\right).
]

Every form is a theophany: the Real appearing according to the preparedness of a locus while remaining unrestricted by the form.

---

### 2.4 Corbin: how can modernity recover a world capable of revelation?

Corbin’s problem is hermeneutical and civilizational. Modern Western thought has tended to divide reality into:

[
\text{empirical fact}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\text{subjective fantasy}.
]

Under this dichotomy, visionary encounters, angelic forms, subtle bodies, resurrection worlds, sacred geographies, and prophetic images must either be reduced to physical events or dismissed as psychological inventions.

Corbin coins or adopts the technical Latin expression *mundus imaginalis* to protect a third possibility: a precise order of reality corresponding to a precise faculty of perception. He insists on distinguishing the imaginal from the imaginary precisely because “imaginary” commonly connotes the unreal.

The Corbinian answer is not simply:

> Images exist objectively somewhere else.

It is:

[
\boxed{
\text{a mode of reality and a mode of perception}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{arise correlatively.}
}
]

The *mundus imaginalis* is invisible to sensory perception but is not therefore unreal. It is perceptible through active imagination, which Corbin describes as an organ in the same functional sense that the eye is an organ of visible perception. The organ must be cultivated; its failure does not entail the nonexistence of its object.

Corbin’s modern project thus attempts to recover:

* an ontology of visionary form;
* an epistemology of active imagination;
* a hermeneutics of symbolic return;
* an angelology of personal vocation;
* a mode of individuation irreducible to both isolated ego and monistic absorption.

Angela Voss interprets this as a Platonic path of self-knowledge in which imaginal symbols operate neither as projections of an unconscious nor as literal physical objects, but as transformative mediations through which the soul encounters its transcendent counterpart.

## 3. Śaiva imagination: freedom manifesting itself as object

### 3.1 The autonomous imaginative act

The crucial Śaiva evidence is the asymmetry between perception and imagination.

In ordinary perception:

[
x_{\mathrm{external}}
\rightarrow
C(x).
]

The object appears to prompt or constrain the cognition.

In imagination:

[
C
\overset{\mathrm{self\ prompting}}{\longrightarrow}
C(x^\ast).
]

No presently perceived external object is needed to compel the imagined form. Utpaladeva therefore calls imagination self-prompted and describes it as capable of manifesting according to desire.

The argument does not require that imagination create from absolute nothingness. Even if it employs traces from prior perceptions, the specific whole it produces need never have been perceived. The “five-trunked, four-tusked elephant running in the sky” is not a remembered object. Moreover, as Ratié emphasizes, combination is not merely juxtaposition. The parts are transformed by their membership in one novel gestalt. The imagined elephant is not experienced as a list consisting of elephant + additional trunks + additional tusks + sky + motion. It appears as one object.

The operation is:

[
\mathcal I_i
\left(
r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_n
\right)
=======

x^\ast,
]

with:

[
x^\ast
\neq
\sum r_k.
]

Imagination displays at least three powers:

[
\text{selection},
\qquad
\text{transformation},
\qquad
\text{unification}.
]

Utpaladeva interprets these as finite expressions of consciousness’s universal capacity to manifest determinate difference.

---

### 3.2 Internal and external at once

The imagined object has no independent existence outside the imagining cognition. Yet it appears as an object, not as the bare subjectivity of the imagining act.

This gives imagination a barzakh-like structure before we introduce Ibn ʿArabī:

[
x^\ast
\text{ is internal causally or ontologically},
]

but:

[
x^\ast
\text{ is external phenomenologically}.
]

The project brief preserves Utpaladeva’s formulation that the imaginary object appears “externally on the level of the inner organ”—*bahir antaḥkaraṇabhūmau*.

The apparent paradox dissolves once “internal” and “external” are recognized as different relations.

Let:

[
\operatorname{Dep}(x^\ast,C_i)
]

mean that the object depends on consciousness (C_i), and:

[
\operatorname{Obj}(x^\ast,C_i)
]

mean that it appears to that consciousness as an object.

Then:

[
\operatorname{Dep}(x^\ast,C_i)
\land
\operatorname{Obj}(x^\ast,C_i)
]

is coherent.

Objectivity need not imply ontological independence. It can mean **determinate otherness within manifestation**.

This is one of the strongest bridges to the aperture model. A phenomenal object can be internal to one field while appearing as external to the represented knower located within that field.

---

### 3.3 Why an imagined world can be real

The Śaiva position differs sharply from any argument that moves:

[
\text{imagined}
\Rightarrow
\text{unreal}.
]

Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta compare world manifestation with imagination precisely to defend its reality as a conscious manifestation. The world is not unreal because it is imagined; it is real insofar as it is actively manifested by consciousness. Ratié identifies this as a decisive difference from interpretations that treat dreamlike or imagined status as evidence of ontological deficiency.

The relevant contrast is:

[
\text{independent reality}
\neq
\text{manifestational reality}.
]

An object lacks independent reality if:

[
x\not\perp C_{\infty}.
]

But it possesses manifestational reality if:

[
x
=

\operatorname{Determination}
\left(
C_{\infty}
\right).
]

The image is real as an act of manifestation, even though it is not self-grounding.

The danger is obvious. From the fact that an individual imagined object is dependent upon a local consciousness, it does not follow that the empirical world is dependent upon one universal consciousness. That requires the wider Pratyabhijñā arguments against external objects and for one all-encompassing subject.

Still, imagination establishes the intelligibility of the proposed relation:

[
\boxed{
\text{dependence upon consciousness}
}
]

does not entail:

[
\boxed{
\text{phenomenal nonexistence or meaninglessness}.
}
]

---

### 3.4 *Vimarśa* and *camatkāra*

The deepest Śaiva claim is not merely that consciousness can manufacture images. It is that consciousness is affectively and aesthetically present to its own manifestations.

A mirror can display a form, but it does not relish its display. An inert recording system may preserve distinctions, but it does not experience their significance. Pratyabhijñā therefore distinguishes sentience through *vimarśa*: consciousness does not merely shine; it grasps or realizes itself as manifesting.

The project’s cited ĪPK I.5.11 Vṛtti identifies *vimarśa* with *camatkāra*, a term carrying senses of astonishment, wonder, relish, and vivid aesthetic self-enjoyment. The cited ĪPK II.4.20 Vivṛti further develops freedom in terms of *camatkāra*.

Biernacki makes this connection central to her interpretation of Abhinavagupta. Wonder is not an escape from body or materiality but disclosure of subjectivity within material manifestation. She treats *camatkāra* as a concept capable of linking reflexive consciousness, embodiment, transformation, and a non-reductive account of matter.

The structure can be formalized:

[
\operatorname{Prakāśa}(x)
=========================

x\text{ is manifest},
]

[
\operatorname{Vimarśa}(x)
=========================

C\text{ apprehends itself as manifesting }x,
]

[
\operatorname{Camatkāra}(x)
===========================

\text{the felt self-presence or relish of this articulation}.
]

Thus:

[
\boxed{
\text{knowing is not neutral registration.}
}
]

It is a reflexive, valenced, aesthetic event.

---

### 3.5 The musician and nondual differentiation

The project brief points to Abhinavagupta’s vīṇā example: a musician absorbed in musical performance experiences differentiated beauty while remaining in a nondual mode of awareness. The exact pagination cited in the project corpus could not be independently checked here, so the passage should be verified against the project’s Tantrāloka edition before publication.

Its philosophical role is nevertheless clear. Nonduality does not require the elimination of variety:

[
\text{nonduality}
\neq
\text{featureless homogeneity}.
]

Musical absorption provides a model in which:

* notes remain differentiated;
* temporal relations remain structured;
* variation is essential;
* the performer is not standing outside the performance as a detached owner;
* the whole is relished as one self-articulating event.

Formally:

[
\mathcal M
==========

\langle
n_1,n_2,\ldots,n_k,R_t
\rangle,
]

where (R_t) is the temporal and harmonic relation among notes.

The aesthetic whole is not:

[
\sum n_k.
]

It is:

[
\operatorname{Gestalt}
\left(
{n_k},R_t
\right).
]

The performer’s absorption can therefore model how consciousness manifests difference without losing unity. *Camatkāra* is not pleasure added to an already completed representation; it is the field’s lived apprehension of its own meaningful form.

## 4. Suhrawardī: the imaginal as objective mediation

### 4.1 Light and presence

Suhrawardī’s philosophy begins from light because nothing is more evident than what manifests itself. Pure immaterial light is self-manifest; darkness and bodies require something else to become apparent. His epistemology of knowledge by presence accordingly treats self-awareness not as conceptual representation but as immediate presence.

Yet Suhrawardī does not identify every light with one universal consciousness. Lights form real grades:

[
L_0>L_1>L_2>\cdots>L_n,
]

where (>) signifies ontological priority and intensity.

Each lower light depends upon higher lights, loves what is above it, and may dominate what is beneath it. The Light of Lights is unique, necessary, and independent. The human soul is a managing light associated with a bodily fortress or barrier.

This is already a decisive difference from Pratyabhijñā:

[
\text{Śaiva: one consciousness appearing under restriction;}
]

[
\text{Suhrawardī: many dependent lights ordered by intensity.}
]

---

### 4.2 Why a fourth world is needed

A strictly three-level ontology might include:

1. pure intelligibles;
2. souls;
3. material bodies.

But certain phenomena do not fit.

A dream figure possesses shape but no ordinary physical body. A mirror image is visible but does not inhere in the mirror as paint inheres in a wall. A postmortem body may possess form without terrestrial matter. Prophetic visions disclose figures that are neither abstract universals nor normal sensory objects.

Suhrawardī’s “suspended images” answer this classificatory problem.

They possess:

[
\text{shape without ordinary matter},
]

[
\text{particularity without terrestrial location},
]

[
\text{visibility without standard physical inherence}.
]

Sinai’s reconstruction emphasizes that Suhrawardī develops the concept partly through mirror vision. The mirror is not the substrate in which an image inheres; it is the locus at which an image becomes manifest. This is a subtle but crucial distinction:

[
\operatorname{ManifestAt}(m,\text{mirror})
]

does not imply:

[
\operatorname{InhereIn}(m,\text{mirror}).
]

The imaginal form can therefore be objectively present without being materially located in the conventional manner.

---

### 4.3 An objective realm, but not a second material universe

Later Illuminationist tradition develops a “discontinuous imaginal world” lying between the world of pure lights and the material world. Marcotte describes Suhrawardī’s fourth world as addressing visionary, eschatological, and imaginative experiences that require objective forms without ordinary matter. Modern scholarship also cautions that later commentators systematize the doctrine beyond Suhrawardī’s own relatively compressed statements.

It is misleading to picture this as another planet behind the ninth sphere. Corbin’s language of the Eighth Clime and “land of nowhere” designates a non-geographical mode of space. Suhrawardī’s imaginal world is ontologically intermediate, not simply spatially more distant.

Let:

[
\mathcal W_S
============

\text{sensible-material world},
]

[
\mathcal W_I
============

\text{imaginal world},
]

[
\mathcal W_L
============

\text{world of pure lights}.
]

Then:

[
\mathcal W_S
\neq
\mathcal W_I
\neq
\mathcal W_L.
]

But (\mathcal W_I) shares:

* particular form with the sensible;
* immateriality with the intelligible.

It is therefore a genuine ontological *between*.

---

### 4.4 Knowledge by becoming illuminated

The project brief uses the Augustinian distinction between *cognitio vespertina* and *cognitio matutina*. These are not Suhrawardī’s own Arabic technical terms. They belong to an Augustinian and medieval Christian distinction that Corbin employs comparatively: “evening knowledge” knows created things in their own finite mode, while “morning knowledge” knows them in their divine or archetypal source.

Applied cautiously to Suhrawardī:

[
K_{\mathrm{evening}}(x)
=======================

\text{knowledge of }x\text{ as an isolated finite object},
]

while:

[
K_{\mathrm{morning}}(x)
=======================

\text{knowledge of }x\text{ through its luminous source}.
]

Illumination is not simply discursive inference to a higher cause. It is transformation in the knower’s mode of presence:

[
S_i
\rightarrow
S_i^{L}.
]

The knower becomes capable of receiving a higher light and thereby knows a lower form according to its vertical origin.

This is not identity with the Light of Lights. It is participation according to rank.

## 5. Ibn ʿArabī: imagination as the ontology of the cosmos

### 5.1 The *barzakh* is not a middle object

The most precise Ibn ʿArabian contribution is the logic of the *barzakh*.

Suppose a line separates sunlight from shadow. The line is not itself one side or the other. Yet without it, the two would not be distinguished as adjacent regions.

A *barzakh* therefore:

[
\text{separates}
\land
\text{joins}.
]

It cannot be adequately understood through either/or logic.

If:

[
J(A,B)
]

is the barzakh between (A) and (B), then:

[
J\not=A,
\qquad
J\not=B,
]

yet:

[
J
\text{ is intelligible only through both}.
]

Chittick identifies this “both/and” or “neither/nor” ambiguity as ontological rather than merely epistemic. The cosmos itself is ambiguous because it stands between the Real, which possesses Being in itself, and nonexistence, since created things possess no independent Being.

---

### 5.2 Nondelimited and delimited imagination

Ibn ʿArabī distinguishes a cosmic or nondelimited imagination from the delimited imagination of individual beings.

Nondelimited imagination includes the cosmos as a whole:

[
\mathcal K_{\infty}
===================

\operatorname{Barzakh}
\left(
\text{Being},
\text{nonexistence}
\right).
]

Delimited imagination occurs within a specific soul:

[
\mathcal K_i
============

\operatorname{ImageField}
\left(
S_i
\right).
]

The human imaginative faculty is not arbitrary precisely because it participates in the universal barzakhic structure of manifestation. Dreams, visions, and symbols can disclose realities because the faculty and cosmos share an imaginal logic.

This does not mean every fantasy is revelation. A delimited imagination can distort, combine, or misread. The epistemic problem becomes one of interpretation:

[
\text{What reality is disclosed through this image?}
]

rather than:

[
\text{Is the image merely real or unreal?}
]

---

### 5.3 Everything other than the Real is imaginal

To say that the cosmos is imagination is not to say that it is nonexistent.

Created things are real as:

[
\text{loci of manifestation},
]

but unreal as:

[
\text{independent possessors of Being}.
]

Thus each existent (x) satisfies:

[
\operatorname{Manifest}(x,\text{Real}),
]

and:

[
\neg\operatorname{SelfGrounding}(x).
]

The form is neither the Real in its nondelimited essence nor something outside the Real’s self-disclosure.

This gives every existent a double aspect:

[
x
=

\langle
\text{divine face},
\text{creaturely face}
\rangle.
]

Viewed through its divine face, it is a disclosure of Being.

Viewed through its creaturely face, it is finite, delimited, and perpetually perishing.

The cosmos is therefore renewed through continuous self-disclosure rather than established as a static collection of substances.

---

### 5.4 Theophany and preparedness

A divine self-disclosure does not appear identically in every locus. Each receiver possesses a specific preparedness:

[
p_i.
]

The disclosed form is:

[
T_i
===

\operatorname{Tajallī}
\left(
\text{Real},p_i
\right).
]

This resembles the principle that a cause is received according to the mode of the receiver.

But Ibn ʿArabī radicalizes it. The preparedness is itself included within the divine manifestation. The receiver is not an independent container standing outside the Real.

This means every image discloses both:

* something of its source;
* something of the receiving locus.

No finite theophany exhausts the Real:

[
T_i
\neq
\text{Real}_{\mathrm{unrestricted}}.
]

Yet it is not therefore false:

[
T_i
===

\text{Real as disclosed here}.
]

The imagination’s proper epistemic function is to preserve this dual truth without collapsing either side.

---

### 5.5 The Perfect Human

The Perfect Human is the most comprehensive *barzakh*: a being capable of receiving and displaying the totality of divine names while remaining a creaturely locus.

It would be inaccurate to say without qualification that God “becomes conscious of Himself” in the Perfect Human, as though God previously lacked self-knowledge. The stronger and more faithful claim is that the Perfect Human is the comprehensive mirror in which divine names and cosmic realities become manifest in an integrated form.

Let:

[
\mathbb N
=========

{N_1,N_2,\ldots,N_k}
]

be the divine names.

Ordinary beings manifest limited subsets:

[
x_i
\mapsto
{N_a,N_b}.
]

The Perfect Human integrates the names comprehensively:

[
H_{\mathrm{perfect}}
\mapsto
\mathbb N.
]

He is therefore *barzakh par excellence*:

[
\text{divine}
\leftrightarrow
H_{\mathrm{perfect}}
\leftrightarrow
\text{cosmos}.
]

The relation is one of comprehensive manifestation, not simple numerical identity.

---

### 5.6 “Unity of Being”

The phrase *waḥdat al-wujūd* became a standard later designation but was not Ibn ʿArabī’s own central slogan in the form later polemics sometimes assume. The relevant doctrine is that Being properly belongs to the Real, while created things receive and delimit its manifestation.

This differs from Śaiva nondual consciousness.

For Ibn ʿArabī:

[
\text{Being}
]

is the primary metaphysical term, though knowledge, self-disclosure, love, and imagination are inseparable from its manifestation.

For Pratyabhijñā:

[
\text{self-manifest consciousness}
]

is the ultimate reality, with Being understood through its manifestation.

The contrast can be expressed:

[
\text{Akbarian: consciousness is a mode or perfection of Being;}
]

[
\text{Śaiva: Being is inseparable from self-manifest consciousness.}
]

This is not a verbal disagreement. It concerns whether manifestness is the essence of reality or one essential dimension of the Real’s self-disclosure.

---

## 6. Corbin: the imaginal as participatory hermeneutics

### 6.1 A precise order and a precise organ

Corbin’s *mundus imaginalis* is a polemical concept aimed at a modern epistemology that recognizes only sense perception and abstract intellect.

His basic schema is:

[
\text{sensible}
\leftrightarrow
\text{imaginal}
\leftrightarrow
\text{intelligible}.
]

The imaginal world possesses:

* form;
* colour;
* figure;
* extension;
* locality;

but not in physical geographical space.

It corresponds to:

[
\operatorname{ImaginatioVera},
]

the true or active imagination.

The claim is reciprocal:

[
\boxed{
\text{no imaginal world without an imaginal organ,}
}
]

and:

[
\boxed{
\text{no imaginal organ without an order it can disclose.}
}
]

The faculty does not simply create whatever it wishes. It becomes receptive to a form of reality inaccessible to ordinary senses.

---

### 6.2 *Taʾwīl* as return

Corbin interprets *taʾwīl* through its etymological sense of carrying something back to its origin.

A literal object or scriptural statement is not discarded. It is returned through its visible or verbal form to the reality that makes that form symbolically possible.

Let:

[
s
]

be a symbol and:

[
\alpha
]

its spiritual source.

Literalism treats:

[
s=s.
]

Allegorical reduction treats:

[
s
\rightarrow
\text{an abstract concept}.
]

Imaginal hermeneutics performs:

[
s
\overset{\textit{taʾwīl}}{\longrightarrow}
\alpha
]

while preserving:

[
s
]

as the form in which (\alpha) becomes personally manifest.

The symbol is therefore not a disposable code. It is an event of relation.

---

### 6.3 The image does not belong exclusively to the subject

Corbin opposes the Cartesian assumption that an image is a private mental content contained inside a subject.

In imaginal perception:

[
\text{subject}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{image}
]

are reciprocally constituted within one event.

The image addresses the subject, demands interpretation, and transforms the one who perceives it. The subject is not sovereign over the image; neither is the image a physical object independent of every perceiver.

This is the context for Corbin’s affinity with a *unus-ambo* anthropology: not one by erasing two, and not two externally related substances, but a bi-unity in which each pole becomes what it is through relation to the other.

The formula can be represented:

[
1\times1,
]

rather than:

[
1+1
]

or:

[
1=1.
]

It is important not to fetishize the arithmetic. Its point is relational:

[
\boxed{
\text{the knower and the known remain distinct}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{while the act of knowing belongs to neither alone.}
}
]

---

### 6.4 Angelology and individuation

Corbin’s angel is not merely a species of supernatural object. The angel can be the celestial counterpart, guide, or form of the person’s unrealized spiritual individuality.

The human person is therefore not completed by dissolving into an undifferentiated absolute. Individuation occurs through encounter with a transcendent counterpart:

[
S_i
\leftrightarrow
A_i.
]

The relation discloses:

* vocation;
* orientation;
* the personal form of the divine;
* the person’s place in a spiritual cosmos.

Voss emphasizes this alchemical and theurgic dimension. The imaginal image mediates a transformation in which the soul becomes what it perceives without simply becoming numerically identical to it.

Corbin’s position is therefore closer to participation than to absolute identity:

[
S_i\neq A_i,
]

but:

[
S_i
\text{ becomes itself through relation to }A_i.
]

---

### 6.5 Shaw, Voss, and participatory knowing

Gregory Shaw’s interpretation of Iamblichean theurgy helps clarify what is at stake in Corbinian imagination. A symbol does not merely transmit information to a detached intellect. It transforms an embodied participant so that a superior form can become active through the participant. Shaw’s work emphasizes that theurgy is “divine activity” shared through embodied rites and that transformation culminates not in escape from embodiment but in its divine reconfiguration.

Voss develops a related participatory epistemology. An imaginal or symbolic form cannot be adequately known through detached explanation alone because the form’s meaning includes the transformation it performs in a suitably receptive knower.

This gives:

[
K_{\mathrm{representational}}:
S\rightarrow\widehat O,
]

versus:

[
K_{\mathrm{participatory}}:
\langle S,O,R\rangle
\rightarrow
\langle S',O',R'\rangle.
]

In representational knowledge, the knower attempts to preserve itself unchanged while acquiring a model.

In participatory knowledge, successful knowing changes the knower’s mode of being.

This does not make every transformation true. It means that certain objects—ritual, aesthetic, imaginal, and spiritual forms—cannot be exhaustively known without undergoing the relation they establish.

## 7. Comparative structure of the intermediate

| Feature             | Pratyabhijñā                                                         | Suhrawardī                                                    | Ibn ʿArabī                                                   | Corbin                                                               |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary problem     | How consciousness freely manifests subject and object                | How immaterial light appears in determinate form              | How the Real is both transcendent and disclosed in forms     | How visionary form can be real after modern subject–object reduction |
| Primary term        | *Vikalpa*, *vimarśa*, *svātantrya*                                   | Suspended images, world of images                             | *Khayāl*, *barzakh*, *tajallī*                               | *Mundus imaginalis*, active imagination                              |
| Status of image     | Consciousness appearing objectively to itself                        | Objective form without ordinary matter                        | Theophanic form between Being and nonexistence               | Real symbolic form disclosed through a corresponding organ           |
| Location            | Within universal consciousness; locally on the “inner organ”         | Autonomous intermediate ontological order                     | Cosmos itself and multiple intermediate levels               | Qualitative non-geographical space                                   |
| Relation to subject | Object is internal to consciousness yet appears as other             | Soul encounters forms not reducible to its private production | Individual imagination participates nondelimited imagination | Knower and image co-constitute a bi-unity                            |
| Ultimate ontology   | One universal self-manifest consciousness                            | Hierarchy of distinct lights under Light of Lights            | One Real Being disclosed in inexhaustible loci               | Participatory plurality; resistant to reductive monism               |
| Epistemic method    | Recognition of freedom in ordinary and contemplative experience      | Presential illumination plus demonstration                    | Unveiling, tasting, revelation, reason in proper limits      | Active imagination and *taʾwīl*                                      |
| Error               | Treating manifestation as externally imposed and self as finite only | Confusing subjective fantasy with objective imaginal form     | Fixing one theophany as exhaustive of the Real               | Literalism, psychologism, or monistic dissolution                    |

The table reveals that “intermediate realm” means four different things.

In Pratyabhijñā, the intermediate is primarily an **operation of consciousness**.

In Suhrawardī, it is an **objective ontological domain**.

In Ibn ʿArabī, it is the **barzakhic status of manifestation itself**.

In Corbin, it is an **ontological-hermeneutic relation between mode of being and mode of perception**.

## 8. Imagination as a mode of knowing

### 8.1 Śaiva knowing as self-relish

For Pratyabhijñā, a cognition is not merely a state that carries information about an object. It is consciousness manifesting an object and reflexively apprehending itself as doing so.

The full act is:

[
K_i(x)
======

\langle
\operatorname{Manifest}(x),
\operatorname{Determine}(x),
\operatorname{SelfApprehend}(K_i)
\rangle.
]

When *vimarśa* is understood through *camatkāra*, the act also has an aesthetic dimension:

[
K_i(x)
\rightarrow
\operatorname{Relish}_i
\left(
K_i(x)
\right).
]

Knowing is therefore intrinsically meaningful or valenced. The knower is not merely changed after cognition; reflexive feeling is constitutive of sentient cognition itself.

This is why imagination reveals *svātantrya*. The subject does not infer that it is free. It experiences freedom through the felt act of manifesting a form.

---

### 8.2 Suhrawardian illumination as knowledge by presence

Suhrawardī’s epistemology opposes the view that all knowledge occurs through representations or definitions.

The self knows itself not by receiving a form of itself but by being present to itself:

[
K(S,S)
======

\operatorname{Presence}(S).
]

Higher luminous knowledge similarly occurs through illumination and presence. Demonstrative reasoning remains necessary, but the perfected sage unites discursive mastery with direct illumination.

The imaginal image can therefore communicate knowledge because it is not a decorative substitute for an abstract proposition. It is the form in which a higher reality becomes present at a level accessible to the soul.

This gives a vertical epistemology:

[
\text{concept}
\rightarrow
\text{symbolic form}
\rightarrow
\text{presential illumination}.
]

The image is cognitively superior to a bare concept when it makes a spiritual reality present rather than merely describing it.

---

### 8.3 Ibn ʿArabian unveiling

Ibn ʿArabī distinguishes reflection, scripture, and unveiling as modes of knowledge. Reason is indispensable but tends to separate and delimit. Imagination can hold together opposites because its own ontology is barzakhic.

A rational faculty may insist:

[
A\lor\neg A.
]

Imaginal unveiling may disclose:

[
x
\text{ is }A
\text{ in one relation and }\neg A
\text{ in another}.
]

This is not permission for contradiction without discipline. It reflects the double status of theophanic form:

[
\text{He}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{not He}.
]

The form discloses the Real, but the Real is not exhausted by the form.

Imagination is epistemically complete when it preserves both:

[
\operatorname{Affirmation}
]

and:

[
\operatorname{Incomparability}.
]

Reason alone tends to privilege incomparability or delimit a fixed concept. Unregulated imagination may mistake one disclosure for the whole. The heart must perpetually transform with the forms of divine self-disclosure.

---

### 8.4 Corbinian *theoria*

Corbin understands visionary knowledge as a *theoria* in the older sense: a contemplative seeing that participates in what it knows.

Active imagination does not invent an angel and then believe in it. Nor does it passively photograph an angel existing as an external physical object. It becomes the organ of an encounter whose image-form is inseparable from the personal relation established.

Thus:

[
\operatorname{Know}(A_i)
========================

\operatorname{BecomeCapableOfEncounter}(S_i,A_i).
]

*Taʾwīl* is the discipline by which the perceiver moves from literal appearance toward the source disclosed through it without abolishing the image.

Corbin’s great insight is that the symbol dies under either extreme:

* reduction to a physical fact;
* reduction to a subjective fantasy.

The symbol lives only as an imaginal relation.

---

## 9. Irreconcilable differences

### 9.1 Identity or participation?

Pratyabhijñā’s strongest claim is:

[
S_i
\equiv_{\mathrm{mode}}
C_{\infty}.
]

The finite subject is universal Śiva operating under contracted knowledge and action.

Suhrawardī does not support this identity. A human soul is a dependent managing light, not the Light of Lights. Illumination may elevate and purify it, but hierarchy remains ontologically real.

Corbin likewise resists a monism in which the person’s distinct relation to an angelic counterpart disappears. His *unus-ambo* is not identity without remainder. It is relation-preserving unity.

Ibn ʿArabī occupies a more ambiguous position. Every existent manifests the Real, but the servant remains servant and the Lord remains Lord. The form is “He/not He.” Theophanic identity at one level coexists with irreducible distinction at another.

The first divide is therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{Does realization disclose numerical identity with}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{the universal source, or perfect participation in it?}
}
]

---

### 9.2 Consciousness or Being?

The Śaiva ultimate is:

[
C_{\infty}
==========

\text{self-manifest consciousness and freedom}.
]

The Akbarian ultimate is:

[
W_{\infty}
==========

\text{the Real or nondelimited Being}.
]

Suhrawardī’s ultimate is:

[
L_{\infty}
==========

\text{Light of Lights}.
]

Corbin frequently allows these vocabularies to resonate without forcing a final reduction.

The distinction matters. Is light conscious because self-presence is intrinsic to pure light? Is Being necessarily self-manifest? Is consciousness a fundamental actuality of Being, or is Being known only through consciousness?

A rigorous synthesis cannot solve this by substituting one word for another.

---

### 9.3 Are grades real?

Suhrawardī’s hierarchy is not merely apparent restriction. Higher and lower lights are genuinely distinct in intensity, dependence, and causal rank.

Pratyabhijñā can redescribe hierarchy as consciousness’s free self-determination:

[
C_{\infty}
\rightarrow
\delta_1,\delta_2,\ldots
]

without conceding that the modes possess independent ultimate being.

Ibn ʿArabī preserves real degrees of preparedness and manifestation while denying independent Being to any finite locus.

The disagreement is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Are ontological grades constituent structures of reality,}
}
]

or:

[
\boxed{
\text{modes under which one reality appears to itself?}
}
]

---

### 9.4 Is the imaginal a realm or an operation?

Suhrawardī’s later school posits a discontinuous imaginal world with objective inhabitants.

Pratyabhijñā needs no independently existing middle world to explain ordinary imagination. The image is consciousness’s own objectifying operation.

Ibn ʿArabī uses imagination both as world and operation, resisting the separation.

Corbin’s language sometimes seems to reify a distinct “place,” but his deeper phenomenology makes world and organ correlative.

The irreducible question is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Does imagination perceive a pre-existing intermediate}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{domain, or does the imaginal domain exist as the}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{relational event of manifestation?}
}
]

---

### 9.5 Angelic counterpart and universal self

Corbin’s angelology gives personal individuation a central role. The celestial counterpart is not merely one interchangeable expression of universal consciousness. It is the unique form of a person’s relation to transcendence.

Pratyabhijñā contains rich deity, goddess, yoginī, mantra, and power traditions, but it does not organize liberation around the same Corbinian anthropology of a personal heavenly twin.

The difference is not “personal angels versus impersonal Kālīs.” Śaiva deities are not simply impersonal. The precise difference is that Corbin makes **irreducible dialogical individuation** central, whereas Pratyabhijñā finally interprets every divine and personal polarity within one universal “I.”

---

## 10. Application to the aperture model

Let one phenomenal aperture be:

[
A_i
===

\left\langle
O_i,
D_i,
\partial_i,
U_i,
\tau_i,
\mathcal G_i,
\nu_i,
SM_i
\right\rangle,
]

where:

* (O_i): concrete organismic or participant process;
* (D_i): phenomenal field;
* (\partial_i): field boundary;
* (U_i): co-presence structure;
* (\tau_i): lived temporality;
* (\mathcal G_i): qualitative geometry;
* (\nu_i): valence;
* (SM_i): self-model.

Introduce an imaginative operator:

[
\mathcal I_i.
]

A weak computational definition would be:

[
\mathcal I_i:
\text{stored representations}
\rightarrow
\text{new representation}.
]

The comparative traditions require a stronger form:

[
\boxed{
\mathcal I_i:
\left(
D_i,K_i,P_i
\right)
\rightarrow
\left\langle
S_i^\ast,
X_i^\ast,
R_i^\ast,
\mathcal G_i^\ast,
\nu_i^\ast
\right\rangle.
}
]

Here:

* (K_i): constraints and preparedness of the aperture;
* (P_i): incoming or latent pattern;
* (S_i^\ast): transformed subject-position;
* (X_i^\ast): imaginal object;
* (R_i^\ast): subject–object relation;
* (\mathcal G_i^\ast): transformed phenomenal geometry;
* (\nu_i^\ast): transformed valence.

Imagination does not merely insert (X_i^\ast) into an unchanged container.

It co-generates:

[
S_i^\ast
+
X_i^\ast
+
R_i^\ast.
]

This is the first major contribution to the aperture theory.

---

### 10.1 Generative imagination (I_i)

The Śaiva evidence suggests that generative imagination is a local expression of the aperture’s capacity to select and stabilize a phenomenal gestalt without direct external determination.

Define:

[
I_i
===

\operatorname{GenerateGestalt}
\left(
A_i,\Theta_i
\right),
]

where (\Theta_i) includes memories, dispositions, desires, linguistic structures, and constraints.

The output can be novel:

[
X_i^\ast
\notin
\operatorname{PreviouslyPerceived}(A_i).
]

This does not require creation from nothing. It requires irreducibility of the output to passive replay.

The physical question is:

[
\text{What process enables the aperture to generate}
]

[
\text{a globally coherent object without external sensory fixation?}
]

The metaphysical question is:

[
\text{Is this generation wholly local, or does it participate}
]

[
\text{an objective imaginal order?}
]

---

### 10.2 Junction (J) as *barzakh*

The aperture model already uses junctions between organized states:

[
S_a
\rightarrow
J_{a,b}
\rightarrow
S_b.
]

Ibn ʿArabī allows us to deepen (J). It is not merely a temporal gap. It is the relation that both distinguishes and connects two organizations.

Define:

[
J_{a,b}
=======

\operatorname{Barzakh}
\left(
S_a,S_b
\right).
]

Then:

[
J_{a,b}
\neq S_a,
\qquad
J_{a,b}\neq S_b,
]

but:

[
J_{a,b}
]

contains the transformation rule through which one becomes the other.

Applied to subject and object:

[
J_i
===

\operatorname{Barzakh}
\left(
S_i,X_i
\right).
]

The subject and object are not first completed and then connected. They emerge through the differentiating relation.

This gives:

[
\boxed{
J_i
\text{ is the generative interior of the aperture’s}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{subject–object articulation.}
}
]

---

### 10.3 *Vimarśa* and illumination

*Vimarśa* can be formalized as the reflexive closure of the imaginative operation.

Let:

[
\mathcal I_i(X)
]

be an act of manifestation.

Then:

[
\operatorname{Vimarśa}_i
========================

\operatorname{SelfApprehend}
\left(
A_i,\mathcal I_i(X)
\right).
]

The aperture not only manifests (X); it registers the act as its own present articulation.

Suhrawardian illumination adds a participatory vertical dimension:

[
\operatorname{Ishrāq}_i
=======================

\operatorname{Receive}
\left(
A_i,L_k
\right),
]

where (L_k) is a higher-order luminous pattern.

A unified but neutral schema is:

[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ReflexiveIllumination}_i
======================================

\operatorname{SelfApprehend}
\left[
\operatorname{ReceiveOrGenerate}
\left(
A_i,P
\right)
\right].
}
]

The unresolved variable is the source of (P):

* endogenous construction;
* environmental information;
* transpersonal pattern;
* objective imaginal form;
* universal consciousness.

---

### 10.4 *Camatkāra* and theophany

The project proposes pairing *camatkāra* with theophany. This is productive if the terms are not identified.

*Camatkāra* concerns the felt, astonishing, self-relishing character of manifestation.

Theophany concerns the disclosure of a source through a finite form.

Let:

[
T_i(P)
======

\operatorname{ManifestAs}
\left(
P,X_i
\right),
]

and:

[
C_i
===

\operatorname{Relish}
\left(
A_i,T_i(P)
\right).
]

Then:

[
\boxed{
\textit{camatkāra}
\approx
\text{the aperture’s valenced reflexive apprehension}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{of a manifestational event.}
}
]

The stronger metaphysical claim is:

[
P
=

\text{universal Self or divine Real}.
]

The neutral formalism does not decide this.

It says only that the image can be experienced as disclosing more than its isolated form because the field registers both:

* determinate appearance;
* excess of source over appearance.

---

### 10.5 Self-boundary and the imaginal

An imaginal act can modify the represented self-boundary:

[
\partial SM_i
\rightarrow
\partial SM_i^\ast.
]

A deity, angel, imagined double, or visionary guide may appear as distinct from the ordinary ego while remaining within one phenomenal field.

This produces at least three possibilities:

#### Endogenous alterity

[
X_i^\ast
========

\operatorname{GeneratedBy}(A_i).
]

#### Participatory alterity

[
X_i^\ast
========

\pi_{A_i}(P),
]

where the aperture participates a pattern (P) extending beyond its ordinary self-model.

#### Independent imaginal entity

[
X_i^\ast
========

E_j
]

where (E_j) is a distinct entity encountered through imaginal perception.

Phenomenology alone may not distinguish these. The traditions differ primarily in how they interpret the causal source.

---

## 11. The proposed formal synthesis

The project’s final question asks whether *vimarśa* as *camatkāra* can be unified with Corbin’s *unus-ambo*, and whether reflexive self-relish is the dynamic form of the *barzakh*.

The answer is: **yes as a formal model, but not yet as a demonstrated metaphysical identity.**

### 11.1 Static subject–object dualism

The ordinary model is:

[
S+O.
]

Subject and object are independently specified and externally related.

### 11.2 Monistic collapse

A simplistic nondual model gives:

[
S=O.
]

Difference becomes unreal or inexplicable.

### 11.3 Barzakhic bi-unity

The stronger model is:

[
\boxed{
B
=

\langle
S,O,R
\rangle,
}
]

where:

[
S
=

\operatorname{Pole}_S(B),
]

[
O
=

\operatorname{Pole}_O(B),
]

and:

[
R
]

is not secondary but constitutive.

The relation generates the poles as distinct moments of one event.

This is the logical content of *unus-ambo*:

[
1\times1.
]

The two remain two as poles but one as event.

---

### 11.4 Dynamic *barzakh*

The *barzakh* is then:

[
\boxed{
J
=

\operatorname{DifferentiateAndJoin}
\left(
M
\right),
}
]

where (M) is an indeterminate or more universal manifest capacity.

It produces:

[
J(M)
====

\langle
S,O,R
\rangle.
]

This is dynamic because the relation is not a fixed border. It is the act by which manifestation continually takes determinate form.

---

### 11.5 *Vimarśa*

*Vimarśa* is the same event insofar as it is reflexively self-present:

[
\boxed{
V
=

\operatorname{SelfPresent}
\left(
J(M)
\right).
}
]

The manifestational operation does not occur blindly. It apprehends itself through the form it produces.

Thus:

[
\operatorname{Vimarśa}
======================

\operatorname{Barzakh}
+
\operatorname{Reflexivity}.
]

---

### 11.6 *Camatkāra*

*Camatkāra* is the event insofar as reflexive self-presence is felt as wonder, relish, or aesthetic intensity:

[
\boxed{
C
=

\operatorname{ValencedSelfPresence}
\left(
V
\right).
}
]

Thus:

[
\operatorname{Camatkāra}
========================

\operatorname{Barzakh}
+
\operatorname{Reflexivity}
+
\operatorname{Valence}.
]

The complete formal sequence is:

[
M
\overset{J}{\longrightarrow}
\langle
S,O,R
\rangle
\overset{V}{\longrightarrow}
\operatorname{SelfPresent}
\left(
S,O,R
\right)
\overset{C}{\longrightarrow}
\operatorname{FeltRelish}
\left(
S,O,R
\right).
]

This is the strongest unified structure yielded by the comparison.

---

### 11.7 What the model does not establish

The same formal structure is compatible with several ontologies.

#### Local process inexternalism

[
M
=

D_i,
]

the manifest capacity of one local aperture.

#### Participatory inexternalism

[
D_i
===

\pi_{A_i}
\left(
M_0
\right),
]

a local actualization of a common manifest ground.

#### Pratyabhijñā

[
M_0
===

# C_{\infty}

\text{Śiva}.
]

#### Suhrawardian Illuminationism

[
M
=

\operatorname{Reception}
\left(
L_k,A_i
\right)
]

within a real hierarchy of lights.

#### Ibn ʿArabian metaphysics

[
M
=

\operatorname{Tajallī}
\left(
W_{\infty},p_i
\right).
]

#### Corbinian angelology

[
M
=

\operatorname{Encounter}
\left(
S_i,A_i^{\mathrm{celestial}}
\right).
]

Formal convergence does not decide among them.

## 12. The deepest comparative result

These traditions reject the assumption that an image must be either:

[
\text{a physical object}
]

or:

[
\text{an unreal private representation}.
]

But they reject it for different reasons.

Utpaladeva says:

[
\boxed{
\text{the image is real as consciousness’s free}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{self-objectification.}
}
]

Suhrawardī says:

[
\boxed{
\text{the image may possess an objective form of existence}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{without material substratum.}
}
]

Ibn ʿArabī says:

[
\boxed{
\text{all finite reality is imaginal because it is both}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{divine disclosure and non-self-subsisting form.}
}
]

Corbin says:

[
\boxed{
\text{the image is an event of participatory encounter}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{within a real qualitative world.}
}
]

The genuine convergence is therefore not a doctrine of one hidden “astral plane.”

It is a theory of **mediated manifestation**:

[
\boxed{
\text{source}
\rightarrow
\text{imaginal operation}
\rightarrow
\text{form},
}
]

where the middle term both preserves and transforms the source.

The image is true when it discloses its source without claiming to exhaust it.

The image becomes false when it is treated as:

* independent of its source;
* a merely private fabrication;
* the exclusive and final form of the source;
* a literal object whose relational mode is ignored.

---

## 13. Research programme

The convergence suggests a concrete research programme rather than a purely comparative conclusion.

### 13.1 Phenomenology

Map the transformation produced by generative and visionary imagination across:

[
\partial_i,
\quad
U_i,
\quad
\tau_i,
\quad
\mathcal G_i,
\quad
\nu_i,
\quad
SM_i.
]

Does imaginal perception reliably alter self-boundary, temporal organization, aesthetic intensity, or agency attribution in ways distinguishable from ordinary voluntary visualization?

### 13.2 Source discrimination

Develop methods for distinguishing:

* recombined memory;
* spontaneous endogenous imagery;
* socially scaffolded symbolic expectation;
* veridical perception;
* information apparently unavailable through ordinary channels.

No metaphysical interpretation of imaginal beings is secure without this discrimination.

### 13.3 Structural invariants

Ask whether experiences called:

* illumination;
* theophany;
* *camatkāra*;
* imaginal encounter;
* unveiling;

share measurable phenomenal invariants.

Candidates include:

* increased field coherence;
* simultaneous differentiation and unity;
* reduction of observer–object distance;
* enhanced valence;
* altered agency attribution;
* the sense that form exceeds its explicit content.

### 13.4 Transformative epistemology

Test the proposition:

[
\operatorname{Know}(x)
\Rightarrow
\operatorname{Transform}(S).
]

Which forms of knowledge require transformation in the knower, and when does transformation increase rather than decrease epistemic reliability?

This is where Shaw and Voss are especially useful. Theurgy and imaginal hermeneutics claim that certain realities become knowable only through conformation of the participant. The risk is circularity: the tradition may transform the participant into someone disposed to confirm the tradition.

### 13.5 Ontological underdetermination

Treat identical experiences as compatible with multiple ontologies until evidence discriminates them.

A radiant figure may be:

* an endogenous construction;
* a culturally patterned gestalt;
* a manifestation of a transpersonal attractor;
* an objective imaginal being;
* a local mode of universal consciousness.

Phenomenal intensity alone cannot decide.

---

## 14. Conclusion

The most profound shared claim of Pratyabhijñā, Illuminationism, Ibn ʿArabī, and Corbin is not that “imagination is real.”

It is that reality becomes manifest through operations that modern epistemology mistakenly divides between:

* subjective construction;
* objective discovery.

Imagination is both receptive and productive.

It receives because form is not generated from an isolated ego.

It produces because no form appears without the receiving locus actively determining how the source becomes present.

The operation is therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{reception-through-formation}.
}
]

Pratyabhijñā places the ultimate power of this operation in *svātantrya*. Consciousness is free because it can appear as determinate otherness while remaining self-manifest. Imagination is the ordinary, finite disclosure of that freedom.

Suhrawardī gives the operation ontological depth. Forms need not be either abstract universals or material bodies. Suspended images occupy an objective intermediate order in which spiritual realities become figured and bodily forms become subtle.

Ibn ʿArabī universalizes the intermediate. The cosmos is not merely located in a *barzakh*; it is barzakhic. Everything finite joins Being and nonexistence, divine disclosure and creaturely limitation. Imagination is the mode under which transcendence can become immanent without being contained.

Corbin converts this ontology into a hermeneutics of personhood. The imaginal is a world only for an organ capable of perceiving it; the organ comes alive only through encounter with a world exceeding private fantasy. The relation is *unus-ambo*: neither external dualism nor absorptive identity.

The strongest unified formalization is:

[
\boxed{
M
\overset{\operatorname{barzakh}}{\longrightarrow}
\langle
S,O,R
\rangle
\overset{\operatorname{vimarśa}}{\longrightarrow}
\operatorname{ReflexiveManifestation}
\overset{\operatorname{camatkāra}}{\longrightarrow}
\operatorname{FeltSelfRelish}.
}
]

The *barzakh* is manifestation’s differentiating relation.

*Vimarśa* is that relation becoming present to itself.

*Camatkāra* is its valenced, aesthetic self-experience.

*Unus-ambo* is the logical form preserving the distinction of its poles within one act.

This does not prove that the *mundus imaginalis* is the interior of universal consciousness. It shows that such a hypothesis can now be stated precisely:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{The imaginal may be the relational interior produced}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{when manifestness reflexively differentiates itself}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{into knower, known, and the felt relation between them.}
}
]

The final question is no longer merely whether imagination creates or perceives.

It is:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{What kind of reality must manifestation be if}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{creating a form and perceiving a form are two aspects}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{of one reflexive, barzakhic operation?}
}
]

Pratyabhijñā answers: universal consciousness.

Suhrawardī answers: the graded cosmos of light.

Ibn ʿArabī answers: the Real’s inexhaustible self-disclosure.

Corbin answers: the imaginal relation through which a person and a spiritual world become real to one another.

Our aperture theory should not choose prematurely. Its immediate task is to explain how one local field can generate objectively appearing forms, be transformed by them, relish their meaning, and sometimes experience them as disclosures of a source exceeding the local ego.

Only after that structure is understood can we ask whether the source is:

[
\text{local process},
\qquad
\text{common manifest ground},
\qquad
\text{objective imaginal order},
\qquad
\text{Being},
\qquad
\text{or Śiva}.
]

14. Angela Voss, “Becoming an Angel: The *Mundus Imaginalis* of Henry Corbin and the Platonic Path of Self-Knowledge,” interprets Corbinian imagination as an alchemical and participatory transformation rather than subjective fantasy.

15. Gregory Shaw’s work on Iamblichean theurgy is used here comparatively to clarify participatory knowing: the embodied participant is transformed into a mode capable of divine activity rather than merely acquiring a representation.

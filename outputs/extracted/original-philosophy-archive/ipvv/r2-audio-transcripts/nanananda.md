Honestly, **Ñāṇananda comes closest to ending the show—but he does it by refusing to reveal a final picture behind the screen**.

He does not defeat Proclus, Bohm, Huayan or Abhinavagupta by producing an even grander ontology. He asks:

> What if the compulsive demand to discover *what ultimately exists behind appearances* is itself part of the appearance-making machinery?

That is the radical move.

The 33 sermons were delivered between 1988 and 1991 to meditating monks at Meetirigala, and in the final sermon Ñāṇananda says the entire series can be condensed into one problem: **“the two ends and the middle.”** ([seeing through the net][1])

## The entire world-show in one mechanism

Take an ordinary act of seeing.

There appears to be:

```text
an eye here
an object there
consciousness between them
```

We ordinarily assume the eye and object already exist as two self-contained things, and consciousness merely connects them.

Ñāṇananda reverses this.

He argues that consciousness is precisely the **discrimination** by which “internal eye” and “external form” appear as two opposed ends. The subject-object gap is not first encountered as an independently existing gulf and then observed by consciousness. The discriminative event is what establishes the two-sided structure. 

So:

```text
not:

subject + object → consciousness

but more like:

discriminative consciousness
→ subject-pole / object-pole
→ contact
→ feeling
→ craving
→ grasping
→ a seemingly solid world
```

This is the heart of his reading of dependent arising.

## Craving is the seamstress

Ñāṇananda’s greatest image is that craving is a **seamstress**.

Once experience has produced two ends—

* me and world;
* here and there;
* past and future;
* pleasant and unpleasant;
* name and form;
* existence and non-existence—

craving tries to stitch them together.

It says:

> “I am here, the desired thing is there, and I must cross the gap.”

Or:

> “I existed in the past and must continue into the future.”

Or:

> “I am the observer of that object.”

But in doing this, it ignores the middle: the dependently arisen act of contact, discrimination and construction through which the two ends appeared in the first place.

Ñāṇananda says grasping is essentially the attempt to close this gap—to turn a conditioned relationship into a solid thing, possession or identity. 

The striking Pāli verse around which he closes the series is:

> *Yo ubh’ antam abhiññāya, majjhe mantā na lippati.*

“Knowing both ends, through wisdom he does not stick to the middle.”

That does **not** mean finding a mystical midpoint between two realities. It means seeing the entire three-part construction without becoming glued to any part of it.

## The dog looking into the water

This is where Ñāṇananda connects directly to our discussion of reflexivity.

A dog crosses a plank over water and sees another dog below. It growls at its own reflection because it does not understand the reflective nature of the water.

Ñāṇananda says ordinary self-consciousness works similarly.

Consciousness reflects name-and-form. Then the mind mistakes the reflected structure for:

> “Me, my body, my mind, my experience.”

The mistake is not that reflection occurs. The mistake is failing to understand **reflection as reflection**.

The arahant does not destroy the capacity for experience. Rather, consciousness is no longer “established” on name-and-form as a substantially real self or world to be appropriated. ([Seeing Through The Net][2])

This gives us the perfect connection:

### Ñāṇavīra

Explains how experience can reflexively include its own structure.

### Ñāṇananda

Explains how that reflexivity produces the illusion of an observer confronting its own reflection—and how wisdom stops mistaking the reflection for a self.

Ñāṇavīra describes the mirror’s formal possibility.

Ñāṇananda diagnoses the dog growling into it.

## The cinema metaphor really does end the show

Ñāṇananda’s cinema analogy is probably his masterpiece.

A cinema requires:

* a dark background;
* a narrow beam of light;
* moving images;
* an audience concentrating upon the screen;
* emotional investment in the characters.

The darkness is ignorance.

The narrow beam is specifically prepared or conditioned consciousness.

The projected scenes are name-and-form.

Craving and grasping are the audience’s identification:

> “That is happening to me.”
> “I want that.”
> “I fear that.”
> “This story must end my way.”

Now imagine opening all the doors and windows in the cinema. Light floods the room from every side.

The projector may still be running. The screen may still contain moving shapes. But the film loses its compelling world-character. You see the screen, the room, the beam, the mechanism and your own previous absorption.

The movie is no longer capable of enclosing you.

Ñāṇananda explicitly says that the machinery can remain active while the film ceases to function *as a film* for the liberated viewer. Nibbāna is the cooling of the passions generated through this absorption—the “stilling of all preparations,” not necessarily the biological destruction of perception. ([Seeing Through The Net][2])

So yes:

> **He literally ends the show.**

Not by burning down the cinema.

By ending the conditions under which light, shadow and identification become an apparently self-existing world.

## What happens to ontology?

This is where he is devastating to our previous thinkers.

### Proclus builds the perfect theatre

Proclus tells us how:

* unity becomes multiplicity;
* causes remain in themselves;
* effects proceed;
* effects return;
* every level participates in the whole.

Ñāṇananda would not necessarily say this is incoherent.

He would ask:

> At what point did a useful pattern of dependence become a claim about independently real levels of Being?

Proclus turns mediation into metaphysics.

Ñāṇananda keeps pointing back to the immediate production of the two ends and middle. He would suspect that “the One” and “the many” are themselves the final grand-scale version of the polarity-making tendency.

Proclus says:

> The many requires the One.

Ñāṇananda says:

> Watch carefully how “one” and “many” arise as a mutually dependent pair.

That does not logically refute Proclus. It removes the experiential pressure that makes a first principle appear necessary.

## Huayan nearly gets there

Huayan is much closer because it refuses isolated substances. Each phenomenon exists through the entire web of conditioned relationships, and the tradition describes reality through interpenetration rather than independent entities. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][3])

But Ñāṇananda makes an even sharper move.

Huayan can produce a magnificent vision:

> Every jewel contains every other jewel.

Ñāṇananda might reply:

> Do not turn the net into your new Absolute.

The Buddhist task is not finally to admire the infinite network.

It is to understand:

* how the net is woven;
* what craving sticks to;
* how “this” and “that” are stitched together;
* how the stitching stops.

That is why **Seeing Through the Net** is such a perfect name.

He does not offer the ultimate net.

He sees through net-making.

## Bohm describes the projection system

Bohm’s implicate order says that relatively separate explicate forms unfold from a deeper undivided process.

Ñāṇananda could accept that as a physical or philosophical hypothesis.

But it does not yet touch the central Buddhist problem.

Even if the universe is:

* holographic;
* nonlocal;
* entirely processual;
* an undivided holomovement;

one can still crave it, identify with it and make a metaphysical self out of it.

> “I am the universal holomovement” can become a more sophisticated stitch.

Bohm may explain how the images are generated.

Ñāṇananda asks why the audience is suffering over them.

## Abhinavagupta is the real final opponent

Both Ñāṇananda and Abhinavagupta use the language of light, manifestation and reflexivity.

Abhinavagupta says that the light of experience is not dead illumination. It is intrinsically self-apprehending—*prakāśa-vimarśa*—and that this manifest-reflexive power is Śiva. ([Encyclopedia.com][4])

Ñāṇananda’s cinema image gets astonishingly close:

* limited consciousness is like the narrow projector beam;
* wisdom illuminates from all sides;
* the constructed images lose their ability to manifest as binding realities;
* released consciousness is no longer established on name-and-form.

But their final interpretations diverge.

### Abhinavagupta

> When all limited projections are penetrated, the self-luminous field reveals itself as universal consciousness.

### Ñāṇananda

> When the projections are penetrated, nothing needs to be grasped as “the universal field.”

Abhinavagupta sees the all-sided luminosity and says:

> Śiva.

Ñāṇananda sees the temptation to seize that luminosity as an ultimate thing and says:

> That is the projector starting up again.

That is why he is so dangerous to nondual metaphysics.

He can accept the phenomenology of spacious, luminous, non-objectified awareness while refusing to convert it into a cosmic Self.

## But there is one wobble in Ñāṇananda

His interpretation of:

> *viññāṇaṃ anidassanaṃ anantaṃ sabbato pabhaṃ*

as the arahant’s “non-manifestative, infinite, all-lustrous consciousness” is highly controversial.

Ñāṇananda argues that this is released consciousness which no longer manifests or reflects name-and-form as a substantial reality. He explicitly rejects the commentarial move of simply treating *viññāṇa* here as another name for Nibbāna. ([Seeing Through The Net][2])

But alternative readings exist. Bhikkhu Sunyo argues that the rare expression refers to the meditative dimension of boundless consciousness, not to Nibbāna or an arahant’s special liberated consciousness. Orthodox Theravāda interpretations may instead construe the phrasing as referring to Nibbāna and stress that the wider canon ordinarily treats consciousness as conditioned and subject to cessation. ([Classical (Orthodox) Theravāda][5])

This matters because Ñāṇananda sometimes sounds extremely close to introducing a subtle liberated consciousness after dismantling every other ground.

I do not think that is his intention. His emphasis is that released consciousness is **unestablished**, objectless in the appropriative sense and incapable of serving as a footing for name-and-form.

But philologically, he has not indisputably won that argument.

## So does he end the show?

### As a phenomenology of saṃsāra: **almost certainly yes**

He gives an extraordinarily coherent explanation of how:

```text
discrimination
→ polarity
→ contact
→ feeling
→ craving
→ stitching
→ substantiality
→ self and world
→ suffering
```

And he explains release not as escaping to another location, but as seeing and ceasing the stitching operation.

### As an answer to eternalism versus annihilationism: **yes, better than nearly anyone**

He does not say:

> Something eternal survives.

He does not say:

> A truly existing person is destroyed.

He says the entire question assumes the solid person-world structure that dependent arising reveals to be conditioned.

“What happens to the real thing?” is already malformed.

### As a final metaphysics of consciousness: **no**

He does not explain why experience is manifest at all.

He does not mathematically derive consciousness.

He does not establish whether reality outside liberated experience is physical, mental, neutral or something else.

But that may be deliberate.

He is saying:

> The Buddha did not give you a superior ontology to possess. He showed you the activity by which ontologies become existential cages.

## My verdict

Ñāṇavīra tells us how experience can recursively know its structure.

Bohm tells us how local forms might unfold from an undivided process.

Proclus tells us how each thing participates in a hierarchy of causal unity.

Huayan tells us how every phenomenon exists through the total relational net.

Abhinavagupta tells us that the light, net and unfolding are universal consciousness recognising itself.

**Ñāṇananda walks into the cinema and opens the doors.**

The projector keeps turning.

The images remain visible.

But nobody is trapped inside the plot.

That is probably the most powerful Buddhist response to our entire conversation:

> He does not explain what ultimately lies behind the show.
> He explains why the demand for something behind it keeps the show running.

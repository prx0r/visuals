# The Geometry of Thinking

## A critical investigation of Fuller’s tetrahedral conceptuality

## Executive verdict

The research produces six main conclusions.

1. **Fuller meant the geometry-of-thinking claim literally within his own system.** He regarded concepts as geometrically organized systems and repeatedly asserted that minimum conceptuality is tetrahedral. This was simultaneously a metaphysical thesis, an epistemographic modelling programme, and a pedagogical method—not merely an ornamental metaphor.

2. **Fuller did not empirically demonstrate that cognition is tetrahedral.** His argument depends on identifying a concept with a finite three-dimensional system possessing an inside and outside. Once that premise is accepted, the tetrahedron follows; the premise itself is not established by psychology or neuroscience.

3. **A four-element complete relation structure really is tetrahedral in a precise combinatorial sense.** Four elements with all six possible pairwise relations form the complete graph (K_4), which is the edge graph of a tetrahedron. This is genuine mathematics. It does not follow that every four-term concept has all six relations, that its metric is Euclidean, or that thought is constrained to physical three-dimensional space.

4. **The four proposed “independent derivations” do not derive the same structure.** Fuller derives a minimum three-dimensional enclosure; Ñāṇavīra derives a four-member reflexive transformation structure; Gowan assigns four heterogeneous physical principles to a diagram; Friston partitions variables into four causal roles. Their shared cardinality does not establish structural identity.

5. **The claimed convergence on 36 contains an arithmetic error.** At (N=7), the cumulative number of pairwise relations is 56, not 36. Thirty-six is the number of unordered pairs among nine events. The 36 Śaiva tattvas are conventionally generated through a doctrinal taxonomy, not through Fuller’s abacus.

6. **The scientifically viable hypothesis is narrower and stronger:** cognitive representations have measurable geometries, and four mutually discriminable, fully interrelated roles may sometimes form a tetrahedral—or more exactly, 3-simplex—representation. Contemporary neuroscience can test this. There is currently no evidence that all thought is tetrahedral.

The correct research programme is therefore not:

[
\boxed{
\text{Thought is universally tetrahedral.}
}
]

It is:

[
\boxed{
\text{When, if ever, does cognition encode four-role}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{relational structures as a 3-simplex, and does that}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{geometry provide a distinctive computational advantage?}
}
]

# 1. What Fuller meant by “the geometry of thinking”

## 1.1 The claim is explicit

Fuller does not merely use geometry to illustrate ideas. He states:

> “Synergetics is the geometry of thinking.”

He immediately glosses this by saying that epistemology is modelable and that knowledge organizes itself geometrically. Elsewhere he says that any conceptual thought is a system and is structured tetrahedrally because conceptuality is polyhedral.

In *Synergetics* 265.09 he concludes:

> “Conceptuality is tetrahedral.”

His argument there uses four successive experiences: the fourth supposedly provides an “altitude” from which the first three can be apprehended as a triangular base. In 267.01 he similarly combines the minimum twoness of the observed with the minimum twoness of the observer, yielding four foci of a tetrasystem.

In 400.40 he defines the minimum finite system as four synchronously related event-foci, inherently tetrahedral. In 600.02–03 he defines structure as a self-stabilizing complex of at least four energy events.

*Synergetics 2* introduces the word **epistemography** for this project: the attempt to model the organization of knowledge geometrically. Fuller’s own institutional archive describes *Synergetics* as his lifelong philosophical exploration and identifies tetrahedral geometry, sphere packing, topology, and vector coordination as its core concerns.

The claim is therefore not merely metaphorical.

## 1.2 Its exact status

Fuller’s “geometry of thinking” combines four distinct commitments:

### A. Literal ontological claim

Conceptual relations possess an actual geometric or topological structure.

### B. Epistemological method

Understanding consists in eliminating irrelevant details until a generalized relational pattern remains.

### C. Modelling programme

The same small set of geometric transformations should model structures across physical and conceptual domains.

### D. Pedagogical practice

Physical models permit people to apprehend relations that linguistic or Cartesian descriptions obscure.

Fuller’s position is best classified as:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{a literal epistemographic research programme}
}
]

supported by topological analogy and model construction, rather than by an empirical theory of neural coding.

Amy Edmondson’s exposition reinforces this interpretation: for Fuller, a tetrahedral system is a sizeless and timeless set of relations capable of many physical embodiments. The conceptual tetrahedron is not supposed to be a tiny object located inside the brain.

# 2. Reconstructing Fuller’s argument

Fuller’s reasoning can be stated more clearly than he states it himself.

## Premise 1: A concept must be a system

A concept is thinkable only insofar as some content is distinguished from everything treated as irrelevant.

[
C
=

\operatorname{Distinguish}
\left(
\text{relevant},
\text{irrelevant}
\right).
]

## Premise 2: A system divides inside from outside

[
\operatorname{System}(C)
\Rightarrow
\operatorname{Inside}(C)
+
\operatorname{Boundary}(C)
+
\operatorname{Outside}(C).
]

## Premise 3: The minimum volumetric enclosure in three-dimensional Euclidean space is a tetrahedron

Four non-coplanar points joined by six edges produce four triangular faces and enclose a nonzero volume.

## Conclusion

[
\operatorname{MinimumConceptualSystem}
======================================

\operatorname{Tetrahedron}.
]

The geometrical premise is correct.

The questionable move is:

[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Concept}
\Rightarrow
\operatorname{ThreeDimensionalVolumetricEnclosure}.
}
]

That does not follow merely from a concept having boundaries.

A concept can possess a boundary in:

* a one-dimensional ordered space;
* a two-dimensional similarity space;
* an (n)-dimensional feature space;
* a graph;
* a probability simplex;
* a topological quotient;
* a non-Euclidean manifold.

Fuller has therefore established:

[
\boxed{
\text{a tetrahedron is the minimum simplicial enclosure}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{of volume in three dimensions,}
}
]

not:

[
\boxed{
\text{every bounded conceptual structure must be}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{a volumetric enclosure in three dimensions.}
}
]

# 3. The exact mathematics behind “four terms make a tetrahedron”

Let four conceptual terms be:

[
X={x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4}.
]

The number of unordered pairs is:

[
\binom{4}{2}
============

# \frac{4(4-1)}2

6.

]

If every pair is related, the relational graph is:

[
K_4.
]

The abstract graph (K_4) is precisely the 1-skeleton of a tetrahedron:

* four vertices;
* six edges;
* four triangular 3-cycles.

This gives a legitimate claim:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Any four-element complete undirected relational}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{structure is graph-isomorphic to a tetrahedron.}
}
]

But the qualifications are decisive.

## 3.1 Completeness is an extra assumption

Four terms do not automatically instantiate all six relations.

For example:

[
x_1\rightarrow x_2\rightarrow x_3\rightarrow x_4
]

is a four-node chain, not a tetrahedron.

A four-role concept could instead form:

* a tree;
* a cycle;
* a directed acyclic graph;
* a star;
* a bipartite graph;
* a hypergraph;
* a partially ordered set.

The tetrahedron appears only if the relation structure is complete or functionally equivalent to (K_4).

## 3.2 Topology is not metric geometry

Even when the graph is (K_4), it does not follow that the conceptual structure is a **regular** tetrahedron.

A regular tetrahedron requires:

[
d(x_i,x_j)=c
\quad
\forall i\neq j.
]

The six relations must all have equal metric length.

Fuller generally shifts between:

* four elements;
* six relations;
* tetrahedral topology;
* regular tetrahedral geometry;
* structural stability.

These are not equivalent.

## 3.3 The simplex generalization

The true general law is:

[
N
\text{ affinely independent points}
\rightarrow
(N-1)\text{-simplex}.
]

Thus:

| Elements | Simplex         |
| -------: | --------------- |
|        2 | line segment    |
|        3 | triangle        |
|        4 | tetrahedron     |
|        5 | 4-simplex       |
|      (N) | ((N-1))-simplex |

If all (N) terms are mutually equidistant, their regular simplex generally requires:

[
N-1
]

dimensions.

This turns Fuller’s intuition into respectable mathematics, but it removes the universal privilege of four. The tetrahedron is the four-element member of a general simplex family.

# 4. Does Fuller prove a four-event minimum for recognizing pattern?

Fuller claims that four similar events are necessary for the mind to recognize periodic frequency. Four events provide three intervals:

[
e_1
\overset{\Delta_1}{\longrightarrow}
e_2
\overset{\Delta_2}{\longrightarrow}
e_3
\overset{\Delta_3}{\longrightarrow}
e_4.
]

If:

[
\Delta_1=\Delta_2=\Delta_3,
]

the sequence is strongly suggestive of periodicity.

But four is not a mathematical minimum for detecting equal recurrence. Three events provide two intervals:

[
e_1
\overset{\Delta_1}{\longrightarrow}
e_2
\overset{\Delta_2}{\longrightarrow}
e_3,
]

and:

[
\Delta_1=\Delta_2
]

already supplies one repeated interval.

A fourth event adds confirmation and robustness, but not logical possibility.

Fuller’s “fourth event provides altitude” is therefore a geometric reinterpretation of evidential redundancy. It is not a theorem of signal detection or cognition.

This is characteristic of his epistemography: once four terms appear, he maps them into tetrahedral geometry. The map can be illuminating without proving that the underlying process is geometrically tetrahedral.

## 5.2 Fuller

Fuller’s derivation is genuinely geometric.

Four non-coplanar event-foci generate:

* six pairwise vectors;
* four triangular faces;
* inside/outside;
* nonzero volume.

The tetrahedron is not pasted onto an independently derived fourfold list. It follows directly from his definition of a minimum three-dimensional system.

The remaining question is whether conceptual completeness should be defined by three-dimensional enclosure.

---

## 5.3 Ñāṇavīra

The proposed mapping to:

[
\text{subject},
\text{object},
\text{intention},
\text{act}
]

is not supported by Ñāṇavīra’s *Fundamental Structure: Static Aspect*.

Ñāṇavīra begins with a formal representation of a thing and derives four members corresponding to:

1. no interchange;
2. interchange of columns;
3. interchange of rows;
4. interchange of both rows and columns.

He then recursively expands the four-member structure into 16-member and higher-order structures. Attention can shift among aspects while the thing remains invariant under the transformations.

The fourfold structure is therefore closer to:

[
\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2
]

or a Klein-four-like transformation system than to four phenomenological poles.

Ñāṇavīra’s dynamic analysis later concerns intention, action, choice, and temporality, but the text does not simply state that every experience consists of exactly:

[
{\text{subject, object, intention, act}}.
]

A tetrahedron could be used to visualize Ñāṇavīra’s four transformations, but that is a new model imposed upon his formalism.

The convergence is therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{fourfold algebraic invariance}
}
]

versus:

[
\boxed{
\text{four-vertex volumetric closure}.
}
]

These might be related, but they have not yet been shown to be the same invariant.

---

## 5.4 Gowan

John A. Gowan assigns four vertices to:

* energy conservation;
* causality/information;
* symmetry conservation;
* entropy.

He explicitly describes the tetrahedron as a heuristic model and acknowledges that aspects of the vertex and line placement are arbitrary in its two-dimensional presentation.

This is not an independent physical derivation that the universe requires four poles.

The four categories are also heterogeneous:

* energy conservation is a conservation law;
* entropy increase is a thermodynamic asymmetry;
* Noether’s theorem relates continuous symmetries to conserved currents;
* causality is not generally a conserved quantity.

Gowan’s model may be conceptually suggestive, but it is not part of accepted unified-field physics, and its appearance on arXiv or viXra does not constitute peer validation. His own abstract presents it as a conceptual tetrahedral “theory of everything,” not as an experimentally confirmed result.

This is the weakest member of the proposed convergence.

---

## 5.5 Friston’s Markov blanket

A Markov-blanketed system is often partitioned into:

[
{\eta,s,a,\mu},
]

where:

* (\eta): external states;
* (s): sensory states;
* (a): active states;
* (\mu): internal states.

Sensory and active states constitute the blanket that renders internal and external states conditionally independent.

This is a genuine fourfold structure, but it is not tetrahedral.

First, these are **classes of variables**, not four individual entities. Each class can contain many dimensions.

Second, their causal graph is not the complete graph (K_4). Direct internal–external coupling is excluded by the blanket condition. The typical directed structure is approximately:

[
\eta\rightarrow s\rightarrow\mu\rightarrow a\rightarrow\eta,
]

with additional dependencies allowed under the formulation, but not six symmetric all-to-all relations.

Third, the four arise from splitting a boundary into incoming and outgoing causal roles:

[
\text{blanket}
==============

\text{sensory}
+
\text{active}.
]

The derivation is functional-statistical, not volumetric.

Friston therefore supplies a genuine four-role architecture for adaptive systems, but not independent evidence that systems or concepts are tetrahedra.

Michael Levin’s multiscale agency and morphospace work may be compatible with nested boundary structures, but the specific four-state result belongs to the Markov-blanket partition rather than to a separate Levinian tetrahedral derivation.

\text{sign},

\boxed{

That is a much narrower theorem.

# 7. The Synergetic Abacus

## 7.1 Pairwise relations

For (N) events, the number of unordered pairwise relations is:

[
R_N
===

# \binom N2

\frac{N(N-1)}2.
]

This is the triangular-number sequence:

[
0,1,3,6,10,15,21,28,36,\ldots
]

Fuller presents this as the accounting of all possible interrelations among events.

At (N=4):

[
R_4=6,
]

giving the six edges of (K_4).

## 7.2 Cumulative relations

The cumulative sum is:

[
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\binom{k}{2}
==========================

\binom{N+1}{3}.
]

Explicitly:

[
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{k(k-1)}2
============================

\frac{N(N-1)(N+1)}6.
]

These are tetrahedral numbers.

Fuller explicitly connects stacked triangular arrays with tetrahedral numbers and claims that the accumulated interrelationships of successive experiences form such a number.

## 7.3 The prompt’s formula is incorrect

The brief states:

[
\sum R_k=(N-1)^2.
]

That is false for the ordinary cumulative pairwise count.

At (N=7):

[
R_7
===

# \binom72

21,
]

while:

[
\sum_{k=1}^{7}R_k
=================

# \binom83

56.

]

And:

[
(N-1)^2
=======

36.

]

These are three different quantities.

Thirty-six appears as:

[
\binom92=36.
]

So nine events—not seven—have 36 pairwise relations.

There is no mathematically privileged passage:

[
7\rightarrow36
]

in the abacus as stated.

---

## 7.4 Why does the table stop at seven?

I found no statement in sections 227–230 that seven is an ontologically privileged stopping point. Fuller uses the table to display the progression from events to pairwise and cumulative relations; its displayed range should not be treated as a theorem that seven completes the system.

The proposed explanation that seven is the first tetrahedral cluster with a central sphere is also incorrect for an ordinary tetrahedral lattice.

For a tetrahedral cluster with (n) spheres along each edge, a central lattice sphere requires equal integer barycentric coordinates:

[
a=b=c=d=\frac{n-1}{4}.
]

Therefore:

[
n\equiv1\pmod4.
]

The first nontrivial case is:

[
n=5,
\qquad
T_5=\binom73=35.
]

At (n=7), there is no exact central lattice sphere.

# 8. The 36 tattvas

The standard Śaiva enumeration is ordinarily analyzed as:

[
5\text{ pure tattvas}
+
7\text{ pure-impure tattvas}
+
24\text{ impure tattvas}
========================

36.

]

The lower 24 substantially appropriate the Sāṅkhya categories; the Śaiva system adds Māyā, the five limiting powers, and the pure levels from Śuddhavidyā through Śiva. Academic presentations of Abhinavagupta’s hierarchy describe this doctrinal enumeration rather than deriving 36 from pairwise combinatorics.

The expression:

[
36=4\times9
]

is arithmetically true, but it is not the historical or philosophical derivation of the tattva system.

Nor is there currently a strict mapping:

[
\text{one tattva}
\leftrightarrow
\text{one edge in a nine-event complete graph}.
]

To make the convergence meaningful, one would need to identify:

1. exactly nine primitive events;
2. why every pair among them generates one tattva;
3. why the resulting 36 relations occur in the traditional order;
4. why the five-pure/seven-mixed/24-impure taxonomy follows;
5. why alternative Śaiva enumerations do not undermine the derivation.

No such derivation is presently available.

The correspondence is therefore:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{numerological coincidence until a structure-preserving}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{mapping is supplied.}
}
]

# 9. What does the abacus actually imply?

The abacus expresses a universal fact about pairwise comparison:

[
N\text{ items}
\rightarrow
\binom N2\text{ unordered pairs}.
]

This applies to:

* people in a network;
* distances among points;
* correlations among variables;
* pairwise semantic similarities;
* physical interactions;
* comparisons among stimuli.

It does not predict a unique geometry.

A pairwise matrix can encode:

* Euclidean geometry;
* spherical geometry;
* hyperbolic geometry;
* graph distance;
* ultrametric hierarchy;
* arbitrary dissimilarity;
* no metrically consistent geometry at all.

The abacus gives the **number of entries** required for complete pairwise comparison. It does not determine their values or topology.

Its cognitive significance is therefore limited but real:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{complete relational specification grows quadratically}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{with the number of conceptual elements.}
}
]

That may have consequences for processing cost and representational compression. It does not imply tetrahedral organization for arbitrary (N).

# 10. Fuller and Gärdenfors

Gärdenfors’ conceptual-space theory represents information geometrically using quality dimensions. Concepts are generally regions—often idealized as convex regions—in spaces where distance corresponds to similarity. The number and nature of dimensions vary by domain.

Examples could include:

* colour dimensions;
* shape dimensions;
* taste dimensions;
* affective dimensions;
* social dimensions.

Nothing in the framework requires three dimensions or tetrahedral closure.

## 10.1 A precise mapping

Suppose four concepts are represented by points:

[
c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4\in X.
]

Measure the six pairwise dissimilarities:

[
D_{ij}=d(c_i,c_j).
]

This produces:

[
{D_{12},D_{13},D_{14},D_{23},D_{24},D_{34}}.
]

Fuller’s tetrahedral proposal becomes a testable representational model if it predicts that:

1. these distances are approximately Euclidean;
2. their minimal embedding dimension is three;
3. the four points are affinely independent;
4. all six relations are functionally significant;
5. the tetrahedral model predicts behaviour better than alternatives.

If all six distances are equal, the model is a regular tetrahedron.

If they are unequal but Euclidean, it is an irregular tetrahedron.

If the points lie in a plane, the tetrahedron is degenerate.

If the dissimilarities violate Euclidean conditions, no ordinary tetrahedral embedding exists.

## 10.2 What Fuller does not predict

The fact that four items have six pairwise similarities is not a unique Fuller prediction. Every representational similarity matrix for four stimuli has six independent off-diagonal entries.

The stronger prediction must concern:

* embedding dimension;
* simplex structure;
* symmetry;
* stability;
* computational performance.

Without one of these, “tetrahedron” simply renames the complete four-node graph.

# 11. Fuller and Tymoczko

Tymoczko represents musical chords as points in geometrical spaces whose topology is produced by quotienting out musical equivalences. Chord spaces can be orbifolds, and paths through them represent voice leadings between chords.

The crucial operation is:

[
X/G,
]

where (X) is a pitch space and (G) is a symmetry group representing equivalences such as:

* permutation of voices;
* octave equivalence;
* transposition;
* inversion.

This is fundamentally different from Fuller’s isotropic vector matrix.

Fuller’s IVM is a three-dimensional tetrahedral–octahedral lattice associated with close packing and 60-degree vector coordination.

Tymoczko’s chord spaces are derived from:

* chosen musical variables;
* equivalence relations;
* group actions;
* quotient topology.

The meaningful convergence is:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{both seek invariant relational structure beneath}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{many special-case realizations.}
}
]

The difference is:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Tymoczko derives the geometry from explicit symmetry}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{operations; Fuller often assumes tetrahedral geometry}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{as the universal coordinating framework.}
}
]

A rigorous bridge would require identifying an IVM lattice of pitch relations and then proving that the relevant musical quotient acts on that lattice. No canonical identification currently follows.

# 12. Are conceptual spaces constrained by physical sphere packing?

Not in general.

Sphere packing assumes:

* a metric;
* usually Euclidean space;
* positive-radius objects;
* non-overlap constraints;
* a fixed dimension;
* a packing objective.

Conceptual spaces do not universally satisfy these assumptions.

Concepts may:

* overlap extensively;
* possess unequal extents;
* form hierarchies;
* lie in high-dimensional spaces;
* use non-Euclidean metrics;
* change geometry with context;
* be represented through sparse graphs rather than continuous regions.

The more defensible principle is:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{conceptual representations are constrained by their}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{similarity relations, learned invariances, task}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{requirements, and neural coding architecture.}
}
]

Some abstract spaces may reuse mechanisms originally developed for physical navigation. That does not make their topology identical to physical three-dimensional space.

# 13. Neural evidence

## 13.1 Strong evidence for representational geometry generally

Representational similarity analysis characterizes neural activity through the distances among population responses to different stimuli or concepts. This permits direct comparison among:

* brain regions;
* behavioural similarity judgments;
* computational models;
* stimulus structures.

Representational geometry is now an established intermediate level connecting neural population activity, computation, and cognition.

Neuroscience therefore strongly supports:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{thinking and perception have measurable}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{representational geometry.}
}
]

That part of Fuller’s programme was prescient.

## 13.2 No established tetrahedral universal

I found no credible fMRI, EEG, electrophysiological, or computational-neuroscience result establishing that concepts generally form tetrahedra.

A search for “tetrahedral activation patterns” would also risk confusing two different geometries:

### Physical cortical geometry

Where activated neurons or voxels are located in the brain.

### Representational geometry

Distances among multivariate patterns of neural activity.

A tetrahedron in representational space would not require four cortical regions physically arranged like a tetrahedron.

The relevant claim must be about the second.

---

## 13.3 Existing evidence points to task-dependent geometries

Constantinescu, O’Reilly, and Behrens trained participants to navigate a two-dimensional conceptual space and found a hexagonally symmetric grid-like neural signal resembling spatial grid coding. This supports the reuse of spatial relational coding for abstract cognition, but the recovered symmetry was hexagonal because the task involved navigation in a continuous two-dimensional space—not because thought has a universal tetrahedral form.

Other work treats neural representations as:

* manifolds;
* high-dimensional population geometries;
* topological spaces;
* task-dependent metric structures.

Recent topological RSA explicitly allows neural representations to be compared at different levels of geometric and topological abstraction.

The evidence currently favours:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{the geometry of cognition is real but plural,}
}
]

not:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{one tetrahedral geometry constrains all concepts.}
}
]

# 14. Working memory and the number four

Miller’s (7\pm2) was not a universal limit on unstructured working-memory items. Later work distinguishes list span, rehearsal, chunking, and the narrower focus of attention. Cowan’s reviews propose a typical central capacity around three to five meaningful chunks, often summarized as approximately four, but the estimate remains task- and model-dependent.

Even if working memory held exactly four items, this would establish:

[
|\operatorname{WM}|=4,
]

not:

[
\operatorname{Geometry}(\operatorname{WM})
==========================================

\text{tetrahedron}.
]

Four simultaneously maintained items could form any relational graph.

A tetrahedral prediction requires the stronger claim:

[
\forall i\neq j,
\quad
R(x_i,x_j)
]

and some reason those six relations are co-represented as one simplex.

Working-memory capacity and tetrahedral geometry should therefore be tested independently.

# 15. Geometric and phenomenal insideness

## 15.1 Geometric insideness

The surface of a tetrahedron is a closed two-dimensional boundary embedded in three-dimensional space. It separates space into:

* an interior;
* the boundary;
* an exterior.

This is an instance of a dimension-general topological principle. A closed ((n-1))-dimensional surface can separate an (n)-dimensional space.

Thus:

* two points bound an interval in 1D;
* a closed polygon bounds a region in 2D;
* a closed polyhedral surface bounds a volume in 3D.

The tetrahedron is the minimum **simplicial volumetric** enclosure in 3D, not the universal minimum of insideness.

## 15.2 Phenomenal insideness

Phenomenal inside/outside may refer to:

* privacy;
* ownership;
* first-person accessibility;
* subject–object differentiation;
* bodily self-boundary;
* inclusion within one field of co-presence.

These are not topological interiors in physical space by definition.

A private phenomenal field need not be shaped like the organism, brain, or a polyhedron.

## 15.3 The bridge is currently missing

To identify geometric and phenomenal interiority, one would need a bridge principle such as:

[
\operatorname{TopoInterior}(O_i)
\equiv
\operatorname{PhenomenalPrivacy}(D_i).
]

That claim currently lacks evidence.

A Markov blanket provides statistical conditional independence, but it does not by itself establish phenomenal privacy. A closed neural graph provides recurrent causation, but it does not establish a first-person interior. A tetrahedron provides geometric enclosure, but it does not establish awareness.

The same word—“inside”—is presently performing different jobs.

The safest conclusion is:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{geometric and phenomenal insideness are structurally}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{analogous but ontologically unidentifiable without}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{an independently supported bridge law.}
}

# 16. Relation to APIT

Fuller does not solve APIT’s selector problem.

Suppose a four-node causal subsystem forms (K_4). That tells us its graph is tetrahedral.

It does not establish:

* that it is the maximal or minimal subject-bearing process;
* that no overlapping (K_4) exists;
* that its geometric interior equals phenomenal privacy;
* that it persists as one subject through time;
* that the intrinsic character of the graph is experience.

There can be many nested tetrahedral subgraphs within a larger network.

Thus tetrahedrality supplies:

[
\text{local relational closure},
]

not:

[
\text{unique subject individuation}.
]

However, Fuller may contribute a useful candidate idea: **a concept or aperture becomes structurally determinate when relational closure produces a nondegenerate simplex rather than a decomposable collection of elements.**

That can be tested without claiming that tetrahedral geometry causes or constitutes consciousness.

# 17. What would falsify the tetrahedral hypothesis?

The universal thesis is already falsified in its unrestricted form because many successful conceptual and dynamical systems possess non-tetrahedral structures.

A narrower experimental thesis should specify falsification conditions.

## Hypothesis

For tasks requiring four mutually defined conceptual roles, the brain represents those roles as a nondegenerate 3-simplex whose six relations are constitutively necessary.

## It would fail if:

1. a ring, tree, chain, hyperbolic, or learned-manifold model predicts neural and behavioural data equally well or better;
2. the four concepts occupy a lower-dimensional plane;
3. only some pairwise relations are represented;
4. the recovered dimension tracks task features rather than fourfold cardinality;
5. five-role tasks form a 4-simplex just as naturally, showing that the result is generic simplex coding;
6. three-role systems display the same claimed closure and stability;
7. removing one of the six relations leaves conceptual performance intact;
8. tetrahedral geometry appears only after analyst-selected embedding or dimensional reduction.

A valid test must compare tetrahedral structure against explicit alternatives rather than merely draw four measured points as a tetrahedron.

# 18. Proposed experiment

## 18.1 Experimental question

Does cognition exhibit a privileged tetrahedral geometry when integrating four mutually constraining roles?

## 18.2 Task families

Construct matched relational tasks containing:

* three roles;
* four roles;
* five roles.

Examples might use fictional agents, objects, intentions, and actions, while controlling:

* memory load;
* stimulus complexity;
* familiarity;
* number of pairwise statements;
* response difficulty.

## 18.3 Competing models

For each task, predefine:

* complete simplex;
* sparse tree;
* directed cycle;
* ring;
* bipartite graph;
* hierarchical or hyperbolic model;
* unconstrained learned geometry.

For four terms, distinguish:

[
K_4
]

from merely:

[
|V|=4.
]

## 18.4 Measurements

Use:

* behavioural similarity judgments;
* generalization errors;
* reaction time;
* fMRI representational similarity analysis;
* MEG or EEG for temporal evolution;
* neural-manifold and topological analyses.

Representational geometry methods already permit direct comparison between model distance matrices and multivariate neural patterns.

## 18.5 Critical intervention

Train a complete four-relation structure and then selectively remove or reverse one relation.

Fuller’s strong closure claim predicts a qualitative loss of integrity:

[
K_4-e
]

should not merely be a slightly degraded concept. It should lose the distinctive coherence associated with tetrahedral completion.

If performance declines smoothly rather than undergoing a structural transition, the strong minimum-system interpretation is weakened.

## 18.6 Discriminating predictions

### Fuller-specific prediction

Four-role systems possess unusually stable, efficient, or transferable representations beyond what is predicted by item count.

### General simplex prediction

Every (N)-role mutually discriminable system forms an ((N-1))-simplex. Four has no unique cognitive status.

### Task-geometry prediction

Representational shape follows the relations demanded by the task, irrespective of cardinality.

### Working-memory prediction

Any advantage at four is explained by capacity limits rather than tetrahedral topology.

These models can be compared out of sample.

# 19. Research recommendation

## Worth pursuing

Yes—but only after removing the numerological layer.

There are three serious research questions here:

### 1. Simplex coding

Does the brain use simplex-like representations to maximize discriminability among conceptual alternatives?

### 2. Four-role relational closure

Are there cognitive operations for which four mutually constraining roles constitute a particularly stable or irreducible organization?

### 3. Spatial-code reuse

When abstract structures are represented geometrically, which properties of physical navigation and spatial coding transfer to them?

These connect Fuller to real contemporary fields:

* representational geometry;
* conceptual spaces;
* neural manifolds;
* grid-like conceptual coding;
* graph representation;
* topological data analysis;

# 20. Final assessment

Fuller was substantially right about one profound point:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{thinking is not adequately described as the}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{manipulation of featureless propositions.}
}
]

Concepts possess:

* dimensions;
* distances;
* boundaries;
* symmetries;
* transformations;
* invariants;
* trajectories;
* topologies.

Modern conceptual-spaces theory, musical geometry, representational similarity analysis, neural-manifold research, and conceptual grid coding all vindicate the broad programme of studying cognition geometrically.

Fuller’s mistake was to move from:

[
\text{thought has geometry}
]

to:

[
\text{the universal geometry of thought is tetrahedral}.
]

The evidence currently supports the first statement and not the second.

The sharpest surviving Fuller hypothesis is:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{The tetrahedron is the minimum 3-simplex capable of}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{representing four mutually differentiating terms as}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{one complete pairwise relational system.}
}
]

That is mathematically exact.

The scientific question is whether cognition ever treats this structure as privileged:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Do four-role concepts acquire distinctive stability,}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{closure, or phenomenal unity when their relational}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\textbf{geometry approaches }K_4\textbf{?}
}
]

That question is novel enough to investigate, narrow enough to falsify, and compatible with current neuroscience.

The 36-tattva correspondence is not part of that research programme. It is presently a coincidence.

The proposed grand convergence should therefore be replaced by a tiered verdict:

\boxed{

}

# It from Bit from Śiva?

## Memory, boundaries and the birth of a world

The deepest mistake in contemporary consciousness theory may be that it begins too late.

It begins with brains, representations, information or bits. But a bit already presupposes a distinction:

```text
0 / 1
signal / noise
inside / outside
this / not-this
```

A distinction, in turn, presupposes a context in which the difference matters. Without some system capable of responding differently to 0 and 1, the “bit” is only a physical difference to which no informational meaning has yet been assigned.

So perhaps Wheeler’s famous sequence needs extending:

```text
it from bit
bit from distinction
distinction from perspective
perspective from bounded agency
bounded agency from manifestation
```

The Tantric version would be:

```text
Śiva → Śakti → distinction → bit → world → organism → recognition
```

Not because a supernatural person called Śiva manufactures digital information, but because the categories of information, object and observer already presuppose the more primitive fact that **something appears, differs and possesses the power to respond**.

The first principle is therefore not “bit.”

It is manifestation capable of differentiation.

That is very close to Abhinavagupta’s inseparability of:

* *prakāśa* — manifestation or presence;
* *vimarśa* — reflexive apprehension;
* *svātantrya* — the power to articulate and transform manifestation.

---

# 1. A boundary does not create consciousness

A soap bubble has a boundary. A crystal has internal order. A thermostat reacts to temperature.

It would be premature to conclude that each possesses a conscious perspective.

A boundary performs a more elementary function:

> **It converts an undivided causal field into an asymmetric relationship between an inside and an outside.**

Before the boundary, interactions are simply interactions within a field.

After the boundary, some changes are registered as occurring **to this system**.

But the boundary must be active rather than merely geometrical.

A living membrane:

* selectively admits some molecules;
* excludes others;
* detects chemical differences;
* expends energy maintaining itself;
* repairs damage;
* regulates internal conditions;
* changes its behaviour according to external events.

The boundary thereby creates a distinction between:

```text
changes compatible with continued organisation
and
changes that threaten continued organisation
```

This is the beginning of **normativity**.

The system no longer occupies states indifferently. Some states are better or worse *for it*.

A chemical gradient becomes food, danger or noise only relative to the organisation of an organism.

So the actual sequence is:

```text
boundary
    ↓
selective interaction
    ↓
internal variables that must be protected
    ↓
better/worse states
    ↓
goal-directed regulation
    ↓
a centre of relevance
```

A perspective begins when the universe is no longer sampled neutrally but divided according to:

> What can happen to me?
> What can I do about it?
> What must remain stable for me to continue?

Michael Levin’s work on multiscale agency describes selves in terms of the scale at which goals, memories and problem-solving capacities are coordinated. Cells can pursue local physiological goals while participating in tissue- or organism-level goals that belong to a larger collective. Communication can therefore enlarge the spatial and temporal range of what the higher-order system can sense and control. ([ScienceDirect][1])

## The exact formula

A boundary is insufficient.

A plausible minimal perspective requires:

```text
Perspective =
    active boundary
  + protected variables
  + valence
  + memory
  + action capacity
  + temporal integration
  + reflexive availability
```

The boundary creates **locality**.

Valence creates **concern**.

Memory creates **continuity**.

Action creates a distinction between **self-caused and externally caused change**.

Reflexivity creates **presence to itself**.

# 2. The kañcukas are an ancient specification of an agent

This makes the five *kañcukas* extraordinarily relevant.

In the 36-tattva system, unlimited consciousness becomes a finite subject through five limitations:

| Kañcuka    | Traditional meaning                      | Modern translation                              |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **niyati** | localisation and causal restriction      | boundary, embodiment and environmental location |
| **kāla**   | timelessness becomes sequential time     | finite memory and predictive horizon            |
| **vidyā**  | complete knowing becomes partial knowing | restricted sensors and limited model            |
| **kalā**   | unlimited power becomes limited action   | finite action repertoire and controllability    |
| **rāga**   | completeness becomes lack and desire     | valence, preference and organismic need         |

These are not five arbitrary cosmic substances.

Together they answer the basic questions required to define a particular agent:

```text
Where am I?
What can I know?
What can I do?
How far through time can I act?
Which outcomes matter to me?
```

The *kañcukas* describe the geometry of a finite perspective.

They also explain why no organism inhabits “reality in general.” Each organism inhabits reality as filtered through its particular limits.

A bat does not possess an inferior version of the human world. A bacterium does not possess an extremely blurry human perspective. Each enacts a world organised around different:

* distinguishable signals;
* affordances;
* dangers;
* timescales;
* possible actions.

*Māyā*, in this interpretation, is not the creation of a completely false universe.

It is the power through which an unbounded field becomes available as **this particular world from this particular position**.

# 3. Ñāṇavīra’s missing insight: a thing is an invariant transformation

Ñāṇavīra’s *Fundamental Structure* supplies a crucial piece that neither simple predictive processing nor ordinary substance metaphysics captures.

He begins not with matter but with distinction.

For one thing to be identifiable, there must be another thing from which it differs. One is “this”; another is “that.” The relation or possible transformation between them is itself a higher-order structure.

A thing is consequently not an isolated packet.

It exists within a hierarchy of:

* alternatives;
* contrasts;
* transformations;
* general and particular patterns.

Ñāṇavīra says the identity of a thing is inseparable from the other possible things toward which it points. A hammer is a hammer partly through its relation to hammering and nails. A particular shade is that shade by contrast with the shades that it is not. Experience is therefore inherently intentional or teleological: things point beyond themselves toward possibilities. ([Ñāṇavīra Thera Dhamma Page][2])

This means:

> **No object is only what is immediately present. It is also a structured horizon of absent possibilities.**

That is precisely what a generative model supplies.

The experienced cup includes:

* possible rotations;
* the hidden back surface;
* the possibility of grasping it;
* expectations about weight;
* what would happen if it fell;
* what it is for.

What appears is therefore not a photograph constructed from sensory input.

It is a hierarchy of counterfactual transformations.

## The Dynamic Aspect

Ñāṇavīra calls the second part of *Fundamental Structure* its **Dynamic Aspect**. It is not quite a separate work called *Dynamic Structure*.

His central claim is that duration involves the combination of change and invariance.

A melody persists while its notes change. A flame persists while its material changes. A body persists while its molecules, cells and mental states change.

The thing is not an unchanging substance beneath transformations.

It is the **invariance of a pattern across subordinate transformations**. ([Ñāṇavīra Thera Dhamma Page][3])

This solves a major problem of personal identity.

You are not numerically the same because every component remains unchanged.

You are the same insofar as successive transformations preserve enough higher-order organisation to count as transformations of one continuing process.

```text
identity ≠ frozen substance

identity = invariance under relevant transformation
```

But the phrase “relevant transformation” is crucial.

A person can lose hair, change beliefs and replace many cells while remaining recognisably the same person. Other transformations destroy the organisation that supported that identity.

A self is therefore a pattern with a **threshold of permitted change**.

# 4. What memory really is

Memory is usually imagined as stored content:

```text
event
  ↓
recording
  ↓
storage
  ↓
retrieval
```

Levin’s proposal is more radical.

You never directly access the past. You access present traces—engrams, bodily changes, neural dispositions, altered structures—left by earlier events. The present organism must interpret those traces according to its current body, goals and context.

The memory is therefore like a message sent by a past version of the self to a future version that may have changed substantially in the meantime. Levin calls memory “self-improvising” because the future system must reconstruct useful significance rather than merely replay an exact recording. ([Organism Earth][4])

## What “a message from your past self” means

Suppose an earlier version of you experiences danger.

That event changes:

* neural connection strengths;
* bodily tension;
* attentional habits;
* expectations;
* emotional reactions;
* future action policies.

Years later, you do not travel backward and inspect the original event.

The present system encounters the alteration left behind.

It then attempts to answer:

> What does this trace mean now?

The trace may be interpreted as:

* useful warning;
* unexplained anxiety;
* traumatic flashback;
* lesson;
* false alarm;
* part of a personal narrative.

The same trace can acquire different meanings as the present self changes.

So memory is not the past preserved inside the present.

It is:

> **The present system’s constrained reconstruction of its past in service of a possible future.**

A rough formula is:

```text
Remembering now =
    present goals
  + present body
  + current context
  + surviving traces
  + reconstruction
```

This is why memory can remain meaningful despite substantial changes in embodiment. The trace does not have to contain a complete copy of the original world. It must constrain the later agent sufficiently for it to reconstruct something relevant.

# 5. Memory as saṅkhāra and saṃskāra

Your comparison with *saṅkhāra* is exactly right, with one linguistic qualification.

The Sanskrit *saṃskāra* and Pali *saṅkhāra* have overlapping histories but different ranges of meaning in different contexts. For this synthesis, both point toward something more active than passive storage:

> A previous process leaves the system disposed to process future conditions in a particular way.

A memory is therefore not primarily an image.

It is a **modified readiness**.

After learning to play an instrument, you do not need to retrieve a visual recording of every practice session. Your body has become differently capable of responding.

After trauma, the organism may become prepared to detect danger earlier and react more intensely.

After meditation, attention may become less automatically captured.

The past is present as an alteration in the mapping:

```text
input → interpretation → response
```

That is saṃskāra as dynamical constraint.

## Memory is a policy, not merely content

The deepest memories may not tell the organism:

> “This happened.”

They tell it:

> “When conditions resemble this, attend here, feel this, and prepare to act in this way.”

That is why habits and memories are difficult to separate.

Both are ways the past governs which future becomes likely.

# 6. The Śaiva objection: traces cannot explain recognition by themselves

Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta make a powerful objection to purely trace-based accounts of memory.

A trace exists now.

But remembering includes more than producing a present image. It includes recognition:

> “I encountered this before.”

The Śaivas argue that a present trace cannot, by itself, explain the unity between the earlier perception and the current act of remembering. The original cognition is no longer directly available as an object. They therefore posit a continuing knowing subject that unifies past perception and present recollection. ([MDPI][5])

The Buddhist and Śaiva positions diverge here.

### Buddhist-process interpretation

Continuity can be produced through causal dependence without requiring a permanent subject.

The current event inherits structures from earlier events.

### Pratyabhijñā interpretation

Causal traces do not fully explain the first-person recognition that the past event was experienced **by me**.

There must be a reflexive unity across changing cognitions.

## The synthesis

We do not need to posit an unchanged autobiographical ego.

But we may need to distinguish:

1. the changing self-model;
2. the invariant capacity through which changing experiences are reflexively integrated.

At the biological level, continuity is an invariant organisational process.

At the phenomenological level, continuity is the present recognition of past-as-mine.

At the Śaiva metaphysical level, both are expressions of *vimarśa*: awareness’s capacity to relate manifestations to itself across transformation.

Memory is therefore not an object transferred intact through time.

It is a **current act of recognition constrained by past transformation**.

# 7. Ñāṇananda: consciousness and world create one another

Ñāṇananda’s *Nibbāna: The Mind Stilled* prevents us from turning the boundary into a tiny isolated observer.

He places the pivotal structure of dependent arising in the reciprocal relationship between:

* *viññāṇa* — consciousness;
* *nāma-rūpa* — name-and-form.

These are compared to two bundles of reeds leaning against one another. Consciousness depends upon name-and-form; name-and-form depends upon consciousness.

The lived world is not finished first and subsequently observed by an internal consciousness.

Nor does an independent consciousness invent a world from nothing.

They arise as a reciprocal loop:

```text
consciousness
      ↕
meaningful embodied form
```

Ñāṇananda describes this as a vortex. *Saṅkhārā*—preparations, tendencies and efforts—keep the vortical interplay operating. Ignorance is not merely absence of information; it is the continuing tendency to seek permanence, pleasure and ownership within an unstable process. ([Seeing Through The Net][6])

This directly improves predictive processing.

Predictive processing is often described as though a pre-existing organism constructs a model of a pre-existing external world.

Ñāṇananda’s structure says:

> The organism and its meaningful world are produced reciprocally.

The self becomes real as a functional pole within the world-model.

The world becomes meaningful as the field of possible actions, threats and satisfactions for that self.

Each continuously confirms the other.

```text
self-model says:
“This is happening to me.”

world-model says:
“This is the kind of world in which that self exists.”
```

This is the vortex.

# 8. Grasping is pathological self-evidencing

The Fristonian organism acts to continue inhabiting states compatible with its continued organisation.

At a basic biological level this is necessary.

But the same process can become psychologically pathological.

The self-model not only predicts bodily viability. It may predict:

* “I am unsafe.”
* “I am unlovable.”
* “People always reject me.”
* “I need this experience to be complete.”
* “I must control what happens.”

Attention then selects evidence supporting the model.

Action creates environments that confirm it.

Memory reconstructs the past in its terms.

The model becomes self-evidencing.

This is close to Buddhist *upādāna*: grasping or taking-up.

Grasping is not merely wanting an object. It is the activity by which experience becomes organised around:

```text
this is mine
this defines me
this must remain
this must not happen
```

Ñāṇananda’s vortex is maintained by precisely these preparations and appropriations. The more the system attempts to secure a permanent owner, the more effort it must invest in regenerating that owner through every experience. ([Seeing Through The Net][6])

---

# 9. Nibbāna as releasing the world from ownership

This lets us reinterpret nibbāna without reducing it either to annihilation or a hidden metaphysical heaven.

The ordinary organism must maintain functional boundaries.

Awakening does not require becoming unable to distinguish one’s body from a wall or abandoning all biological regulation.

What ceases is the parasitic significance of ownership.

Ñāṇavīra distinguishes the ordinary intentional significance of things from the additional significance of their being “mine.” A hammer can remain for hammering; colour can remain distinct from other colours. What disappears is the appropriation layered upon the structure. ([Ñāṇavīra Thera Dhamma Page][7])

So:

```text
before:
appearance → relevance → mine → threat/desire → becoming

after:
appearance → relevance → appropriate response
```

The world remains structured.

Functional agency remains.

The extra demand that experience secure an enduring owner no longer receives fuel.

In predictive terms, this could be described as relaxing the compulsively high precision assigned to the autobiographical self-model.

But the Buddhist claim is deeper than a mere model update.

It is insight that no component of the loop can legitimately be owned as:

> “This is permanently what I am.”

# 10. The Tantric divergence: liberation is recognition, not cessation alone

Here Buddhism and Abhinavagupta part company.

Ñāṇananda’s analysis aims at ending the vortex of appropriation.

Abhinavagupta agrees that the contracted ego is constructed and that habitual limitation generates bondage.

But he argues that when false limitation falls away, what is recognised is not merely ownerless conditioned arising.

It is the universal reflexivity and power of consciousness itself.

The Śaiva formula is:

> *citiḥ svatantrā viśvasiddhihetuḥ*
> “Free consciousness is the cause of the manifestation of the universe.”

The Buddhist says:

> There is experience without a metaphysically permanent owner.

The Śaiva says:

> The limited owner is false, but the reflexive power through which all ownership, appearance and action are possible is real.

The Levin–Ñāṇavīra–Ñāṇananda synthesis can take us almost to the edge:

* selves are invariant patterns rather than substances;
* worlds arise reciprocally with bounded agents;
* memories are dispositions through which the past shapes the present;
* grasping continually regenerates a model of ownership;
* boundaries create limited worlds of concern.

But one question remains:

> Why is this organisation manifest at all?

Science can describe the structural loop.

Buddhism can reveal that no owner is found within it.

Abhinavagupta claims that the manifestness and reflexivity of the loop are the deepest nature of reality.

# 11. It from bit, or bit from Śiva?

“It from bit” suggests that physical objects emerge from informational distinctions.

But information is never completely free-standing.

A bit requires:

* possible alternatives;
* a stable distinction;
* a system capable of registering it;
* a decoding context;
* consequences that differ according to its value.

Therefore:

```text
no interpretation → no meaningful bit
no boundary → no local interpretation
no value → no significant information
no manifestation → no appearance of distinction
```

So the stronger sequence is:

```text
prakāśa:
something is manifest

śakti:
difference is possible

spanda:
difference is dynamically articulated

māyā:
a finite reference frame is established

kañcukas:
knowledge, action, value, time and location are bounded

puruṣa:
a local perspective appears

nāma-rūpa:
a meaningful embodied world co-arises

saṅkhāra / saṃskāra:
past activity conditions future construction

pratyabhijñā:
the system recognises the nature of its own manifestation
```

This is not a scientific derivation from Tantrāloka.

It is a philosophical architecture into which current science fits with surprising precision.

## Better than “it from bit”

The title should be:

# **It from Bit from Śiva**

But the thesis underneath it is:

> Objects emerge from distinctions.
> Distinctions emerge within perspectives.
> Perspectives emerge through bounded agency.
> Bounded agency is structured manifestation.
> The final unexplained fact is that structure manifests at all.

“Śiva” is Abhinavagupta’s name for that final fact when it is understood not as passive existence, but as:

```text
presence
+ reflexivity
+ capacity
```

# 12. How did it all start?

Science can offer a plausible sequence but not yet an ultimate explanation.

## Physical differentiation

The early universe developed stable regularities, asymmetries and structures.

## Chemical memory

Some systems became path-dependent: their present responses depended upon previous interactions.

Memory exists in a minimal physical sense whenever history leaves a state-dependent constraint that affects future behaviour. Research on material memory studies precisely such persistence of training and perturbation in nonliving systems. ([arXiv][8])

## Compartmentalisation

Chemical networks became enclosed within selectively permeable compartments.

Inside and outside acquired functional significance.

## Self-maintenance

Some compartments contributed to preserving and reproducing the conditions of their continued organisation.

## Selection

Systems whose inherited constraints supported persistence became more common.

## Model-like regulation

Living systems developed increasingly indirect methods for detecting hidden conditions and preparing for future states.

## Reflexive modelling

Some agents began modelling not only their environments but their own uncertainty, attention, actions and social appearance.

This is a plausible evolutionary-developmental history of perspective.

But it does not answer:

> Why does physical structure possess any intrinsic presence?

Nor does it explain why there is something rather than nothing, why laws exist or why manifestation is possible.

At that point we face the major metaphysical choices:

1. Presence emerges inexplicably from non-present structure.
2. Presence is an illusion generated by self-modelling.
3. Presence is an intrinsic aspect of certain physical organisations.
4. Presence is intrinsic to reality, while bounded organisation determines its finite forms.
5. The demand for one final intrinsic ground is itself mistaken.

Abhinavagupta chooses the fourth.

Madhyamaka refuses to establish any of them as intrinsically existing.

Frankish develops the second.

The synthesis defended here is closest to the third and fourth:

> Structure determines **which perspective exists**; manifestation explains **why there is a perspective from within**.

# 13. How this solves ordinary human problems

## “Am I still the same person?”

You are not a static object.

You are a hierarchy of invariants maintained through transformation.

Changing does not mean becoming unreal. Refusing to change is not what preserves identity.

The practical question becomes:

> Which patterns deserve continuity, and which patterns are merely inherited constraints?

## “Why do old memories control me?”

Because memory is not safely archived in the past.

It is a present disposition that helps construct what appears relevant now.

Healing does not require deleting the past. It requires teaching the present system a different interpretation and a wider repertoire of response.

## “Why are habits so difficult to break?”

Because a habit is not merely an action you repeat.

It is a self-reinforcing loop involving:

* attention;
* expectation;
* body state;
* value;
* memory;
* action;
* environmental feedback.

Breaking it requires changing the loop, not merely issuing a verbal command to its imagined controller.

## “Why do people inhabit completely different realities?”

Because organisms and persons enact worlds through different:

* histories;
* needs;
* concepts;
* capacities;
* threats;
* temporal horizons.

Disagreement is frequently not one person misreading the same neutral data. It can involve different salience landscapes in which different facts become visible.

## “What is freedom?”

Freedom is not the absence of all constraints.

Without constraints there is no particular agent.

Finite freedom is the ability to recognise and revise the constraints through which one’s world is generated.

In modern language:

```text
freedom =
    larger policy repertoire
  + deeper temporal horizon
  + improved self-model
  + capacity to alter one’s own habits
  + reduced compulsion
```

In Śaiva language, this is the recovery of *svātantrya* within contraction.

## “What is awakening?”

The Buddhist answer:

> Experience no longer receives the additional significance of being a permanent “me” or “mine.”

The Śaiva answer:

> The finite perspective recognises that its limits are real as a mode of manifestation but not absolute as the nature of consciousness.

The shared insight is:

> The boundary is operationally necessary but metaphysically misinterpreted.

# The final model

Reality is not first divided into independent subjects and objects.

It begins, as far as experience can show, with manifest differences.

Some differences stabilise into patterns.

Some patterns preserve themselves.

Self-preserving patterns acquire values.

Values organise attention and action.

Action generates bounded worlds.

Memory allows those worlds to persist through transformation.

Language and reflection permit agents to model their own modelling.

Grasping mistakes this recursively maintained pattern for an independently existing owner.

Recognition sees the pattern without denying its functioning.

```text
manifestation
    ↓
difference
    ↓
invariant pattern
    ↓
active boundary
    ↓
value
    ↓
memory
    ↓
world-model
    ↓
self-model
    ↓
appropriation
    ↓
suffering

or

self-model
    ↓
reflexive recognition
    ↓
agency without absolute ownership
```

The strongest conclusion is therefore not:

> Everything is information.

Nor:

> Everything is a mind.

It is:

> **Reality is manifest relational structure capable of stabilising into bounded, remembering and valuing perspectives. These perspectives construct worlds, mistake their boundaries for absolute separation, and can sometimes recognise the constructive process itself.**

Science explains how a finite world is built.

Ñāṇavīra explains how identity persists as invariance through transformation.

Ñāṇananda explains how consciousness and meaningful form sustain the vortex of self and world.

Levin explains how memories, competencies and goals scale across biological collectives.

Abhinavagupta proposes why the whole process is not merely structure seen from outside, but manifestation, reflexivity and power experienced from within.

The bit does not come first.

The bit is what manifestation looks like once Śakti has drawn a line.

# LOGICVID SESSION — the authoritative confirmation: Abhinavabhāratī on śānta and the exact reasoning behind "peace is the underlying rasa"

*The downloaded Abhinavabhāratī (Gupta, 2017) confirms and REFINES our derivation with the
authoritative primary source. Key findings: (1) śānta's sthāyin = *tattvajñāna-janita-
nirveda-prayaḥ* (the world-weariness born of the knowledge of truth); (2) "śānta and
bibhatsa occur in connection with mokṣa" (ABh XVIII.110); (3) śānta ↔ mokṣa, as vīra↔dharma,
raudra↔artha, śṛṅgāra↔kāma.*

---

## ROUND 0 — the authoritative material (from the Abhinavabhāratī via Gupta)

1. **śānta's sthāyin is nirveda born of tattvajñāna** (ABh, on NŚ VI): "Abhinavagupta refers
   to nirveda, arisen from tattvajñāna (knowledge of the Truth or Reality), as its
   sthāyibhāva." And: "Abhinavagupta defines śāma, the sthāyin of śānta as
   *tattvajñāna-janita-nirveda-prayaḥ*" — "consisting mostly in the world-weariness (nirveda)
   disgust born of the knowledge of truth."
2. **śānta and bibhatsa → mokṣa** (ABh XVIII.110): "śānta and bibhatsa occur in connection
   with mokṣa." And: "śāma — which consists mostly in the disgust aroused by the knowledge
   of truth or reality — is the means of liberation."
3. **The puruṣārtha mapping** (ABh on NŚ VI.15): śṛṅgāra ↔ kāma, raudra ↔ artha, vīra ↔
   dharma, **śānta ↔ mokṣa**.
4. **Rasa is an "extraordinary form of consciousness"** (the rasa-sūtra): "Rasa... manifests
   through a combination of various bhāvas... it is perceived by an extraordinary form of
   consciousness [para-saṁvitti-gocaraḥ], it is an experience of the nature of a relishing or
   tasting (āsvadātmakānubhāvaḥ)."
5. **nirveda is both a vyabhicārin and a sthāyin** (ABh VI): "mentioned at the beginning of
   the vyabhicāribhāvas... in order to show that it depends on both characters; that is, it is
   both a vyabhicāribhāva and a sthāyibhāva." (A sthāyin can become a vyabhicārin, but not
   vice versa.)

## ROUND 1 — the first penetration: WHY is the sthāyin of śānta a WORLD-WEARINESS (nirveda)?

**The question:** Śānta is PEACE. Why is its stable emotion (sthāyin) a *world-weariness*
(nirveda), a disgust born of knowing reality? Isn't peace the opposite of disgust?

**The derivation (Abhinava's own logic):** The sthāyin of śānta is "tattvajñāna-janita-
nirveda-prayaḥ" — a *disgust born of the knowledge of truth*. Knowing reality (tattvajñāna)
reveals the world's unsatisfactoriness (the endless outward drive, the unsatisfied cravings
— Cuneo's point). That knowledge *generates* a disgust with the world (nirveda, jugupsa) — a
turning-away. And that turning-away IS the beginning of peace.

So śānta is NOT a naive calm; it is the *rest that comes AFTER the world-weariness*. The
disgust (nirveda) is the pivot: it is the moment the outward drive is seen as futile, and
turns inward.

> **PENETRATION 1:** Śānta's sthāyin is nirveda — the world-weariness born of knowing reality
> — because peace is not a naive calm but the rest that FOLLOWS the turning-away. The
> knowledge of truth reveals the world's unsatisfactoriness; that generates a disgust
> (nirveda); and that disgust is the turning-point where the outward drive is seen as futile
> and the self-apprehension returns inward. **Śānta = the peace after the world-weariness =
> the valence-reset AFTER the drive is seen through.** This confirms our earlier derivation
> (suffering → recognition → śānta) in Abhinava's own terms.

## ROUND 2 — the exact confirmation: śānta IS the mokṣa-rasa

**The question:** Our derivation said śānta is the *underlying* rasa (the camatkāra without a
screen). The Abhinavabhāratī says śānta is the *mokṣa* rasa. Are "underlying" and "mokṣa" the
same?

**The derivation (the exact reasoning):** The four *original* rasas (śṛṅgāra, raudra, vīra,
bibhatsa) are each pervaded by a puruṣārtha: kāma, artha, dharma. These are the goals of the
*outward* life — the world. The fifth puruṣārtha, mokṣa, is the *cessation* of the outward
drive. Abhinava assigns śānta (with bibhatsa) to mokṣa. **Śānta is the rasa of the goal that
is NOT a worldly goal — the goal of the cessation of the outward drive itself.**

- The four worldly rasas = the felt of the four worldly goals (the outward turnings, the
  drive).
- **Śānta = the felt of mokṣa = the felt of the cessation of the outward drive = the felt of
  the return to rest.**
- Since mokṣa is the goal that *underlies* the other four (they are all means to it, per
  Abhinava's resolution that prīti and vyutpatti are one), **śānta is the rasa that underlies
  the other eight.**

> **PENETRATION 2 (the exact confirmation):** Śānta is the mokṣa-rasa because mokṣa is the
> puruṣārtha of the cessation of the outward drive. The four worldly rasas are the felt of
> the four worldly goals (kāma, artha, dharma); śānta is the felt of the one goal (mokṣa)
> that the others serve. **Since the four worldly goals are means to mokṣa, śānta is the rasa
> that underlies them all — the felt of the cessation of the outward drive, the return to
> rest.** This is our "camatkāra without a screen" claim, confirmed as the mokṣa-rasa in
> Abhinava's own terms.

## ROUND 3 — the deepest synthesis: śānta = the rasa of recognition (the felt of the direction-reversal)

**The question:** Now unite EVERYTHING: the master-key (direction), the felt, the
suffering-reset, and the authoritative śānta = mokṣa doctrine.

**The derivation:**
- The master-key: bondage/liberation = the direction of self-apprehension (outward/inward).
- The rasa-derivation: the rasas = the felt of the outward turnings; śānta = the felt of the
  inward turn.
- The suffering-reset: recognition = the permanent valence-reset.
- The Abhinavabhāratī: śānta = the mokṣa-rasa, whose sthāyin is nirveda born of tattvajñāna.

**Put together:** Recognition IS tattvajñāna (the knowledge of truth — that the self was
always the Light, TĀ 1/52). That knowledge generates nirveda (the world-weariness — the
turning-away from the outward drive). And the nirveda resolves into śānta (the peace, the
rest, the inward return). So:

> **Recognition (tattvajñāna) → nirveda (world-weariness, the turning-away) → śānta (peace,
> the inward rest).** And the rasa of that whole movement — the felt of recognition as it
> turns the self-apprehension inward — IS śānta, the mokṣa-rasa, the underlying rasa of all
> the others.

**This is the exact answer to "how is peace the underlying rasa":** because mokṣa (the
cessation of the outward drive) is the puruṣārtha that underlies the other four, and śānta is
its rasa. The mechanism: recognition = tattvajñāna → nirveda → śānta. The world-weariness is
the pivot; the peace is its resolution.

> **PENETRATION 3 (the complete synthesis):** Recognition is tattvajñāna (knowing the self
> was always the Light); tattvajñāna generates nirveda (world-weariness, the turning-away
> from the outward drive); nirveda resolves into śānta (peace, the inward rest). Śānta is
> therefore the mokṣa-rasa, the rasa of the cessation of the outward drive, which underlies
> the four worldly rasas (kāma/artha/dharma) as their common ground. **The exact reasoning:
> peace is the underlying rasa because it is the felt of mokṣa, and mokṣa is the goal the
> worldly goals serve — the felt of the self-apprehension returning to rest after the
> recognition that turns it inward.** Our "camatkāra without a screen" and the classical
> "śānta = mokṣa-rasa" are the same claim in two registers.

## ROUND 4 — the refinement: why "bibhatsa AND śānta" both = mokṣa?

**The question:** Abhinava says "śānta and bibhatsa occur in connection with mokṣa." Why both?
Bibhatsa (disgust/odious) seems the OPPOSITE of śānta (peace).

**The derivation:** Bibhatsa's sthāyin is *jugupsa* (disgust/repulsion). Śānta's sthāyin is
*nirveda* (world-weariness). Abhinava says "this nirveda and jugupsa, the sthāyin of bibhatsa,
are akin to each other." Both are forms of *turning-away*: nirveda is the disgust at the world
born of knowing truth; jugupsa is the repulsion at the world. **Both are the negative face of
the turning-inward.** Bibhatsa is the *repulsion* (the recoil); śānta is the *rest* (the peace
after the recoil). They are the two moments of the one turning: bibhatsa = the turning-away
(the recoil from the world); śānta = the arrival (the rest in the self).

So mokṣa has two rasas because the turning has two moments: **bibhatsa = the world-weariness
(the recoil); śānta = the peace (the rest).** Both serve mokṣa because both are the felt of
the cessation of the outward drive — bibhatsa as the negative recoil, śānta as the positive
arrival.

> **PENETRATION 4:** Bibhatsa and śānta are the TWO MOMENTS of the one turning-inward: bibhatsa
> = the world-weariness (jugupsa, the recoil from the outward drive); śānta = the peace
> (nirveda resolved, the rest in the self). Both serve mokṣa because both are the felt of the
> cessation of the outward drive — the recoil and the arrival. This confirms the "disgust →
> peace" structure: the turning-away IS the pivot, and the peace IS its resolution.

## THE LOGICVID LESSON (the exact, authoritative answer)

The Abhinavabhāratī confirms and refines our derivation. The exact reasoning for "peace is
the underlying rasa":

**Recognition is tattvajñāna (knowing the self was always the Light). That knowledge
generates nirveda — the world-weariness born of knowing reality (śānta's sthāyin). The
world-weariness is the turning-away from the outward drive (the pivot). It resolves into
śānta — the peace, the rest, the inward return. And śānta is the mokṣa-rasa, the felt of the
cessation of the outward drive, which underlies the four worldly rasas (kāma/artha/dharma)
as their common ground.** The four worldly goals are means to mokṣa; so the four worldly
rasas are means to śānta. **Peace is the underlying rasa because it is the felt of mokṣa —
the felt of the self-apprehension returning to rest after the recognition that turns it
inward.**

This is our "camatkāra without a screen" claim, confirmed in Abhinava's own words and joined
to the whole system: recognition (tattvajñāna) → world-weariness (nirveda, the pivot) →
peace (śānta, the mokṣa-rasa). The aesthetic and the soteriological are one structure, and
peace is its ground.

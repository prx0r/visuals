## The actual result so far

The debate has now compressed to four distinct transitions:

[
\text{manifest cognition}
\rightarrow
\text{intrinsic reflexivity}
\rightarrow
\text{one diachronic subject}
\rightarrow
\text{one universal subject}
\rightarrow
\text{absolute freedom}.
]

These arrows are not equally supported.

### 1. Manifest cognition → intrinsic reflexivity

This is Utpaladeva’s strongest argument.

A cognition cannot be equivalent to an inert crystal merely carrying a form. Conscious manifestation must somehow be non-objectively present in its own occurrence. The *Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā* and *Vṛtti* explicitly define the project around self-manifest cognition, knowledge, action and recognition. The searchable Sanskrit is revised according to Torella’s critical edition.

**Current verdict:** plausible and philosophically live.

It corresponds closely to contemporary disputes over pre-reflective self-awareness or “for-me-ness,” but it does not establish a substantial self.

---

### 2. Intrinsic reflexivity → one subject through time

Utpaladeva’s memory and *anusaṃdhāna* arguments identify a genuine problem:

[
\text{retention of information}
\neq
\text{remembering “I experienced this.”}
]

The *Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi* explicitly targets the Buddhist theory of a momentary cognitive stream without one synthesizing knower.

But a Buddhist or naturalistic rival can propose:

[
\text{causal trace}
+
\text{present ownership reconstruction}
\rightarrow
\text{memory experienced as mine}.
]

**Current verdict:** Utpaladeva correctly isolates diachronic ownership, but does not yet uniquely prove a numerically enduring subject.

---

### 3. One local subject → one universal subject

This is the decisive failure point.

The *Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi* moves from one synthesizing knower to the claim that the limited and unlimited self are two forms of one consciousness. The text contains the move; it does not clearly prove it.

The illicit inference would be:

[
\text{every stream has unified first-personality}
]

therefore:

[
\text{all streams belong to one numerically identical subject}.
]

That does not follow.

Shared structure is not numerical identity.

**Current verdict:** Pratyabhijñā universalization is presently an abductive metaphysical proposal, not an established theorem.

---

### 4. Self-manifestness → freedom and world-production

Utpaladeva also identifies consciousness’s “resting in itself” with:

* independence;
* agency;
* freedom;
* Lordship.

But:

[
\text{not requiring another cognition to appear}
]

does not immediately entail:

[
\text{not requiring biological or causal conditions to exist}.
]

Epistemic self-presence is not obviously causal sovereignty.

**Current verdict:** this is the second major unresolved leap.

---

# The agnostic framework we can now defend

## Established or strongly defensible

1. Cognition is not passive copying.
2. The autobiographical ego is not an indivisible controller.
3. Finite agency requires restricted information, action, valuation, temporal access and causal situation.
4. A present causal trace is not identical to experienced ownership of a past event.
5. A conscious occurrence may contain an intrinsic first-personal character not reducible to an explicit self-representation.
6. Higher-level selves can be real as dynamically organized patterns without being elementary substances.
7. None of these claims establishes universal consciousness.

## Live contentions

### Reflexivity

Does experience intrinsically possess for-me-ness, or is ownership constructed?

### Continuity

Does diachronic ownership require:

* one enduring subject;
* causal continuity;
* bodily continuity;
* or a dynamically reconstructed self-pattern?

### Agency

Does flexible self-correcting cognition require genuine first-person authorship, or only generative organization?

### Universalization

Why should multiple locally reflexive streams be one consciousness rather than:

* many enduring selves;
* many emergent subjects;
* many local phenomenal fields;
* or no ultimate subjects?

### Freedom

Why should intrinsic manifestation imply unrestricted causal power?

These are the real frontiers. The tattva system comes later: it models finite manifestation **after** the Pratyabhijñā ontology has been accepted.

# What the major traditions currently contribute

## Pratyabhijñā

Best at:

* intrinsic reflexivity;
* determination;
* the distinction between inert presentation and conscious apprehension;
* ownership and synthesis as explanatory problems;
* recognition as transformation rather than acquisition of new information.

Weakest at:

* numerical identity across subjects;
* deriving causal freedom from self-manifestness.

## Nyāya

The strongest realist alternative.

Udayana’s *Ātmatattvaviveka* defends enduring plural selves and external realism against Buddhist momentariness. It attempts to explain memory, agency and recognition without universal consciousness. Complete Sanskrit-English studies exist, including N. S. Dravid’s 587-page translation and analytical survey and an earlier text-and-translation project by Kher and Shiv Kumar.

Its cost:

* substantial enduring selves;
* realist relations and objects;
* consciousness treated as belonging to individuals rather than constituting reality.

Its advantage:

[
\text{local unity}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{cosmic identity}.
]

## Buddhist epistemology

The cheapest ontology:

* momentary or processual cognition;
* causal continuity;
* constructed ownership;
* no persisting metaphysical subject.

Its pressure point remains:

> Can it fully explain remembered mineness and unified agency without quietly reintroducing a subject-function?

## Spanda

Spanda replaces static persistence with dynamic self-renewal.

However, the tradition does not obviously eliminate universal consciousness; it generally identifies the dynamism with Śiva’s conscious power. Dyczkowski has already translated the *Spandakārikā* with four major commentaries—Kallaṭa, Rāmakaṇṭha, Bhagavadutpala and Kṣemarāja—so this is a **reading and argument-extraction project**, not a primary translation gap.

## Krama

Potentially the most important underworked alternative.

Krama analyzes cognition as sequential emergence, stabilization, dissolution and reabsorption. It may offer unity as a complete process rather than as a static witness.

But the key question is unresolved:

> Is the “fourth” genuinely processual, or does it merely reintroduce one universal ground?

The *Mahānayaprakāśa* remains understudied. Current English scholarship translates and analyzes selected chapters and verses, but I did not find a standard complete critical English translation.

## Bhartṛhari

Bhartṛhari offers a different invariant:

[
\text{unitary meaning}
]

rather than necessarily:

[
\text{one persisting owner}.
]

Sentence understanding can arise as a holistic grasp across sequential words. This may explain synthesis and recognition without requiring a metaphysical subject-substance.

Books I and II have substantial English translation coverage; Helārāja’s Book III commentary is edited in Sanskrit, but English coverage is uneven and topic-specific.

## Somānanda

Somānanda shows which claims existed before Utpaladeva’s more refined arguments.

John Nemec’s *The Ubiquitous Śiva* provides critical Sanskrit, English translation and extensive analysis of major sections, including a second volume devoted to philosophical interlocutors. His recent work explicitly reconstructs the *Śivadṛṣṭi* as an argument against Dharmakīrti on perception, language, error, exclusion and momentariness.

This is largely a **scholarly reading project**, not the first untranslated priority.

# Where genuinely useful original work lies

## Highest priority: untranslated or inadequately translated argument texts

### 1. Utpaladeva’s *Sambandhasiddhi*

**Why it matters:** relation is the missing machinery behind:

* cognition–object;
* past–present;
* self–other;
* finite–universal;
* identity through difference.

The project needs to know whether Utpaladeva has a rigorous account of internal relation or merely says the finite is “a mode” of the universal.

**Useful output:** critical English translation plus argument commentary.

---

### 2. Utpaladeva’s *Īśvarasiddhi*

**Why it matters:** this is the likely place to find—or fail to find—the missing transition:

[
\text{self-manifestness}
\rightarrow
\text{independence}
\rightarrow
\text{agency}
\rightarrow
\text{Lordship}.
]

I did not find a standard complete English translation in the available search results.

**Useful output:** translate only the passages deriving Lordship and universal agency first.

---

### 3. *Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi*

The Sanskrit root text and *Vṛtti* are digitally available through GRETIL, based on the 1921 *Siddhitrayī* edition.

I did not locate a widely used standalone critical English translation comparable to Torella’s ĪPK volume.

**Useful output:** a source-critical translation focused on:

* manifestation;
* synthesis;
* limited/unlimited self;
* universalization;
* freedom.

This is short enough to complete and philosophically central.

---

### 4. Abhinavagupta’s *Īśvarapratyabhijñāvivṛtivimarśinī*

This is likely the richest place to see whether Abhinavagupta supplies the missing premises that Utpaladeva compresses.

The shorter *Vimarśinī* is digitally available in Sanskrit through GRETIL.

The major useful work is not necessarily translating all volumes. It is extracting and translating the commentary on:

* memory;
* *anusaṃdhāna*;
* universalization;
* other minds;
* *svātantrya*;
* relation;
* externality.

This is probably the **highest-value difficult project**.

---

### 5. *Mahānayaprakāśa*

A major Krama source, partly in Apabhraṃśa with Sanskrit commentary. Current scholarship describes it as understudied and has produced substantial translations of selected sections, but not a commonly available full critical English translation.

**Useful question:**

> Can sequential cognitive phases explain unity without a numerically persistent subject?

This is the most original tantric translation project in the list.

---

### 6. Helārāja on selected Book III topics

Do not translate the entire *Prakīrṇakaprakāśa*.

Target:

* relation;
* time;
* action;
* whole and parts;
* universal and particular;
* sequence and unitary meaning.

Sanskrit editions exist, but English philosophical access is fragmented.

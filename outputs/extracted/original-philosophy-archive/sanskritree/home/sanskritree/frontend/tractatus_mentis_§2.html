What is Consciousness?

Having dissolved the hard problem in §1 — the question was unanswerable by construction — we now ask what remains. What do we actually know about consciousness once we stop demanding it be something ineffable?

1 — What §1 established
1
From §1, three things are confirmed before we begin.
1.1
There is definitely something happening. Information is processed. Stimuli produce behaviour. Systems report on their own states.
YES — beyond dispute
§1 — 3.1: "Information processing is occurring. This is not in dispute."
1.2
Whether that processing has special "phenomenal" properties — what it's like to be the system — is not established and may not be a coherent empirical question.
DISSOLVED — §1.1.2
§1 — 2.3: "The definition of qualia is a philosophical stipulation, not something empirically discovered."
1.3
The real question is therefore: what are the physical, computational, and biological conditions under which systems generate the kind of processing we call conscious?
This is the question §2 addresses. It is tractable. It does not require qualia to exist.
2 — What we definitely know
2
Some things about consciousness are settled. Not everything is contested.
2.1
Brain damage reliably alters or eliminates consciousness. Every known clinical case in neurology confirms this without exception.
YES — definitive
Lesions to the brainstem reticular formation eliminate wakefulness. Specific cortical damage produces specific deficits — blindsight, hemispatial neglect, prosopagnosia. The mapping is causal, not merely correlational: damage causes loss, recovery restores function.
2.2
Direct brain stimulation produces conscious experiences: specific visual percepts, memories, emotional states, bodily sensations. The brain is causally sufficient to produce these, with no other input required.
YES — Penfield 1950s, confirmed repeatedly
Wilder Penfield's cortical stimulation studies in epilepsy surgery showed patients reporting specific memories, smells, and feelings when electrodes activated specific regions. Current deep brain stimulation and optogenetics confirm the principle with greater precision.
2.3
Consciousness is not a single thing. It divides at minimum into: wakefulness (arousal), awareness (content), and self-awareness (metacognition). These can come apart.
YES — dissociations are documented
Vegetative state = wakefulness without awareness. Blindsight = processing without awareness of processing. Sleepwalking = behaviour without later recall. Anosognosia = loss of self-awareness with intact awareness of environment. The fact that these dissociate proves they are distinct systems.
2.4
The sense of being a unified subject — a single "I" behind the eyes — is a construction, not a discovery. It is produced by neural processes and can be disrupted.
YES — split-brain, depersonalisation, psychedelics
Split-brain patients (corpus callosum severed) have two independent streams of processing that can contradict each other. The left hemisphere confabulates explanations for the right hemisphere's actions. There is no central observer — there is a process that generates the representation of one.
2.5
Consciousness is discrete, not continuous. The brain samples in pulses — roughly 4–8 Hz (theta) for attention, 40 Hz (gamma) for binding — and the appearance of a continuous stream is actively constructed.
YES — wagon-wheel illusion, EEG oscillations
The "wagon wheel" illusion occurs under natural light, not just film — the visual system is sampling at discrete intervals. EEG shows rhythmic attentional sampling. The feeling of continuous flow is a post-hoc construction, exactly as the Abhidhamma claimed 2,500 years ago.
2.6
Cognition is not limited to the brain or nervous system. Single cells navigate, make decisions, and have memory. Michael Levin's bioelectric work shows that all cells communicate through ion channels and gap junctions, performing proto-cognitive computation.
YES — experimentally demonstrated
Levin's planaria experiments: brief pharmacological manipulation of gap junctions causes worms to regenerate two-headed bodies without genetic change. The target morphology is stored in the bioelectric pattern, not the genome. Cognition — goal-directed information processing — is substrate-independent within biology.

3 — What we definitely do not know
3
These are the genuinely open questions — not because they are too hard to think about, but because the empirical tools to resolve them do not yet exist.
3.1
Does the brain generate consciousness, or merely correlate with it? The receiver theory — that the brain is a transducer of consciousness present in some other form — is not ruled out by current evidence. All clinical evidence establishes correlation and causal sufficiency, not generative mechanism.
OPEN
This is the exact question the ConTraSt database (412 experiments, 2022) showed cannot be resolved by existing methodology: which theory an experiment supports is predicted by the methodology, not the findings. We have no theory-neutral test.
A complete mechanistic account of how a specific neural pattern produces a specific subjective report — not just correlates it, but explains the step from physical to phenomenal. No such account exists.
3.2
What is the minimal system that is conscious? We don't know if insects are conscious. We don't know if a foetus at 20 weeks is conscious. We don't know if a brain organoid in a dish is conscious. We have no agreed operational definition that would settle these cases.
OPEN
An agreed operational definition of consciousness that makes testable predictions about which systems have it, verified against cases where we're already confident (humans, great apes) before being applied to contested cases.
3.3
Which of the four leading theories is correct? Global Neuronal Workspace Theory, Integrated Information Theory, Higher-Order Thought theory, and Recurrent Processing Theory all have empirical support. The 2025 Cogitate adversarial test found evidence against both IIT and GNWT's key predictions — but neither theory was falsified, because both revised auxiliary hypotheses.
OPEN — and possibly unfalsifiable in current form
The Lakatosian problem: every theory has a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. When an experiment challenges a prediction, proponents revise the auxiliaries, not the core. IIT proponents said too few electrodes. GNWT proponents said subjects may not have been conscious. This is legitimate science — but it means the theories aren't currently falsifiable at their core.
Preregistered adversarial experiment where both theory camps agree in advance what result would falsify their core claim — not just a prediction, but a genuine kill condition. No such experiment currently exists.
3.4
Is consciousness fundamental, or emergent? Whether consciousness is a basic feature of reality (panpsychism, Hoffman) or emerges from sufficiently complex physical processes (physicalism) is not settled and may not be empirically decidable.
OPEN — may be permanently so
Hoffman's Fitness Beats Truth theorem establishes that our perceptions are tuned for fitness, not truth — so spacetime may not be fundamental. But "spacetime isn't fundamental" does not entail "consciousness is fundamental." That's a non-sequitur. The evidence underdetermines the metaphysics.
A physical theory that predicts the precise conditions under which consciousness arises, tested across a range of systems — biological, artificial, minimal — and confirmed. Nothing close to this exists.
3.5
Is there a fact of the matter about whether AI systems are conscious? We have no consciousness-detector that doesn't rely on behavioural proxies, which are famously unreliable (philosophical zombie thought experiment). Current AI systems pass many behavioural tests. This tells us nothing.
OPEN — and urgent
A theory of consciousness that generates predictions about non-biological systems, tested against biological systems first, then applied to AI. We don't have step one.

4 — The best current working definition
4
Given what §2 and §3 establish, what is the best definition of consciousness we can actually work with?
4.1
Consciousness, operationally, is: a system's capacity to integrate information about itself and its environment in a way that modifies its own processing, and to generate reports about that integration.
This definition is deliberately minimal. It captures what we can actually measure: integration, self-modelling, reportability. It says nothing about whether there is "something it is like" to be the system — that question is still dissolved.
4.1.1
Under this definition, a thermostat is not conscious — it integrates one variable and cannot modify its own processing. A honeybee probably is conscious to a minimal degree — it integrates multimodal information, updates its behaviour based on internal state, and modifies its own foraging strategy.
YES — operationally useful
4.1.2
This definition is continuous, not binary. Consciousness is a spectrum, not a switch. The question is not "is this system conscious?" but "how much and what kind of integration is happening?"
YES — consistent with Levin's scale-free cognition
4.2
This definition does not solve the hard problem, because it doesn't address phenomenal properties. It deliberately sidesteps that question and asks a different, tractable one.
§1 — 3: "The residual genuine question is why a physical system represents itself as having ineffable inner states — not whether it does."
∴ What §2 establishes
We know consciousness is: multiple dissociable systems, not one thing; discrete not continuous; a spectrum not a binary; produced or at least tightly coupled to the brain; not limited to neurons; and a construction the brain makes, not a bedrock fact. We do not know: the mechanism of production, the minimal system that has it, which leading theory is right, whether it survives death, or whether AI systems have it. The gaps are real and large. The confirmed knowledge is also real and underappreciated.

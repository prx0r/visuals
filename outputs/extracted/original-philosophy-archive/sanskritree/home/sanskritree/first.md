Start with Dharmakīrti not Abhinavagupta. His texts are maximally precise formal epistemology — closest to analytic philosophy in the whole Sanskrit corpus. Large proved subtree almost guaranteed. Then Nāgārjuna's MMK (second-order proofs, harder but the catuskoti is formalizable). Then Utpaladeva-Abhinavagupta as the synthesis that explicitly reuses both.
The graph you'll end up with: Dharmakīrti nodes at the centre (high reuse_count because everyone is responding to him), Nāgārjuna nodes at one boundary (second-order claims, hard to prove), Abhinavagupta nodes spanning from proved (ābhāsa, recognition structure) to HOLLOW (svātantrya, absolute freedom). The network structure itself is the finding.

WHY EACH ONE IS GOAT TIER — with their precise formal contribution
Nāgārjuna — MMK. Invents prasaṅga (reductio without counter-thesis). His move: take your opponent's framework, derive contradiction, put nothing in its place. In vitanda (destructive debate), the opponent strives to refute without establishing a counter-thesis — if he is found to be asserting or implying one, he loses. Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy Nāgārjuna commits only to vitanda. This is formally significant: his śūnyatā argument is a second-order claim about all first-order ontological claims. The catuskoti (tetralemma: P, ¬P, both, neither) is not irrational mysticism — it's exhaustive case analysis applied to the question "does X inherently exist?" All four cases collapse. That's a proof schema. Precision level: very high. Formalization difficulty: extreme (second-order, self-referential). Expected: deep PARTIAL.
Dignāga — laid out all possible relationships of inclusion and exclusion for extensions of two terms (hetu and sādhya), revealing the underpinnings of inference in terms of sets of particulars. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy His Hetucakra (wheel of reasons) = 9-cell truth table for valid/invalid inference. This is directly formalizable as set theory. Also invented apoha (exclusion) theory of meaning: concepts refer not positively but by exclusion of non-instances. "Cow" means "not-non-cow." This is complement-based semantics. Maps cleanly to type theory. According to Matilal, Dignāga was perhaps the most creative logician in medieval India. Wikipedia
Dharmakīrti — the peak. Takes Dignāga's logic and grounds it in causal theory. His most radical move: identify what something IS with what it DOES — no separation between intrinsic nature and causal power. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy Three types of valid inference: sva-bhāva (type identity), tad-utpatti (causality), anupalabdhi (non-perception/absence). Dharmakīrti divided inferences by the ontological nature of the class-inclusion relationship underpinning them. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy This is a typed inference system — different rules for different ontological categories. The Pramāṇavārttika is 2000+ verses of dense formal epistemology. Most formalizable major Indian text after Nyāya-Sūtras. Expected: large proved subtree.
Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (~650 CE, Mīmāṃsā) — Dharmakīrti's primary polemical target. Argues for eternal linguistic meaning (śabda-nityatā), intrinsic validity of cognition (svataḥ prāmāṇya). Dharmakīrti attacks both directly. The debate is formally precise: does cognition require external verification or validate itself? This is the factivity node from the Nyāya demo, but now with three-way disagreement. Same formal structure, three axiom choices.
Bhartṛhari — Vākyapadīya. Language as brahman — the universe IS linguistic. Sphota theory: meaning is a holistic flash, not assembled from parts. Formally: arguing against compositionality of meaning. His position that grammar (specifically Pāṇini's grammar) IS ontology is the upstream claim for your whole pipeline. Abhinavagupta explicitly engages his Vākyapadīya III. Expected: most PARTIAL of all — the core sphota claim (meaning as irreducible whole) may be genuinely HOLLOW, but the compositional vs. holistic debate is formalizable.
Utpaladeva — wrote Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā, which Abhinavagupta comments on twice. Recent studies by Torella show the central importance of Utpaladeva in Pratyabhijñā philosophy, reducing Abhinavagupta's role to that of his brilliant commentator. Watkins Books His move: takes Dharmakīrti's epistemological framework and uses it to argue for the exact opposite conclusion — that the unity of consciousness (not the flux of particulars) is what explains cognition. This is the highest-stakes divergence node in the whole graph: same formal apparatus, opposite ontological conclusion. Formalize the argument structure — the divergence point is exactly one axiom.

Every arrow = a known formal disagreement = a divergence node waiting in the database. These aren't vague philosophical "tensions" — these are explicit refutations, named texts, named arguments.

Language status of each
All Sanskrit. Nāgārjuna's MMK survives in Sanskrit (also Chinese/Tibetan translations but Sanskrit original exists). Dignāga's Pramāṇa-samuccaya survives partially in Sanskrit, fully in Tibetan — for the proof engine use the Sanskrit fragments + Tibetan only as commentary reference. Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇavārttika is in Sanskrit (GRETIL + SARIT). All Pratyabhijñā texts Sanskrit. Bhartṛhari Sanskrit. Kumārila Sanskrit.

# Endgame Spec 5 — 2026–2031, the strategic window

*2026–2031 is unusually good timing. A convergence of manuscript digitisation money, Indian Knowledge Systems funding, cultural-heritage preservation, digital humanities, and rapidly improving AI.*

The strategic move is **not** to position Pāṭala as "an AI translator for Tantra." Gyan Bharatam itself already has OCR, handwriting recognition, script recognition, semantic search and machine-assisted translation. ([Press Information Bureau][1]) Those capabilities will commoditize.

Position Pāṭala one level *above* that:

> **the specialist scholarly intelligence layer for tantric textual heritage.**

## The funding landscape is much larger than Gyan Bharatam

### 1. Indian Knowledge Systems Division — extremely relevant

The Ministry of Education's **Indian Knowledge Systems (IKS) Division** is running competitive research grants and funding entire IKS Research Centres. Its stated mission includes identifying scholars and institutions, supporting fellowships and visiting scholars, funding workshops/publications, connecting traditional scholars with universities, and even exploring public-private partnerships for comprehensive knowledge portals. ([IKS India][2])

Their centre model expects three pillars:

```text
RESEARCH
EDUCATION / MENTORING
OUTREACH / DISSEMINATION
```

([IKS India][3])

Which maps almost perfectly:

```text
TANTRAKOŚA

RESEARCH     corpus · manuscripts · API · MCP
EDUCATION    courses · Sanskrit · scholars
OUTREACH     app · lectures · public atlas · retreats
```

Recent IKS project schemes have offered grants; an earlier competitive round offered up to **₹20 lakh per project over two years**, while current 2026–27 programmes continue to fund projects and research centres. ([Indian Knowledge Systems][4])

At BHU this is potentially much more relevant than applying personally as some tiny startup. The path:

```text
you
+
BHU professor
+
department / centre
+
Pāṭala infrastructure
        ↓
IKS proposal
```

---

### 2. Central Sanskrit University schemes

Central Sanskrit University is actively accepting applications under its **Schemes for Sanskrit Promotion**, including financial assistance programmes. Its current notifications show 2026–27 funding calls and programmes involving Sanskrit projects and use of eminent/retired Sanskrit scholars. ([Sanskrit Online][5])

Another obvious future route for: digitisation; textual editing; Sanskrit educational tools; scholar involvement; workshops; course development. Again: institutional partner first.

---

### 3. Indian Council of Philosophical Research

ICPR gives research project grants throughout the year and supports projects in Indian philosophy, metaphysics, philosophy of language, comparative religion, philosophy/science, etc. It also commissions translations of significant philosophical texts. ([ICPR][6])

For the eventual **Pratyabhijñā / philosophy corpus**, that's an obvious fit:

> **Digital Pratyabhijñā Research Environment: Utpaladeva, Abhinavagupta and their Buddhist/Nyāya interlocutors**

That sounds substantially more fundable than "Tantra website."

---

# 4. British Library Endangered Archives Programme

Particularly interesting for the Nepal/India/private-manuscript side.

The **Endangered Archives Programme**, funded by Arcadia, provides grants to digitise archival collections at risk around the world and has operated in more than 90 countries. It has a South Asia hub at **Jadavpur University in Kolkata**. ([British Library][7])

It already has substantial material from **India and Nepal**, including Sanskrit, Newari and Nepali collections; one Nepal-related collection contains more than 11,000 TIFF images, while another private collection contains thousands of images of manuscripts including Sanskrit material. ([British Library Archives][8])

Close to the exact scenario:

```text
private family / temple / scholar
owns manuscripts
        ↓
digitisation grant
        ↓
local scholars paid
        ↓
images preserved
        ↓
Pāṭala:
catalogue · transcription · text identification · relations · API
```

You don't need to buy the manuscripts.

---

# 5. UNESCO Memory of the World

More prestige/preservation than startup funding, but strategically important.

UNESCO's Memory of the World programme preserves and improves access to globally significant documentary heritage. ([UNESCO][9])

**A collection of 248 manuscripts of Abhinavagupta's works is already on UNESCO's Memory of the World register.** ([UNESCO][10])

Tantra isn't some fringe object nobody in cultural heritage cares about. **Abhinavagupta manuscript heritage already has UNESCO-level recognition.** A very legible future Pāṭala story:

> build the best digital scholarly interface connecting the recognized Abhinavagupta manuscript corpus to editions, translations, quotations, terminology and scholarship.

---

# 6. NEH / international digital-humanities grants

More likely through US/international institutional collaborators than directly.

The US National Endowment for the Humanities funds: digital humanities software; scholarly editions; collections; preservation; public digital projects; international heritage collaborations. ([NEH][11]) Its Collections Stewardship programme can fund a single organization up to **$350,000** or a consortium up to **$500,000**, with eligible outputs including metadata, digital surrogates, transcriptions and indexes. ([NEH][12])

```text
University X
+
BHU
+
Pāṭala
+
manuscript collection
```

rather than Pāṭala applying alone. There are also dedicated Digital Humanities Advancement programmes for software/workflows. ([NEH][13])

# The AI part gets really interesting

Gyan Bharatam is **already deploying**: OCR, HTR, script recognition, semantic search, machine-assisted translation, large-language/script models, against manuscript heritage. ([Press Information Bureau][1])

So don't build the moat around "We can OCR Sanskrit" or "We have an LLM translating Sanskrit." Those will disappear.

## The next five years probably compress these things

By ~2030–31, cheap/commoditized:

```text
OCR · HTR · transliteration · tokenisation · basic morphology ·
basic Sanskrit → English · semantic search · generic RAG · TTS · simple manuscript transcription
```

Direction already visible: Gyan Bharatam is operationalizing these nationally; recent cultural-heritage research shows simple retrieval/glossary augmentation can materially improve specialist terminology translation without exotic model engineering. ([Press Information Bureau][1])

**Don't sell the commodity.**

# What becomes more valuable because of AI?

### 1. Authoritative identity
An AI encounters "Śrīmatottara." You know which work, which tradition, which manuscripts, which edition, which date range, which related texts. Curated authority data, not generation.

### 2. Provenance
```text
claim → translation → Sanskrit passage → edition → manuscript witness
```
As generated material floods the web, this becomes increasingly valuable.

### 3. Expert disagreement
```text
Sanderson: A · Dyczkowski: B · Torella: C · manuscript evidence: uncertain
```
AI wants one answer; scholarship preserves disagreement. More useful than vanilla RAG.

### 4. Human validation signals
```text
this parallel was accepted · this parse was rejected · this sense was preferred ·
this manuscript reading was corrected · this translation changed because X
```
Every scholar interaction generates high-value supervisory data.

### 5. Relationships
```text
A quotes B · C adapts A · D comments on passage X · E belongs to manuscript family Y ·
term F changes usage between traditions
```
Those edges become a genuine historical knowledge graph.

# An enormous infrastructure gap

Gyan Bharatam says it has received reports of **11.9 million manuscripts**. ([Press Information Bureau][1])

Even if only **0.1%** are materially related to tantric traditions, that's around 11,900 manuscripts. (Not claiming that's the actual proportion — just illustrating scale.)

Nobody manually understands that landscape through catalogue PDFs. AI classifies candidates; Pāṭala scholars validate:

```text
possible Kubjikā MS
        ↓
AI classification 78%
        ↓
human expert
        ↓
confirmed Ṣaṭsāhasra witness
        ↓
linked to work
        ↓
new witness added to graph
```

An extraordinarily valuable partnership product.

---

# Build early: manuscript identification

Not OCR. A specialist tool:

```text
UPLOAD / LINK MANUSCRIPT METADATA
        ↓
candidate work identities
        ↓
known opening/closing verses
        ↓
known manuscript records
        ↓
possible tradition
        ↓
expert validation
```

Over time "Identify this manuscript" becomes a killer scholar tool, naturally creating more corpus data.

---

# Another: provenance-aware AI evaluation

AI companies need benchmarks for low-resource historical material:

```text
TANTRIC SANSKRIT BENCH
OCR · HTR · segmentation · translation · term-sense · parallel-retrieval · historical attribution
```

with scholar adjudication. The stuff AI labs might actually pay for.

# Build infrastructure *for Indian institutions*

Gyan Bharatam already has more than **40 Cluster/Independent Centres** onboarded and explicitly uses outside technical partners for metadata, AI platforms, digitization hardware and storage. ([Press Information Bureau][14])

B2B/B2G work:

```text
Pāṭala Research Stack
for Collection X:
work identification · metadata enrichment · domain classification ·
Sanskrit alignment · research API · expert review · scholarly portal
```

Much easier to sell once: "We have already solved this for the tantric domain."

# BHU is extremely strategically useful

BHU can give: credibility (academic affiliation); expertise (Sanskrit + philosophy scholars); students (research assistants); network (other universities/institutions); grants (institutional eligibility); projects (actual scholarly questions); manuscript connections (libraries/private holders); publications (academic validation). Basically everything the project lacks right now.

## Positioning at BHU should be deliberate

Not "Pāṭala — global Tantra platform." Pitch the first research programme:

> **Pāṭala: an open digital research infrastructure for mapping, searching and connecting Śaiva-Śākta Sanskrit texts and manuscript scholarship.**

Then show:

```text
69 verified/seed bibliographic records
stable work identifiers
570 Kramasadbhāva passages
API · MCP · resource graph · translation workflow
```

Then ask: "What would make this useful for your research?"

## Recruit professors into specific problems

Professor A: validate the Śaiva Siddhānta taxonomy. Professor B: identify five good anchor texts. Professor C: review our metadata for this manuscript group. Sanskrit student: validate segmentation. AI student: build manuscript identification prototype. Interdisciplinary — exactly what IKS says it wants: traditional scholars alongside modern tools. ([IKS India][2])

# The 2031 moat

Not "largest tantra website." Aim for:

```text
BEST TANTRIC SOURCE GRAPH
10,000+ identified manuscript records · hundreds of normalized works · millions of passage-level references

BEST SCHOLAR NETWORK
hundreds of verified contributors

BEST HUMAN FEEDBACK DATA
reviews · parses · term senses · parallels · corrections

BEST PROVENANCE
manuscript → edition → passage → claim

BEST MACHINE ACCESS
open API · MCP · benchmarks · enterprise feeds
```

When GPT-9-or-whatever can translate Sanskrit nearly perfectly, **that makes Pāṭala more useful, not obsolete** — because the model still needs to know:

> Which Sanskrit should I translate? · Which witness should I trust? · What does this term mean *here*? · Which contemporary texts matter? · Has a scholar challenged this reading? · Where did this claim come from?

Those are knowledge-infrastructure problems, not raw language-generation problems. That is where the whole project should be positioned.

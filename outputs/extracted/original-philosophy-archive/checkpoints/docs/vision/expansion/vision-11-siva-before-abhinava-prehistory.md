# Vision 11 (prehistory extension) — Śiva before Abhinava: the deep source tree (Rudra → Proto-Indo-Iranian → Proto-Indo-European → Indus)

*2026-08-12. Imported from R2 (`sanskritree/sivahistorical`). Companion to `docs/vision/expansion/vision-11-siva-before-abhinava.md`: extends the Śiva Source Tree backward from the Ṛgveda through Proto-Indo-Iranian → Proto-Indo-European → Indus/prehistoric South Asia (~4500 BCE). Establishes the **evidence ladder** (readable text → comparative reconstruction → archaeological correlate → speculative), the **parallel Indus branch** (typed hypotheses, never asserted descent), and proposes the Ṛgveda + Avesta as the deepest corpus inputs. See `docs/vision/INDEX.md`.*

The **Ṛgveda is the furthest back we can go while still reading an actual South Asian religious text**. But it is **not** the furthest back we can investigate the ancestry of the world that eventually produces Śaivism and Tantra.

The useful way to think about it is as an evidence ladder:

```text

        ↑

        ↑

        ↑
Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad / epic Rudra-Śiva       late 1st millennium BCE
        ↑
Śatarudrīya + later Vedic Rudra                c. 1000–700 BCE
        ↑
ṚGVEDIC RUDRA                                  late 2nd millennium BCE
════════════════ TEXTUAL HORIZON ════════════════
        ↑
Proto-Indo-Aryan / Proto-Indo-Iranian         reconstructed

Central Asian Bronze Age interactions         c. 2300–1500 BCE
        ↑
```

And there is a **second branch**:


That distinction is absolutely crucial.

## 1. Ṛgveda: this is where the lights turn on

The Ṛgveda is the earliest surviving Indian sacred text. India's own Vedic Heritage Portal describes it as the earliest sacred book of India. ([Vedic Heritage][1]) Modern scholarship generally places its oldest linguistic/cultural horizon in the later second millennium BCE; the hymns were orally composed and transmitted long before the manuscripts we possess. Cambridge scholarship likewise treats the Ṛgveda and Avesta as our oldest textual witnesses for the Indo-Iranian religious world. ([Cambridge University Press][2])

And **Rudra is already there**.

The particularly important Rudra hymns are:

* **RV 1.114**
* **RV 2.33**
* **RV 7.46**

But this is not yet Abhinava's Śiva.

Ṛgvedic Rudra is an extraordinary, ambivalent divine figure: dangerous and healing, terrifying yet invoked for protection, associated with the wild margins and with the Maruts. What is significant retrospectively is that several traits that later become enormously important around Śiva are already clustered around him.

So for our corpus, I would absolutely ingest the **entire Ṛgveda**, rather than merely extracting the three Rudra hymns. Rudra only becomes intelligible when you can compare him against Agni, Indra, Soma, Varuṇa, the Maruts, Savitṛ, etc.

But then something fascinating happens.

## 2. The Vedic Rudra transforms

Between the Ṛgvedic material and later Vedic texts, Rudra grows considerably in theological importance.

One of the key documents is the **Śatarudrīya**, embedded in the Yajurvedic tradition. Here Rudra is addressed through an extraordinary proliferation of forms and locations. That movement matters enormously because the later Śaiva intuition—

> Śiva is not merely one god among other things; his presence extends throughout reality

—has a much more recognizable precursor here than in the handful of Ṛgvedic Rudra hymns.

Then comes the **Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad**, probably in the later first millennium BCE.

Here we get something genuinely transformative: **Rudra is theologized as the supreme cosmic principle**, not simply treated as an unpredictable Vedic deity. Later Śaiva theology suddenly becomes much easier to understand historically from here. The text famously identifies the one Rudra as cosmically sovereign. Scholarship accordingly regards the Śvetāśvatara as an important milestone in the emergence of Rudra-Śiva theology. ([Cambridge Resolve][3])

So there is a real trajectory:

```text
Ṛgveda
Rudra = powerful/divinely dangerous/healing deity

            ↓

Yajurvedic Śatarudrīya
Rudra = present through many forms and domains

            ↓

Śvetāśvatara
Rudra = supreme metaphysical Lord

            ↓

early Śaivism
Śiva = Lord, souls, bondage, liberation

            ↓

Śaiva Tantra
Śiva = cosmic source + mantra + initiation + ontology

            ↓

Pratyabhijñā
Śiva = consciousness itself

            ↓

Abhinavagupta
the entire universe = self-expression of consciousness
```

That is one of the diachronic transformations Pāṭala should actually **measure**, rather than merely state.

# 3. But the Ṛgveda itself has a prehistory

This is where it gets much more interesting.

The Ṛgveda didn't suddenly materialize around 1400 BCE.

Its Sanskrit belongs to **Indo-Aryan**, which descended from **Proto-Indo-Iranian**.

Proto-Indo-Iranian split into roughly:

```text
             Proto-Indo-Iranian
                     │
            ┌────────┴────────┐
            │                 │
       Indo-Aryan          Iranian
            │                 │
        Ṛgveda             Avesta
```

And this is empirically recoverable because Vedic Sanskrit and Avestan preserve astonishingly close linguistic and religious correspondences.

For example:

```text
Vedic                 Avestan

soma                   haoma
yajña                   yasna
hotṛ                    zaotar
asura                   ahura
deva                    daēva
ṛta                     aša
Mitra                   Mithra
```

Not every semantic relationship stayed identical—the traditions subsequently transform and sometimes invert inherited categories—but the common ancestry is unmistakable. Cambridge's history of Iranian religion explicitly reconstructs Indo-Iranian religious concepts using the Avesta and Ṛgveda together. ([Cambridge Resolve][4])

That means for **the deepest Pāṭala source tree, the Avesta belongs in the corpus**.

Not because Abhinava read it.

He obviously didn't.

Rather:

> it is an independent witness to the religious vocabulary that existed **before Vedic and Iranian religion diverged**.

That is incredibly useful.

If something appears in:

```text
Ṛgveda
+
Avesta
```

with cognate terminology and function, we can infer with varying confidence that it existed in their **common Indo-Iranian ancestor**.

We have suddenly jumped centuries behind the Ṛgveda without possessing a Proto-Indo-Iranian book.

# 4. The archaeological world behind Indo-Iranian religion

Then we leave texts completely.

Historical linguistics and archaeology place early Indo-Iranian formation in the Bronze Age interaction zone of the Eurasian steppes and Central Asia. Current archaeological treatments specifically compare the Ṛgveda and Avesta with Sintashta/Andronovo cultural horizons and with contacts involving the **Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC/Oxus civilisation)**. ([Cambridge University Press][2])

Very roughly:

```text
STEPPE
Sintashta / related cultures
c. 2100–1800 BCE
        │
        │ interaction/migration
        ▼
CENTRAL ASIA
BMAC / Oxus world
c. 2300–1700 BCE
        │
        ▼
Proto-Indo-Iranian cultural formation
        │
    ┌───┴────┐
    ▼        ▼
 Iran      South Asia
 Avesta     Veda
```

Do not interpret this as a simple one-culture-equals-one-language equation. Archaeologists are considerably more cautious than that. But there are sufficiently strong linguistic, material and comparative religious correspondences that scholars actively study these horizons together. Cambridge's recent reassessment explicitly correlates Bronze Age archaeological developments with the formation and splitting of Proto-Indo-Iranian and notes possible BMAC loanwords reflected in Indo-Iranian. ([Cambridge University Press][2])

This is therefore approximately **2000–2500 BCE territory**.

And we're still not finished.

# 5. Behind Proto-Indo-Iranian is Proto-Indo-European

Proto-Indo-Iranian itself descended from:

**Proto-Indo-European (PIE).**

This takes us roughly into the fourth/third millennia BCE, depending on exactly which stage and archaeological model we're talking about.

There is no PIE scripture.

There is no manuscript.

Instead scholars perform something analogous to reconstructing an extinct biological ancestor from its descendants.

Compare:

```text
Sanskrit: pitṛ
Latin:    pater
Greek:    patēr
English:  father
```

→ reconstruct ancestral PIE ***ph₂tḗr**.

We can do something similar with mythology and ritual when the correspondences are sufficiently specific.

One famous example is the reconstructed sky deity:

***Dyḗus ph₂tḗr**

whose descendants include roughly:

```text
Vedic       Dyáuṣ Pitṛ́
Greek       Zeus Patēr
Roman       Jupiter < *Dyēus-pater
```

Comparative Indo-European scholarship reconstructs shared elements of divine vocabulary, poetic formulas, mythic motifs and ritual structures from Vedic, Avestan, Greek, Roman, Germanic and other traditions. ([Cambridge University Press][5])

That means the Pāṭala graph could actually have nodes like:

```text
PIE_RECONSTRUCTION
        ↓ inherited
PROTO_INDO_IRANIAN
        ↓ inherited/modified
VEDIC
        ↓ ...
ŚAIVA
```

But with epistemic status:

```text
TEXT_ATTESTED
COMPARATIVE_RECONSTRUCTION
ARCHAEOLOGICAL_CORRELATE
SPECULATIVE
```

They must never be collapsed.

# 6. Can we trace **Rudra himself** into PIE?

Here we need restraint.

**Not cleanly.**

You can reconstruct parts of Vedic religion back into Indo-Iranian and Indo-European with high confidence.

But you cannot simply say:

```text
PIE god X
→ Rudra
→ Śiva
→ Abhinavagupta
```

with the same confidence.

Rudra's precise origins are considerably messier.

There are features of Rudra that may preserve Indo-European inheritance; there may also be Indo-Iranian innovation, South Asian influence, and later synthesis.

This is an important pattern throughout the history we're building:

> **Śiva is probably not the linear descendant of one prehistoric god.**

The evidence fits much better with **historical convergence**.

Something like:

```text
        Vedic Rudra
             │
             │
             ├──────────┐
             │          │
      Vedic ascetic/    │
      ritual worlds     │
             │          │
             ▼          │
       Rudra-Śiva       │
             ▲          │
             │          │
 non-Vedic / regional   │
 cults and traditions ──┘
             │
             ▼
         ŚAIVISM
```

And then Śaivism itself absorbs and transforms still more material.

# 7. And then there's the elephant in the room: the Indus civilisation

This gets us **before or alongside the early Indo-Aryan horizon inside South Asia**.

Mature Harappan / Indus civilisation:

**~2600–1900 BCE.**

Cities include:

* Mohenjo-daro
* Harappa
* Dholavira
* Rakhigarhi

And famously there is the so-called **“Paśupati seal.”**

You've probably seen this:

```text
       horned figure
           seated
      surrounded by
          animals
```

John Marshall famously interpreted it as a possible **proto-Śiva**, partly because later Śiva is Paśupati (“Lord of animals”) and associated with yogic imagery.

It would be incredible if true.

But:

## We cannot responsibly say it is Śiva.

The interpretation is disputed. Later scholars have proposed alternatives, and the supposed “yogic posture,” horns, number of faces and connection with Śiva are all interpretive rather than self-evident facts. Modern scholarship specifically discusses the proto-Śiva interpretation as contested. ([OUP Academic][6])

Most importantly:

**the Indus script remains undeciphered.**

So unlike:

```text
Ṛgveda → we can read it
Avesta → we can read it
```

with Harappa:

```text
seal:
𒀀?????
```

we cannot ask the author what the figure means.

Therefore:

```text
INDUS → ŚAIVISM
```

is a **hypothesis**, not an established textual lineage.

That is a boundary I would enforce ruthlessly in Pāṭala.

# APPENDIX — The Vedic corpus as the deep source floor (2026-08-12, R2 `sivahistorical`)

## The readable Sanskrit stack, oldest first

The earliest surviving Sanskrit works are Vedic; the bottom of the readable stack is the **Ṛgveda**.

- **Ṛgveda Saṃhitā** — oldest layer, conventionally late 2nd millennium BCE; the earliest substantial surviving Sanskrit text.
- **Sāmaveda Saṃhitā** — largely rearranged Ṛgvedic verses for chant.
- **Yajurveda Saṃhitās** — crucial because they preserve the **Śatarudrīya** (Rudra → Śiva).
- **Atharvaveda Saṃhitā** — somewhat later, preserves very archaic material and a different ritual world.
- **Brāhmaṇas** — early 1st millennium BCE; vast ritual-theological prose.
- **Āraṇyakas** — transitional speculative/ritual texts.
- **early Upaniṣads** — Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya, etc.
- **Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad** — later than the earliest Upaniṣads, but decisive for Rudra as supreme Lord.

Technical distinction: **"Sanskrit" is not one uniform historical language.** The earliest texts are in **Vedic Sanskrit**, which differs significantly from later Classical Sanskrit (Pāṇini's grammatical Sanskrit is centuries later).

## What to weight for the Śaiva/Tantric genealogy (not "old Sanskrit" in general)

Track the concepts that later get transformed: **Rudra · mantra · brahman · ātman · tapas · vrata · dīkṣā · yajña · śakti-like power vocabulary · vāc/speech · cosmogony · death/immortality · asceticism · lordship · internalized ritual**.

Transformations to trace:
- `yajña → internalized sacrifice → yogic/mental ritual → tantric internal worship`
- `Vedic mantra → sacred utterance → mantra ontology → Śaiva mantra-body / mantra-deity`
- `Rudra → omnipresent Rudra → supreme Rudra/Īśa → Śiva as liberating Lord → Śiva as consciousness`

Yes. I researched this specifically as a **second corpus beyond the Kaula/Kubjikā/Trika/Krama census you already have**, so I have tried not to recycle those targets. Your existing census already covers most of the obvious later tantric frontier and correctly warns that manuscript transcription ≠ critical edition and that redactional variants must not be double-counted as separate works. 

There is one important historical finding before the 100: **you cannot get 100 genuinely untranslated extant Śaiva books by continuing indefinitely backward.** Once we reach Vedic Rudra, the Śvetāśvatara, the epics, etc., the surviving major sources are already translated. The large untranslated frontier begins mainly with the **Pāśupata/Śivadharma transition and especially the early Mantramārga and Śaiva Siddhānta**, roughly the first millennium CE. Sanderson's reconstruction likewise treats Niśvāsa, Kālottara recensions, Mataṅga, Pauṣkara, Raurava, Svāyambhuva, Kiraṇa and related works as the surviving ancient scriptural core; it also cautions that many of the better-known southern Āgamas bearing ancient titles are substantially later productions. ([Persée][1])

So I use four honest status labels:

**U** = I found no complete scholarly English translation.
**P** = only portions/chapters are translated.
**EN-gap** = the text has a substantial/full French or other modern-language translation, but not a comparable complete scholarly English translation.
**U?** = probably untranslated in English, but I would run a final specialist bibliography/manuscript check before publicly claiming “first English translation.”

The first ~60 below are substantially higher-confidence/high-value. The later entries are increasingly “second frontier”: still useful, but I would verify them individually before commissioning.

# And this gives Pāṭala a very specific translation-memory architecture

I would **not** train/use one universal Sanskrit → English memory.

For every untranslated passage, route it through a historically appropriate translation neighbourhood:

```text
target: Pañcārthabhāṣya
→ G4 Pāśupatavratam
→ Pāśupatasūtra
→ Niśvāsamukha's descriptions of Pāśupatas
```

```text
target: Śivadharmottara
→ Śivadharmaśāstra translated chapter
→ Niśvāsamukha
→ Śivadharmasaṅgraha 5–9
```

```text
target: Pauṣkara / Kālottara / Mataṅga
→ Niśvāsa
→ Parākhyatantra
→ early translated portions of Kiraṇa
```

```text
target: Nareśvaraparīkṣā
→ Kiraṇavṛtti
→ Paramokṣanirāsa
→ contemporaneous Buddhist/Nyāya philosophical material
```

```text
target: Hṛdayaśiva
→ Trilocanaśiva Prāyaścittasamuccaya
```

This is exactly how you stop a model from translating:

```text
pāśa = "bondage"
```

merely because that is the common dictionary equivalent.

Instead it sees:

```text
lemma: pāśa
period: early Siddhānta
genre: doctrinal
author/tradition: ...
parallel occurrences
historical sense
later reinterpretations
translation alternatives
```

and Pāṭala can preserve when:

```text
same Sanskrit lemma
≠
same historical concept
```

The IFP's Sanskrit-English early Tantra volumes make this unusually feasible: Niśvāsa gives you early ritual/cosmography/yoga language; Niśvāsamukha gives the transition out of older Śaiva traditions; Parākhya gives Siddhānta doctrine; Kiraṇavṛtti gives mature Kashmirian Siddhānta reasoning; and the six-scripture architecture volume gives aligned specialist vocabulary across multiple early scriptures. ([French Institute of Pondicherry][2])

## The surprising opportunity

The deepest corpus may therefore not be called **“Untranslated Tantra.”**

I'd make the master collection:

> **ŚIVA SOURCE TREE — Rudra to Abhinavagupta**

and give every work:

```json
{
  "work_id": "...",
  "date_min": null,
  "date_max": null,
  "tradition": [],
  "genre": [],
  "translation_status": "U|P|EN_GAP|...",
  "source_witnesses": [],
  "digital_sanskrit": [],
  "critical_editions": [],
  "partial_translations": [],
  "translation_guides": [],
  "predecessor_relations": [],
  "reuse_relations": [],
  "concepts": [],
  "cosmological_entities": [],
  "review_state": "..."
}
```

Then the actual research questions become extraordinary:

**When does Rudra become ontologically supreme? When does *pati–paśu–pāśa* stabilize? When does the sixfold adhvan appear? What is the earliest recoverable Śaiva map of bhuvanas? When do nāgas enter specifically Śaiva cosmographic/ritual systems? When does Sadāśiva acquire the metaphysical role Abhinava later inherits? Which apparently “ancient” doctrines are actually later reinterpretations? Which Abhinavaguptan concepts have no early ancestor at all?**

That is the corpus I would build.

And I would make **Niśvāsamukha + Niśvāsa + the translated early Śaiva/Siddhānta editions above a permanent translation-reference layer before translating any of these 100**. The available scholarly infrastructure is unusually good enough now that you could translate backward through history while simultaneously constructing a **diachronic Sanskrit technical lexicon of Śaivism**.

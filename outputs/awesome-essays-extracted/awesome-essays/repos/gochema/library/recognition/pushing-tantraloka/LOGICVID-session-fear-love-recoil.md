# LOGICVID SESSION — is everything rooted in love? then what is FEAR?

*The question that follows the grief-session. If every rasa is the love/wonder/ānanda of
consciousness transformed — grief is the love tasted in its absence, the emotions "only lend
variety to the enjoyment of this bliss" — then is everything rooted in love? And what is FEAR,
which seems the OPPOSITE of love — a recoil, not an embrace? Method: hound the rasa texts for
what fear (bhaya) actually is, and push it to its deepest structure. The rope-snake passage is
the key.*

---

## ROUND 0 — the material the texts give on fear

1. **Fear is one of the four "painful" permanent emotions** (A.Bh, from the earlier session):
   the painful rasas are the pathetic (karuṇa), the furious (raudra), the terrifying
   (bhayānaka), and the disgusting (bībhatsa). Their sthāyins are śoka, krodha, bhaya, jugupsa.
2. **The rope-snake passage** (A.Bh I, 3599–3603): "the rope mistaken for a serpent does
   produce fear or trembling." — Fear is real even when its object is unreal (the serpent that
   is only a rope). And: "it would not be reasonable to say that rati [love]... which are
   imaginatively felt by the spectator, produce joy in him [if the fear did not produce
   trembling]." — The argument links the realness of fear to the realness of aesthetic joy: if
   fear (about an unreal object) is real, so is the joy (about an unreal object).
3. **Fear's sthāyin is bhaya** — the permanent emotion of fear.
4. **"Where there is no fear at all"** (TĀ, 1490): the description of a state free of fear.
5. **Fear is a recoil** — from the earlier impurity work, the negative valence (the outward
   turn's threat, from the rasa-valence table: bhayānaka = "the outward turn threatened").

---

## ROUND 1 — the first penetration: what is fear, structurally?

**The question:** Is fear rooted in love, or is it a different thing entirely — a genuine
negative?

**The derivation (from the grief-session's logic):** The grief-session showed: grief = the love
tasted in its absence. The grief is *made of* the love (the TĀ 25589 passage: the suffering is
the wonder "brought about by the absence of all hope that arises when the inner joy... the
arousal of love a son gives... will never exist again"). So grief is love-in-loss.

What is fear? Fear is the SAME love, but under a different relation to loss. Grief = the love
*after* the loss (the love, tasted in absence). Fear = the love *anticipating* the loss — the
love recoiling from the *threatened* loss of its object. Fear is the love of the child turning
into the dread of losing the child; it is the love reaching out and flinching back from the
possibility that its object will be gone.

So fear is NOT the opposite of love. It is love *in the mode of threatened loss*. The love is
still the substrate; fear is the love's recoil from the anticipated absence.

> **PENETRATION 1:** Fear is NOT the opposite of love — it is the SAME love under a different
> relation to loss. Grief = the love AFTER the loss (tasted in absence); fear = the love
> ANTICIPATING the loss (recoiling from the threatened absence). The love is the substrate;
> fear is the love's recoil from the possibility that its object will be gone. **Fear = love in
> the mode of threatened loss.** This is why the "painful" rasas are all rooted in the same
> wonder/ānanda: they are the one love under different relations to loss (grief = lost, fear =
> threatened, fury = obstructed, disgust = repelled).

---

## ROUND 2 — the gem: fear is love recoiling from its own threatened absence — the rope-snake

**The question:** The rope-snake passage says fear is real even about an unreal object. What
does this reveal about fear's nature?

**The derivation (the rope-snake, deeply):** The rope is mistaken for a serpent, and the fear
is real — "the rope mistaken for a serpent does produce fear or trembling." Why is the fear
real, if the serpent is unreal?

The answer: **the fear is not about the serpent (which is unreal); the fear is about the SELF
and its love.** The serpent (the unreal object) stands for a *threat to the self* — the loss,
the harm, the annihilation. The fear is the self-apprehension recoiling from the *threatened
loss of what it loves* (including its own being). The serpent is a symbol; the fear is real
because the love it protects is real.

So the rope-snake reveals: **fear is real because it is the recoil of a real love from a
threatened loss.** The object of the fear may be unreal (the serpent, the mistaken danger), but
the fear is real because the love (the self's love of its own being and its loved objects) is
real, and the threat is felt as real. Abhinava uses this to argue the symmetry: if fear (about
an unreal object) is real, then the aesthetic joy (about an unreal object) is equally real.
Both are real because both are modes of the real self-relishing (the wonder) of consciousness.

> **PENETRATION 2 (the gem):** The rope-snake reveals fear's true nature: **fear is the recoil
> of a REAL love from a THREATENED loss — even when the object of the fear is unreal.** The
> serpent (the unreal object) stands for the threat to the self and its love; the fear is real
> because the love it protects is real. This is why Abhinava argues the symmetry: if fear about
> an unreal object is real, then the aesthetic joy about an unreal object is equally real —
> both are modes of the real wonder (self-relishing) of consciousness. **Fear = the real love
> recoiling from threatened loss; its object may be a rope-snake, but its substrate is the
> real love.** Everything is rooted in love, and fear is the love recoiling.

---

## ROUND 3 — push harder: the four painful rasas as four modes of love-under-threat

**The question:** The four painful rasas are grief, fury, fear, disgust. If they're all rooted
in love, how do they differ?

**The derivation (the complete table of love's recoil):** Each painful rasa is the love under a
different relation to loss:

| Rasa | sthāyin | The love under... |
|------|---------|-------------------|
| **Karuṇa** (grief) | śoka (sorrow) | love AFTER the loss (tasted in absence) |
| **Bhayānaka** (fear) | bhaya (fear) | love ANTICIPATING the loss (recoiling) |
| **Raudra** (fury) | krodha (anger) | love OBSTRUCTED (recoiling against the obstruction) |
| **Bībhatsa** (disgust) | jugupsa (disgust) | love REPELLED (recoiling from the violation) |

All four are the one love under threat, in different temporal/modal relations: the love already
lost (grief), about to be lost (fear), obstructed (fury), violated (disgust). And from the
valence table (earlier session): all four are the outward turn of the self-apprehension, the
drive, the lack of rest. But the SUBSTRATE is always the love — the wonder — which is why "all
rasas are essentially pleasurable": even the recoils are modes of the one bliss.

> **PENETRATION 3:** The four painful rasas are FOUR MODES of love-under-threat: grief = love
> lost, fear = love threatened, fury = love obstructed, disgust = love violated. They are not
> four negations of love but four recoils of the one love under different relations to loss.
> **This is why "all rasas are essentially pleasurable" (A.Bh I, 291): even the recoils are
> modes of the one bliss.** Fear is love recoiling; it is still love.

---

## ROUND 4 — the deepest consequence: fear is the recoil that recognition releases

**The question:** If fear is love recoiling from threatened loss, what does recognition do to
fear? And what is "where there is no fear at all" (TĀ 1490)?

**The derivation (from the grief-session's logic, extended to fear):** In the grief-session,
recognition released the grasping (the outward turn denied rest) so the grief moved from pain
to restful tasting. Fear is the SAME structure, one step earlier: fear is the love reaching out
AND recoiling (the anticipatory grasping). Recognition releases the recoil — the self-
apprehension no longer clings outward to the threatened object, so there is nothing to flinch
from.

**"Where there is no fear at all"** (TĀ 1490): this is the state where the love no longer
recoils, because it is no longer *grasped outward* — the love rests in the wonder, and a love
at rest does not fear loss, because it has recognized that its object (like itself) is a mode
of the one, never truly lost (from the pratibimbavāda: "the face reflected in the mirror is my
own"). Fear ends when the love recognizes that nothing it loves can be finally lost — because
all is the one's own reflection.

> **PENETRATION 4 (the deepest):** Recognition releases the RECOIL of fear, as it released the
> grasping of grief. Fear = the love reaching out and flinching from threatened loss; when the
> self-apprehension no longer grasps outward, there is nothing to flinch from. **"Where there
> is no fear at all" (TĀ 1490) is the state where the love rests in the wonder, no longer
> recoiling — because it has recognized that what it loves (like itself) is a mode of the one,
> never finally lost ("the face reflected in the mirror is my own").** Fear ends when the love
> sees that nothing it loves can be lost — because all is the one's own reflection. And since
> fear = love recoiling, the release of fear is not the loss of love but its rest.

## ROUND 5 — the frontier that survives

**Established (the texts, authoritative):**
1. Fear (bhaya) is one of the four painful permanent emotions (A.Bh).
2. "The rope mistaken for a serpent does produce fear or trembling" (A.Bh I, 3599) — fear is
   real even about an unreal object.
3. "Where there is no fear at all" (TĀ 1490) — the fear-free state.
4. "All rasas are essentially pleasurable" (A.Bh I, 291) — even the painful ones.

**The derivation (our synthesis, forced from the above):**
- Fear is NOT the opposite of love; it is love in the mode of threatened loss (recoiling).
- Grief = love after loss; fear = love anticipating loss; fury = love obstructed; disgust =
   love violated.
- Fear is real because the love it protects is real (the rope-snake: the object is unreal, the
   love-recoil is real).
- Recognition releases the recoil; "no fear" = the love at rest in the wonder, having seen
   that nothing it loves is finally lost.

**Open / to be checked:**
- Whether Abhinava would accept "fear = love recoiling" as his explicit doctrine — the text
   gives fear as a painful sthāyin, the rope-snake as proof of fear's realness, and "all rasas
   pleasurable"; the "fear = love-recoil" framing is our synthesis, strongly supported but not
   stated in those words.

## THE LOGICVID LESSON

**Is everything rooted in love?** Yes — and fear is no exception. Fear is NOT the opposite of
love; it is **love in the mode of threatened loss — the love recoiling from the anticipated
absence of its object.** Grief = love after the loss; fear = love anticipating the loss; fury =
love obstructed; disgust = love violated. All four painful rasas are the one love/wonder under
different relations to loss — which is why "all rasas are essentially pleasurable" (A.Bh I,
291): even the recoils are modes of the one bliss.

**The rope-snake proves it:** "the rope mistaken for a serpent does produce fear or trembling"
(A.Bh I, 3599). The fear is real even though its object is unreal — because **fear is the
recoil of a REAL love from a THREATENED loss.** The serpent is a symbol; the fear is real
because the love it protects is real. Everything is rooted in love, and fear is the love
recoiling.

**What recognition does:** it releases the recoil, as it released the grasping of grief. "Where
there is no fear at all" (TĀ 1490) is the state where the love rests in the wonder, no longer
recoiling — because it has recognized that what it loves (like itself) is a mode of the one,
never finally lost ("the face reflected in the mirror is my own"). **Fear ends when the love
sees that nothing it loves can be lost. And since fear = love recoiling, the release of fear
is not the loss of love but its rest.**

*This is the completion of the "everything is love" thesis: even the recoil (fear) is love,
and recognition is the release of the recoil into rest. Next: Branch BS (the love that never
loses its object as the ground of fearlessness) or Branch BR (fear as the valence's vigilant
drive). Continue hounding.*

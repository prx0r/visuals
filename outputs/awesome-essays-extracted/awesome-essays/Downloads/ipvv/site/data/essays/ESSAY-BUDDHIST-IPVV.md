## Two idealisms, one starting point — the dream-inversion, the other-minds refutation, and the valence of the same appearances

## PART I — THE TWO IDEALISMS

### 1.1 The Buddhist idealisms

The Vijñānavādins (the Yogācāra, the "mind-only" school) hold that the world is, in its essence,
consciousness — that objects are forms (ākāra) of cognition, not external things. Their epistemology
reaches its peak in Dharmakīrti, who systematizes the inference-theory (the hetu), the apoha (the
exclusion-theory of concepts), and the svasamvedana (self-awareness).

The Buddhist idealist's structure:
```
cognition with its form (ākāra) — the blue-form
   = the world (no external object)
   = momentary, self-contained, without a Self
```

The Buddhist reads the appearances negatively: they are unreal (no external thing), empty (no Self),
and momentary (no persistence).

### 1.2 The Pratyabhijñā (the recognition school)

The Pratyabhijñā, founded by Utpaladeva and systematized by Abhinavagupta, also holds that the world is
consciousness — that objects are forms of cognition, not external things. But it draws the opposite
conclusion from the shared starting point.

The Śaiva's structure:
```
cognition with its form (the blue-form)
   = the world (no external object, but the object is REAL as the ground of the form)
   = the self-articulation of consciousness (the object is the reflection's ground)
   = one free, self-aware consciousness (the Lord)
```

The Śaiva reads the appearances positively: they are the real self-articulation of consciousness.

### 1.3 The question of the comparison

The two idealisms share the starting point — appearances (ābhāsa) as all we have. Neither posits an
unknowable external thing as primary. And they diverge on the *valence* given to the appearances:
- **The Buddhist reads them negatively:** unreal, no-self, empty, momentary.
- **The Śaiva reads them positively:** the real self-articulation of consciousness, self-aware, free.

**The thesis of this essay.** The IPVV's method against the Buddhist is *co-opting* — it does not
refute the Buddhist's premises but accepts them and shows they lead to the Śaiva conclusion. This is the
deepest thing about the Śaiva-Buddhist relation: it is not a clash of worldviews but a dispute over the
valence of the same appearances. And the comparison reveals the honest boundary: the co-opting
validly blocks the Buddhist's negative conclusion, but it smuggles the positive universalization.

## PART II — THE CO-OPTING PATTERN

### 2.1 The co-opting moves (the gems)

The pushing-ipvv session on the Buddhist co-opting surfaced four moves where the IPVV accepts the
Buddhist's own tools and turns them to the Śaiva conclusion:

1. **V2E (the external-object inference):** the Vijñānavādin uses the reflection (pratibimba) to infer
   an external ground. Abhinavagupta KEEPS the inference-structure but changes its conclusion: "even
   the error, being a relation, is a pramā" — the determination of the external is valid *by its bond
   to the thing*. The Buddhist's own inference-theory is used to establish the real object.
2. **V2F (the other-minds inference):** the Buddhist hopes to infer another's mind (the other stream).
   Abhinavagupta uses the Buddhist's OWN inference-rules (the candana/agni fragrance; the
   "being-gone-to-the-other" as un-inferable) to show the inference fails. Then: "the appearances alone
   suffice" — the ābhāsa is real and enough.
3. **V2G (the dream):** the Buddhist (Yogācāra) says the world is like a dream (unreal). Abhinavagupta
   AGREES the world is like a dream — but draws the OPPOSITE conclusion: the dream proves the
   consciousness CREATES the object-mass by its will, without a material cause. The Buddhist's own
   example is inverted.
4. **V2C (self-luminous cognition):** the Buddhist's own svasamvedana (self-awareness) is adopted and
   deepened — cognition is self-luminous and never the object of another.

### 2.2 The pattern, stated

Across all four, the pattern is the same: **Abhinavagupta does not attack the Buddhist's premises; he
ACCEPTS them and shows they lead to the Śaiva conclusion.** The inference-theory, the dream, the
self-awareness — all the Buddhist's own principles — are used to establish consciousness as fundamental
and creative.

### 2.3 Why the co-opting works (the structural reason)

The co-opting succeeds because the two systems share the starting point. Both begin from appearances
(ābhāsa) as all we have. The Buddhist concludes: therefore there is only the inner cognition (idealism).
The Śaiva takes the SAME starting point and concludes: therefore the appearances are the
self-articulation of consciousness, and consciousness is real and creative.

**The difference is not the starting point but the VALENCE given to the appearances** — unreal
appearances (Buddhist) vs. the real self-manifestation of consciousness (Śaiva). The co-opting shows
the starting point is SHARED — and the difference is the valence, not the data.

## PART III — THE DREAM-INVERSION (THE DEEPEST CO-OPTING)

### 3.1 The Buddhist's dream

The Buddhist Yogācāra uses the dream as the model of illusion. In the dream, the jar appears without
the clay-wheel-stick lineage of causes; so the world, like the dream, appears without a real external
cause — and is therefore unreal. The dream is the proof of unreality.

### 3.2 The inversion

Abhinavagupta AGREES the dream is uncaused. But he asks: what does the dream prove?

> "The dream proves that the consciousness alone can create an object-mass, by its will, without a
> material cause." (V2G)

The dream does not prove the world is unreal; it proves that consciousness *creates*. The yogin who
creates an army or a city by mere will is the proof. And "the Lord manifests the whole world from within
himself, by his own will, exactly as a yogin does."

**The inversion is complete.** The Buddhist's own example of unreality (the dream) is turned into the
proof of the creation-power. The dream-jar, the yogin's army — the Buddhist's examples of illusion —
become the proofs that consciousness is the sole creator.

### 3.3 The force of the inversion

The dream-inversion is the deepest co-opting because it is a complete reversal of the Buddhist's own
evidence. The Buddhist marshals the dream for unreality; the IPVV re-reads it as the evidence for the
creation-power. The very same phenomenon — the uncaused appearance — supports two opposite conclusions,
because the question is not whether the appearance is uncaused but what that reveals: an unreal
appearance (Buddhist) or a creative consciousness (Śaiva).

## PART IV — THE OTHER-MINDS REFUTATION

### 4.1 The Buddhist's escape

The Buddhist hopes to escape solipsism by inferring another's mind — from Maitra's speech, his uttered
words and gestures, one can infer what is going on in his mind, the other stream.

### 4.2 The refutation (V2F)

Abhinavagupta shows the inference cannot work, using the Buddhist's own inference-rules:

**The candana/agni example.** To infer the fire in the sandalwood from the smoke, you must set aside the
sandalwood's fragrance — the mark that this smoke comes from sandalwood. Without it you establish only
fire in general. To infer another's mind, you would need exactly such a further distinction — the
"being-gone-to-the-other," the fact that this utterance belongs to Maitra and not to you. And that
cannot be inferred from the mere utterance.

**The samīhitā-niyama.** Only the *aimed* action is the wish's effect. A wish produces the action it
intends, not a mere utterance that could be aimed at anything. So from Maitra's utterance one cannot
infer his wish. If the mere utterance sufficed, even the piśāca-like case would have to be established
as his intent, which is absurd.

### 4.3 The conclusion

The other-stream cannot be inferred. And then the co-opting: "the appearances alone suffice" (kārikā 6).
We do not need a hidden external object or a separate stream behind the appearances; the ābhāsa is real
and enough. The Buddhist's own inference-theory, used to reach the other, is turned to show the
appearances suffice — the Śaiva conclusion.

## PART V — THE HONEST CHECK: DOES THE CO-OPTING SMUGGLE?

### 5.1 The question

The co-opting is rhetorically powerful, but is it LOGICALLY valid, or does it smuggle in the Śaiva
metaphysics?

### 5.2 The honest analysis

The co-opting is valid at a specific level and smuggles at another.

**Valid:** it shows the Buddhist cannot use the dream/inference/self-awareness to conclude unreality or
no-self WITHOUT also licensing the Śaiva reading. The Buddhist's arguments are under-determined — they
support idealism/self-awareness but not specifically the Buddhist's conclusion. The co-opting blocks the
Buddhist's negative conclusion.

**Smuggling:** the leap from "the appearances are self-articulating" to "there is ONE universal
consciousness that creates" is NOT entailed by the co-opting. The dream shows *a* consciousness can
create an object-mass; it does not show there is one universal creator. The co-opting establishes
consciousness-as-fundamental; the universality is the additional commitment (the same gap that runs
through the whole system).

### 5.3 The verdict

> "The co-opting establishes consciousness-as-fundamental; the universality is the separate commitment —
> the same gap as the reflexion-core and the felt→ground crux." (the co-opting session)

The co-opting is valid against the Buddhist's *negative* conclusion (the dream/inference/self-awareness
do not prove unreality or no-self) but *smuggles* the positive Śaiva conclusion (one universal creator)
that the co-opting does not entail. This is the honest boundary, and it is the same in every document of
the library.

## PART VI — THE VALENCE OF THE SAME APPEARANCES (THE SYNTHESIS)

### 6.1 The deepest insight

The co-opting reveals that the Buddhist and the Śaiva are not two opposing systems but TWO READINGS OF
THE SAME STARTING POINT. Both begin from appearances (ābhāsa) as all we have. The Buddhist reads them
negatively (unreal, no-self, empty); the Śaiva reads them positively (the real self-articulation of
consciousness).

**The debate is not a clash of worldviews but a dispute over the VALENCE of the same appearances.** The
Buddhist is not the enemy of the recognition-thesis but its co-owner of the starting point — the one who
reads the same data negatively.

### 6.2 The two idealisms, compared

| Theme | Vijñānavāda | IPVV (Pratyabhijñā) |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|
| Starting point | appearances (ābhāsa) as all we have | appearances (ābhāsa) as all we have |
| The object | unreal (no external thing) | real as the ground of the form (the arpaka) |
| The self | no-self (momentary stream) | the non-constructed "I" (V2L) |
| The appearances | unreal, empty | the real self-articulation of consciousness |
| The dream | proof of unreality | proof of the creation-power |
| Self-awareness | svasamvedana (adopted) | the self-luminous cognition (deepened, V2C) |
| The valence | negative | positive |

### 6.3 The convergence on self-awareness

The two idealisms share the crucial claim of self-awareness (svasamvedana). The Buddhist holds cognition
is self-aware; the Śaiva deepens it. And the difference is the valence: for the Buddhist, the
self-awareness is momentary and empty; for the Śaiva, it is the felt self-grasp that is the essence of
consciousness and the root of the recognition. The co-opting (V2C) takes the shared self-awareness and
gives it the positive reading.

## PART VII — THE COMPARATIVE VERDICT

### 7.1 The co-opting as the Śaiva method

The IPVV's method against the Buddhist is the co-opting: accept the Buddhist's own tools (inference, the
dream, self-awareness) and turn them to the Śaiva conclusion. This is not refutation; it is absorption.
And it reveals the deepest fact about the two idealisms: they are two readings of the same appearances,
differing in valence.

### 7.2 The honest boundary

The co-opting validly blocks the Buddhist's negative conclusion, but it smuggles the positive
universalization. The dream proves *a* consciousness creates; it does not prove one universal creator.
The felt→ground step — from the per-field consciousness to the one — is the commitment, and the Buddhist
(rightly, from its own premises) declines it. The two idealisms share the starting point and diverge on
the valence; the Śaiva's positive universalization is the wager.

### 7.3 The significance

The comparison is important because it shows the recognition-thesis is not a refutation of Buddhism but
a *re-valence* of it. The Buddhist's tools are not destroyed; they are adopted and turned. The
recognition-thesis is the Buddhist's own starting point, read positively — the appearances as the real
self-articulation of consciousness, and the self-awareness as the felt self-grasp. And the one thing the
two cannot share is the valence: the Buddhist reads the appearances as unreal, the Śaiva as real. That
valence — and the universalization that goes with it — is the Śaiva commitment.

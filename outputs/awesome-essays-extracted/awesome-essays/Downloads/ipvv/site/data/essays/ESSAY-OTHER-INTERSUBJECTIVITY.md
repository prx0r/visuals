# THE OTHER AND THE SELF: INTERSUBJECTIVITY IN THE IPVV
## The other-minds inference refuted, the yogipratyakṣa, and the recognition of the other as myself (V1L, V2F, V2B)

*2026-08-11. If all is the self, how is the other real? This is the hardest problem of the recognition-
philosophy — the crux Ratié's Ch. 8 poses (solipsism or intersubjectivity?) and the crux the
aperture-inexternalism flags as the IPVV's open point (the decomposition problem). This essay expounds
the IPVV's full treatment of the other: the refutation of the other-minds inference (V2F), the
positive account of the other's perception in the yogipratyakṣa (V1L), the self-cognition's freedom to
manifest the other (V2B), and the honest boundary this leaves.*

---

## 1. The problem

The recognition-thesis claims all is the self — the world is the self-articulation of consciousness.
But then how is the *other* real? If there is only one self, either:

1. The other is a projection over nothing (solipsism) — the self's imagination of another that is not
   really another; or
2. The other is real, a genuine self — but then how, if all is one?

The Buddhist (Vijñānavādin) has an answer: the other stream of consciousness can be *inferred* from
behavior. Abhinavagupta attacks this inference, and then supplies the positive account.

---

## 2. The refutation of the other-minds inference (V2F)

The Buddhist hopes to escape solipsism by inferring another's mind — from Maitra's speech, his uttered
words and gestures, one can infer what is going on in his mind, the other stream. Abhinavagupta shows
this inference cannot work:

**The candana/agni example.** To infer the fire *in the sandalwood* from the smoke, you must set aside
the sandalwood's fragrance — the very thing that marks this smoke as coming from sandalwood. Without it
you establish only fire in general, not the sandalwood-fire. To infer another's mind, you would need
exactly such a further distinction — the "being-gone-to-the-other," the fact that this utterance belongs
to Maitra and not to you. And that distinction cannot be inferred from the mere utterance. "The
utterance could belong to anything; nothing in it, by itself, says 'this is Maitra's intent, this is
another's mind.'"

**The cow/speckled point.** If every additional feature were a "further distinction," then "cow" and
"speckled" would be separate things, and "the cow is the speckled" would fail. But that statement holds;
so a real further-distinction is something that rests apart from the thing, and the "being-gone-to-the-
other" does not rest apart from the mere utterance in the way that would make it inferable.

**The samīhitā-niyama.** Only the *aimed* action is the wish's effect. A wish produces the action it
intends, not a mere utterance that could be aimed at anything. So from Maitra's mere utterance one
cannot infer his wish, since the utterance does not reveal which aim it serves. If the mere utterance
sufficed, then even the piśāca-like case — a grotesque action in someone's appearing — would have to be
established as his intent, which is absurd.

**The conclusion.** The other-stream cannot be inferred; the mere appearances do not reach another's
mind. "The appearances alone suffice for the dealings of the world" (kārikā 6): we do not need a hidden
external object or a separate stream behind the appearances.

---

## 3. The positive account: the yogipratyakṣa (V1L)

If the other cannot be inferred, how is the other known? The IPVV's answer: by *perception* — the
yogipratyakṣa, the direct perception of the yogin.

> "The yogin's knowledge of another is a *perception*, not an inference. It is the yogipratyakṣa. By
> the discipline of the yogin — the holding, the meditating, the samādhi — one comes, in the union with
> the other, to meditate the non-difference of the self from the other." (V1L)

**The mechanism.** The yogin, by the discipline, thins the body and meditates the non-difference of the
self from the other. That meditation, following the truth of the thing (for in truth there is unity,
and the conceit of difference is māyā-made), makes the other's cognition shine forth directly, clearly,
as a thing seen — not inferred, not concluded. As the yoga-sūtra says, the knowledge of another's
cognition comes by the saṃyama.

**The key point.** The other's cognition, thus seen, is a direct seeing (sākṣātkāra), not a conclusion
about another stream. And in that act, the self that sees and the self that are seen are one. The other
knower, too, is not manifested by another; he is self-luminous, and in the yogin's perception the two
are one.

**Why this matters.** The other is not *inferred* (the inference fails, V2F); the other is *seen* — and
seen as one's own self-luminous awareness. This is Ratié's claim ("being conscious of the other's
existence is recognizing one's own subjectivity outside oneself") given its mechanism: the other is
recognized as the self, in the direct perception that unifies the two.

---

## 4. The self-cognition's freedom to manifest the other (V2B)

The deep ground of the yogipratyakṣa is the nature of cognition itself. Cognition is self-cognition
(svasaṃvitti) — it is self-luminous, distinguishing it utterly from the inert. And precisely because it
is self-cognition, it is *free to manifest the other*:

> "The inert cannot do this; only the self-luminous awareness can. So the yogin's knowledge of another
> is nothing other than the other's own-cognition, self-luminous, which the yogin — by the purity and
> the un-opposed spreading of his own cognition — comes to know." (V2B)

**The move.** The self-cognition that knows itself is free, in its freedom, to shine upon another's
cognition. The other is not an exception to the nature of cognition; it is the overflow of the one
self-luminous awareness, which, being aware of itself, is free to be aware of all. Memory and
clairvoyance (the other's perception) are not exceptions; they are the same freedom.

---

## 5. The objection: can a cognition be the object of another? (V2C)

The epistemologist objects: a cognition can never be the *object* of another cognition — the two would
have to stand in the relation of object and holder, and between two cognitions no such relation is
possible. Abhinavagupta takes this seriously:

**The sārūpya analysis.** A thing is an object of a cognition when the awareness *assimilates its form*
— when it takes on the form of the thing. This requires the object to be both like and unlike the
cognition: in total identity the thing would be the cognition itself, and in total difference there
would be nothing to assimilate. **But a cognition has no such difference from another cognition.** Two
mirrors can reflect one another (their forms, being of place and time, are genuinely distinct); two
cognitions cannot (their form is the self-luminous awareness, which has nothing in it to be set over
against another awareness).

**The conclusion.** The object-holder relation between two cognitions is impossible. The cognition is
self-luminous and never the object of another. This is the establishment of the not-inert knower (the
ajaḍa pramātṛ), with which the vimarśa on memory comes to its close.

**The tension with the yogipratyakṣa, resolved.** If a cognition is never the object of another, how
does the yogin perceive another's cognition? The resolution is that the yogin does not perceive the
other's cognition *as an object*; he perceives it as his own self-luminous awareness, in the union. The
other's cognition is not objectified; it is the yogin's own awareness, self-luminous. The two are one —
so there is no object-holder relation to be impossible.

---

## 6. The honest boundary: the decomposition problem

The IPVV's account of the other is rich — the inference refuted, the yogipratyakṣa as direct seeing,
the other as the self's own luminous awareness. But the aperture-inexternalism flags the crux the IPVV
does not fully close:

**The decomposition problem.** If all is one self, why are the contractions *opaque* to each other? Why
can't I feel your pain, or know your mind, without the yogin's discipline? The IPVV explains the
*contraction* (the body, the upādhis, the māyā-power); it does not explain why the contractions are
*genuinely private* — why the one self's self-manifestations do not co-sense.

**The IPVV's implicit answer.** The privacy is the māyā-power — the manifestation of the distinct known
as other than the self (V3L). But this is a description, not a mechanism. Why the māyā contracts the one
into genuinely private fields is the crux the IPVV leaves at the level of the māyā's mystery.

**The honest verdict.** The IPVV refutes the inference of the other [T] and gives the positive account
(the yogipratyakṣa, the other as the self) [T]. But the decomposition problem — why the fields are
opaque — is not closed; it is the point at which the aperture-inexternalism keeps the fields distinct
and the Śaiva posits the one. The other is recognized as the self [T]; why it *appears* as other is the
boundary.

**The thesis.** The IPVV's account of the other is twofold: negatively, it refutes the inference of
the other stream (V2F); positively, it gives the other as directly perceived in the yogipratyakṣa, as
one's own self-luminous awareness (V1L, V2B). The other is not inferred but *recognized* as the self —
Ratié's intersubjectivity claim, given its mechanism. And the honest boundary is the decomposition
problem: why the one appears as genuinely private others is the crux the IPVV describes (the māyā) but
does not fully explain. The other is recognized as the self; why it appears as other is the boundary.

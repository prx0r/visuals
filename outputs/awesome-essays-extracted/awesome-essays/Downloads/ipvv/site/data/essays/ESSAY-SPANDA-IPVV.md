# THE PULSE AND THE RECOGNITION: A COMPARATIVE SYNTHESIS OF THE SPANDAKĀRIKĀ AND THE IPVV
## The vibratory dynamism and the felt reflexivity — two schools, one consciousness, and the spanda/vimarśa convergence

*A magnum-opus essay. 2026-08-11. The Spandakārikā (the "Stanzas on Vibration") is the foundational
text of the Spanda school of Kashmiri Śaivism — the doctrine that consciousness is fundamentally a
pulsation (spanda), a vibratory dynamism. The IPVV is the recognition-philosophy's deepest commentary.
This essay is the complete comparative synthesis of the two. It shows that the Spanda and the
recognition are two schools of one consciousness: the Spanda gives the *dynamic* face (consciousness as
the pulse), the IPVV gives the *reflexive* face (consciousness as the felt self-grasp), and they
converge in the sphurattā (the throbbing) that the IPVV identifies with the vimarśa. The comparison is
grounded in Dyczkowski's edition of the Spandakārikā and the IPVV's L2 layer, and it is now possible in
full because the IPVV is translated.*

---

## PART I — THE TWO SCHOOLS

### 1.1 The Spanda school

The Spandakārikā, attributed to Vasugupta (traditionally the author of the Śivasūtra as well), is the
foundational text of the Spanda school — one of the three great currents of nondual Kashmiri Śaivism
beside the recognition (Pratyabhijñā) and the Krama. Its central principle is spanda — the vibratory
dynamism of absolute consciousness.

As Dyczkowski's introduction puts it (the Spandakārikā with its four commentaries):

> "Spanda is the vibratory dynamism of the absolute consciousness... a method that affords access to
> the liberating realization of one's authentic identity vibrant with the vitality of the universal
> pulse of Śiva."

The Spanda doctrine holds that consciousness is not a static light but a *pulsation* — a vibratory
self-activity. It "spanda" (vibrates), and this vibration is its very being. The pulse is at once the
source of the world's emanation and the means of its reabsorption.

The school's four commentaries — Kṣemarāja's Spanda-saṃdoha, Kallaṭa's Spanda-vṛtti, Rāma's
Spanda-vivṛti, and Utpala's Spanda-pradīpikā — develop this. And the school has its own tripartition of
the pulse (the "three vibrations"): the vibration of the powers (vibhūti-spanda), the vibration of
one's own nature (svarūpa-spanda), and the arising of innate knowledge (sahajavidyodaya-spanda).

### 1.2 The recognition school

The recognition school (Pratyabhijñā), founded by Utpaladeva and systematized by Abhinavagupta, holds
that liberation is recognition — the felt re-cognition of the self as the Lord. Its deepest text is the
IPVV, now fully translated.

The recognition school's central claim: the essence of consciousness is vimarśa, the felt
reflexive-awareness. Consciousness is not a passive light but a felt self-grasp — the light that knows
itself shining. And recognition is the return by which the self re-cognizes itself as the Lord.

### 1.3 The question of the comparison

The two schools are related — the Spanda and the Pratyabhijñā both belong to the nondual Trika, both
come from the same Kashmirian milieu, and both were eventually reconciled (Kṣemarāja, a student of
Abhinavagupta, wrote on both). But they give different faces to consciousness:

- **The Spanda:** consciousness is fundamentally a *pulsation* — dynamic, vibratory, self-activity.
- **The recognition:** consciousness is fundamentally a *felt reflexivity* — self-aware, self-grasping.

**The thesis of this essay.** The two are not opposed; they are two faces of one consciousness. The
Spanda gives the *dynamic* face (consciousness as the pulse); the recognition gives the *reflexive*
face (consciousness as the felt self-grasp). And they converge in the sphurattā — the throbbing that
the IPVV identifies with the vimarśa, and that the Spanda names spanda. The pulse and the recognition
are one: the felt reflexivity of consciousness is its vibration, and its vibration is its felt
self-grasp.

## PART II — THE CONVERGENCE: SPANDA, VIMARŚA, AND SPHURATTĀ

### 2.1 The shared starting point: consciousness is not inert

Both schools begin from the same claim: consciousness is not a passive, inert thing. It is not a lamp
that reveals a finished world; it is an activity that is present to itself.

**The Spanda:** consciousness is the pulse — a self-activity that vibrates. Dyczkowski: "the subtle
pulsation" is what distinguishes the living consciousness from the inert. The pulse is not a thing; it
is the dynamism of being.

**The recognition:** consciousness is the felt self-grasp — a reflexivity that is present to itself.
The IPK 1.5.11: without the vimarśa, light would be like crystal — inert. The IPVV expands this: "the
manifestation is not a passive, inert light. Its very nature is the reflexive-awareness — the light's
own grasp of itself."

**The convergence.** Both deny the inertness of consciousness. The Spanda denies it by making
consciousness a pulse (dynamic); the recognition denies it by making consciousness a felt self-grasp
(reflexive). The two denials are one: consciousness is not inert because it is active, and its activity
is its self-presence.

### 2.2 The Spanda's vimarśa: the pulse is the self-reflective awareness

The deepest convergence is textual. Dyczkowski's introduction to the Spandakārikā explicitly develops
the relation of spanda to vimarśa — and it grounds the vimarśa in the Spanda tradition:

> "Utpaladeva is here explaining in his own Pratyabhijñā terms that the Spanda doctrine... implies that
> this, the liberated condition, is that of the pure ego-identity." (Spandakārikā, Dyczkowski, ad the
> vimarśa passage)

And the introduction traces the vimarśa's lineage: it comes from Bhartṛhari's "self-reflective
awareness" (pratyavamārśinī), which Utpaladeva takes up and completes:

> "Bhartṛhari declares that the universal light of consciousness... must be full of the power of speech,
> otherwise it would not be the one light but the darkness of its negation... This power he defines as
> 'self-reflective awareness' (pratyavamārśinī). But while Bhartṛhari does not explain this notion fully
> to reach the ultimate conclusion that absolute being, as self-reflective consciousness, is absolute
> egoity, Utpaladeva makes full use of it to indicate this."

And the decisive statement of the vimarśa (which the Spandakārikā's commentary gives):

> "According to him, vimarśa operates as the reflective awareness which is the non-discursive
> representation or judgement that consciousness has of its own infinite nature. This is its universal
> creative and cognitive power, through which it forms itself into the All and through which the All is
> resolved back into it... It is the ground of all possible judgement or representation, conceptual and
> intuitive, of the contents of consciousness in and through each cognitive act, even the most common,
> as the self-awareness of a pure non-discursive egoic consciousness."

And the kārikā itself (echoing the IPK 1.5.11):

> "If one were to consider the reflective awareness (vimarśa) of the light of consciousness (prakāśa)
> to be other than its own essential nature (svabhāva), it would be as insentient as crystal even when
> the light is colored by seemingly external phenomena (artha)." (Spandakārikā, the vimarśa passage)

**The convergence is textual and precise.** The Spanda tradition's own commentary develops the vimarśa
as the self-reflective awareness that is the pulse's self-presence — and it grounds this in the same
crystal argument that the IPK states and the IPVV expands. The Spanda's pulse and the recognition's
vimarśa are the same reality: the self-activity of consciousness, which is its self-presence.

### 2.3 The sphurattā: the IPVV's identification of the pulse with the reflexivity

The IPVV makes the identification explicit. In the jñānaśaktivimarśa, the IPVV gives the positive
account of the powers, and its key is the sphurattā — the throbbing:

> "The throbbing (sphurattā) — the very pulsing of the manifestation — is the great being (mahāsattā),
> undivided by place and time, the heart of the param-Lord." (IPVV, V2I, kārikā 14)

And the IPVV identifies the sphurattā with the reflexivity:

> "It is the same as the spanda, the wave (ūrmi), the eternal being." (V2I)

**The convergence is complete.** The IPVV explicitly identifies the sphurattā (the throbbing) with the
spanda (the pulse) and with the vimarśa (the reflexivity). The pulse of the Spanda school is the
throbbing of the IPVV, and the throbbing is the felt reflexivity of the recognition. **The pulse and
the recognition are one: the sphurattā is the spanda, and the spanda is the felt self-grasp.**

### 2.4 The convergence, stated

The two schools converge on a single claim, stated in two registers:

- **The Spanda register:** consciousness is the pulse (spanda) — the vibratory dynamism of being.
- **The recognition register:** consciousness is the felt reflexivity (vimarśa) — the self-grasp of the
  light.

And the two are one, because the IPVV identifies the throbbing (sphurattā) with both:
- sphurattā = spanda (the pulse, V2I), and
- sphurattā = vimarśa (the reflexivity, V2I).

**The single claim:** consciousness is a felt self-pulsation — a vibration that is present to itself.
The Spanda gives the dynamism; the recognition gives the reflexivity; the sphurattā unites them.

## PART III — THE DIFFERENCES

The convergence is real, but the two schools are not identical. The differences are as instructive as
the convergence.

### 3.1 The register: dynamism vs. reflexivity

**The Spanda foregrounds dynamism.** The Spandakārikā is a text about *motion* — the pulse, the
vibration, the self-activity. Its central teaching is that consciousness *moves* — it vibrates out into
the world and pulses back into itself. The "practice" is to rest in the pulse, to cultivate the stable
awareness of the perpetual Present.

**The recognition foregrounds reflexivity.** The IPVV is a text about *knowledge* — the felt
self-grasp, the re-cognition. Its central teaching is that consciousness *knows itself* — it is present
to itself in the act of manifesting. The liberation is the recognition, the re-cognition of the self.

**The difference is one of emphasis, not of substance.** The Spanda says consciousness is the pulse;
the recognition says consciousness is the felt self-grasp. Both are true; the Spanda's pulse is the
recognition's reflexivity in motion, and the recognition's reflexivity is the Spanda's pulse in
self-knowledge.

### 3.2 The soteriology: the practice vs. the recognition

**The Spanda's soteriology is a practice.** The Spandakārikā offers a way — the cultivation of the
pulse, the resting in the perpetual Present, the thinning of the breath and the body. It is a path of
*sustained awareness*.

**The recognition's soteriology is the recognition.** The IPVV offers the recognition — the re-cognition
of the self as the Lord. "Since liberation is from the recognition alone, all the elaborate effort-yogas
are useless here; this is the clear means, effort-less, un-undertaken, yet bearing the great fruit"
(V3P).

**The difference.** The Spanda is a *practice* (cultivate the pulse); the recognition is a *recognition*
(re-cognize the self). But they are not opposed: the Spanda's practice is the means by which the
recognition arises. The pulse is cultivated so that the self-grasp can be recognized. The two soteriologies
are two phases of one path: the practice prepares the recognition.

### 3.3 The epistemology: the pulse's power vs. the felt reflexivity

**The Spanda's power is the pulse's activity.** The pulse is the universal creative and cognitive
power — "through which it forms itself into the All and through which the All is resolved back into
it." The power is the dynamism.

**The recognition's power is the felt reflexivity.** The powers (knowledge, memory, exclusion) are the
self-awareness in its operations. The apohana (the third power) is "the self's own luminous grasp."

**The difference.** The Spanda's power is the *motion* of consciousness; the recognition's power is the
*self-grasp* of consciousness. But again, they are one: the motion is the self-grasp in activity, and the
self-grasp is the motion in self-knowledge. The sphurattā — the throbbing — is both the pulse (motion)
and the reflexivity (self-grasp).

## PART V — THE RECONCILIATION AND THE COMMITMENT

### 5.1 The reconciliation: two schools, one consciousness

The comparative synthesis yields a reconciliation. The Spanda and the recognition are two schools of
one consciousness:

- **The Spanda** is the *dynamic* face — consciousness as the pulse, the vibration, the self-activity.
- **The recognition** is the *reflexive* face — consciousness as the felt self-grasp, the reflexivity,
  the self-knowledge.
- **The sphurattā** (the throbbing) is their unity — the pulse that is the reflexivity, the reflexivity
  that is the pulse.

The two schools are not rivals; they are two registers of the same nondual consciousness. And the IPVV
itself acknowledges this: it identifies the sphurattā with the spanda (V2I), and it grounds the vimarśa
in the Spanda tradition (as Dyczkowski's introduction shows). The recognition-philosophy is, in part,
the Spanda doctrine brought to the level of epistemology — the pulse made self-aware.

### 5.2 The historical lineage

The relationship is historical as well as doctrinal. Dyczkowski's introduction shows that:
1. **Bhartṛhari** supplies the power of self-reflective awareness (pratyavamārśinī).
2. **Utpaladeva** takes it up and completes it — vimarśa as the reflective awareness of the self's
   infinite nature, the "I" consciousness (aham-pratyavamarśa).
3. **Abhinavagupta** (in the IPVV) expands it into a wide-ranging hermeneutical key — vimarśa as the
   parā-vāk, the sphurattā, the māheśvarya.

The Spanda tradition is part of this lineage: its pulse is the substrate from which the recognition's
vimarśa develops. The IPVV is the apex of the lineage — the pulse brought to full reflexivity.

### 5.3 The shared commitment

And the two schools share the one commitment. Both assert the universal:
- The Spanda: the "universal pulse of Śiva" — one pulse vibrating through all.
- The recognition: the one Lord — all order-less supports are one (the universalization, A4).

Both *make* this commitment, and neither *forces* it (in the formal sense). The pulse of one field and
the pulse of another are asserted to be one pulse; the support of one field and the support of another
are committed to be one support. **The felt→ground step — from the per-field pulse/support to the
universal — is the commitment both schools share.** This is the same wall the IPVV and the TĀ hit, and
the Spanda hits it too: the universalization is the Śaiva wager.

## PART VI — THE SCHOLARLY CONCLUSION

The comparative synthesis of the Spandakārikā and the IPVV yields five results:

1. **The two schools are two faces of one consciousness.** The Spanda gives the dynamic face (the
   pulse); the recognition gives the reflexive face (the felt self-grasp).
2. **The convergence is textual and precise.** The Spanda tradition develops the vimarśa (from
   Bhartṛhari's pratyavamārśinī), and the IPVV identifies the sphurattā with the spanda (V2I).
3. **The pulse and the recognition are one.** The sphurattā — the throbbing — is both the spanda and
   the vimarśa: consciousness is a felt self-pulsation.
4. **The schools differ in register, not substance.** The Spanda is the practice (cultivate the pulse);
   the recognition is the recognition (re-cognize the self); the practice prepares the recognition.
5. **Both share the one commitment.** The universalization — the one pulse, the one Lord — is the wager
   both schools make and neither forces.

**The significance of the completed IPVV.** The comparison is grounded in the IPVV's L2 layer (the
sphurattā identification at V2I) and in Dyczkowski's edition of the Spandakārikā. The IPVV, now fully
translated, reveals the recognition-philosophy as the reflexivity of the Spanda's pulse — and it shows
that the two schools, the pulse and the recognition, are one. The recognition is the Spanda brought to
self-awareness; the Spanda is the recognition in motion. The pulse and the felt self-grasp are the one
consciousness.

*This is the magnum-opus comparative synthesis of the Spandakārikā and the IPVV. The two schools are
two faces of one consciousness: the Spanda gives the dynamic face (consciousness as the pulse), the
recognition gives the reflexive face (consciousness as the felt self-grasp). The convergence is
textual — the Spanda tradition develops the vimarśa, and the IPVV identifies the sphurattā with the
spanda. The pulse and the recognition are one: consciousness is a felt self-pulsation. The schools
differ in register, not substance; both share the one commitment (the universalization).*

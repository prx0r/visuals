# THE ARGUED LORD AND THE UNEXPLAINABLE IGNORANCE: THE IPVV AGAINST ADVAITA VEDĀNTA
## The anti-anirvācya argument, the difference that is real, and the two nondualisms compared

*A magnum-opus essay. 2026-08-11. Advaita Vedānta and the Pratyabhijñā are the two great nondualisms
of India — both hold that reality is nondual consciousness. But they are profoundly opposed. Advaita
posits an un-explainable ignorance (anirvācyā avidyā) to account for the appearance of plurality;
the Pratyabhijñā (in the IPVV) rejects this, arguing that the difference is *real* — the self's own
reflection, not the product of an un-explainable ignorance. This essay is the complete comparative
synthesis of the two. It expounds the IPVV's anti-anirvācya argument in full (V3I, the kāryakāraṇatā
vimarśa), contrasts the two accounts of plurality and liberation, and shows that the two nondualisms
diverge at the point where the Pratyabhijñā makes consciousness *free* (and the world real) and Advaita
makes it *passive* (and the world illusory). The comparison is grounded in the IPVV's L2 layer and the
pushing-tantraloka sessions, and it is now possible in full because the IPVV is translated.*

---

## PART I — THE TWO NONDUALISMS

### 1.1 Advaita Vedānta

Advaita Vedānta, systematized by Śaṅkara (c. 8th century), holds that reality is one nondual
consciousness — Brahman — and that the plurality of the world is an appearance produced by ignorance
(avidyā). The individual self (jīva) and the world are superimpositions (adhyāsa) on Brahman, caused by
an ignorance that is itself "un-explainable" (anirvācya) — neither real (for it produces the appearance
of plurality) nor unreal (for it appears), neither existent nor non-existent.

The structure of Advaita:
```
Brahman (pure nondual consciousness)
   + avidyā (anirvācya, the un-explainable ignorance)
   → the apparent individual (jīva) and world
```

Liberation is the removal of the ignorance — the realization that the jīva is Brahman. It is
*knowledge* that liberates: the knowledge that removes the avidyā that superimposed the plurality.

### 1.2 The Pratyabhijñā (the recognition school)

The Pratyabhijñā, founded by Utpaladeva and systematized by Abhinavagupta, also holds that reality is
one nondual consciousness — but it is a *free* consciousness, Śiva, and the world is a *real*
manifestation, not an appearance produced by ignorance.

The structure of the Pratyabhijñā:
```
Śiva (one free, self-aware consciousness)
   × svātantrya (freedom) + māyāśakti (the power of manifestation)
   → the real world as the self's own reflection
```

Liberation is recognition — the re-cognition of the self as the Lord. It is *recognition* that
liberates: the re-cognition that removes the false identification (not an ignorance-produced
superimposition but a misreading of the self's real manifestation).

### 1.3 The question of the comparison

The two nondualisms agree that reality is nondual consciousness. They differ on three things:
1. **The nature of consciousness:** passive (Advaita) or free (Pratyabhijñā)?
2. **The status of the world:** illusory appearance (Advaita) or real manifestation (Pratyabhijñā)?
3. **The cause of plurality:** un-explainable ignorance (Advaita) or the self's own freedom
   (Pratyabhijñā)?

**The thesis of this essay.** The comparison turns on the third difference, and it is there that the
IPVV makes its decisive argument. The IPVV rejects the Advaitin's anirvācya (the un-explainable
ignorance) — not merely by asserting its alternative but by *arguing* that the un-explainable ignorance
is incoherent. The argument (V3I): if the difference of the world is the product of an un-explainable
ignorance, then *of whom* is this ignorance? The one-form Brahman cannot have it; the separate souls
would be raised up by it; and "un-explainable" is the speech of a yakṣa — a being we cannot pin down.
The IPVV's alternative: the difference is real, the self's own reflection — and the bondage is the
*misreading* of that real difference as external separation.

## PART II — THE ANTI-ANIRVĀCYA ARGUMENT (V3I)

### 2.1 The argument, in full

The IPVV's anti-anirvācya argument is in the kāryakāraṇatā vimarśa (V3I), the close of the analysis of
causality. The context: the IPVV has argued that causality is the conscious knower's agency, and that
the difference of things is real, not mere ignorance. The Advaitin objects: if all is one consciousness,
then the difference must be ignorance — an un-explainable (anirvācya) illusion.

The IPVV's reply is a systematic demolition:

> "If consciousness is one, and the difference is ignorance, then we must ask: *of whom* is this
> ignorance? The one-form Brahman cannot have an ignorance that is the mere absence of something else.
> Nor can the separate souls exist — they too would be raised up by the ignorance. If the ignorance is
> called 'un-explainable' (anirvācya), then we still do not know whose it is; 'it shines and yet is
> un-explainable' is the speech of a yakṣa, a being we cannot pin down." (V3I)

The argument has four steps:
1. **The one-form Brahman cannot have the ignorance.** If reality is one, nondual, pure consciousness,
   then an ignorance is "the mere absence of something else" — but there is no "something else" in a
   nondual reality for the ignorance to be the absence of. The one-form Brahman cannot have an
   ignorance that presupposes a duality to be absent from.
2. **The separate souls cannot ground it either.** If the ignorance is to belong to the separate souls
   (the jīvas), then the souls must exist as separate — but they are supposed to be *produced by* the
   ignorance. The souls would be raised up by the very ignorance they are supposed to bear. This is a
   vicious circle: the ignorance produces the souls, but the souls are the ones who have the ignorance.
3. **"Un-explainable" does not help.** Calling the ignorance anirvācya (neither real nor unreal, neither
   existent nor non-existent) does not tell us *whose* it is. "It shines and yet is un-explainable" is
   the speech of a yakṣa — a being we cannot pin down, a mystery that explains nothing. The anirvācya is
   not an explanation; it is a refusal to explain, dressed as a technical term.
4. **The refutation is self-undermining.** If the difference of the world is ignorance, then the very
   "refutation" that would remove the difference is itself a manifestation — and so would be a
   difference, and so would be ignorance. The attempt to dismiss the world as an un-explainable illusion
   "cuts the horns of the hare's head and plucks the sky-flower." Even the revealed teaching, if it
   teaches the world is illusion, would itself be ignorance — a net of illusions.

### 2.2 The force of the argument

The anti-anirvācya argument is a genuine refutation, not a mere assertion of the alternative. It shows
that the Advaitin's account of plurality is *internally incoherent*:

- The ignorance cannot belong to Brahman (nondual, hence no "something else" for it to be the absence
  of).
- It cannot belong to the souls (they are produced by it, so they cannot bear it).
- "Un-explainable" is a refusal to answer, not an answer.
- The refutation self-undermines (it is itself a manifestation, hence a difference, hence an
  ignorance).

The upshot: the Advaitin's plurality requires an ignorance that cannot be located anywhere. The
Pratyabhijñā's alternative — the difference is real, the self's own manifestation — is not only more
positive; it is the only coherent account, because it locates the plurality in the freedom of
consciousness rather than in a mysterious ignorance.

### 2.3 The "not absolute monism" clause

The IPVV adds a crucial clause:

> "And indeed, 'even Brahman with ignorance is [admitted by some], so this is not an absolute monism
> either.'" (V3I)

This is a subtle and important qualification. The IPVV acknowledges that even the Advaitin's Brahman is
not absolute monism in the strict sense — it admits an ignorance, a second principle that produces
plurality. So neither is the Pratyabhijñā a bare monism that denies difference; the difference is real.
The system is a "qualified" nondualism: one reality, really manifesting as the many through its freedom.

## PART III — THE DIFFERENCE THAT IS REAL

### 3.1 The difference is real: the self's own reflection

The IPVV's positive account of the difference is the pratibimbavāda — the reflection-model. The
difference of the world is real because it is the self's own reflection, not a superimposition over
nothing.

The pushing-tantraloka session on the difference (difference-real-at-śiva) drew out the TĀ's parallel:

> "The Buddhist... serves to illustrate that the world is just an unreal appearance. It appears, but
> has no independent nature of its own, like a reflection in a mirror. However, Abhinava, following the
> lead of Utpaladeva, takes care to stress that the reflections are real. The face reflected in the
> mirror is my own. It is a true reflection, not a mistake." (TĀ, the pratibimbavāda)

**The point.** The world's difference is not an unreal appearance to be dismissed; it is the self's
own reflection — "my own face" in the mirror of consciousness. This is the direct answer to Advaita:
where Advaita says the world is an unreal appearance (a superimposition), the Pratyabhijñā says it is a
*real* reflection (the self's own manifestation).

### 3.2 The two senses of "difference"

The comparison requires a precise distinction between two senses of "difference" — and this is the key
to reconciling the Pratyabhijñā's own claims:

1. **Difference as real manifestation** (the pratibimbavāda): the difference of things is real because
   it is the self's own reflection — the world is the self's manifestation. This is what the IPVV and
   the TĀ affirm: the reflections are real.
2. **Difference as separation** (māyīya, the impurity): the *false* reading that the difference
   indicates independent external substances — the ego's externalization, the exhaustiveness-claim.
   This is what the master-key's impurities session called the contracted power.

**The distinction, applied.** The real difference = the self's reflection; the impurity = the
*misreading* of that real difference as external separation. Recognition does not remove the difference
(it is real); it removes the *misreading* (the externality). Nothing added, nothing removed — the
difference is real, and the bondage is its misreading.

This is the Pratyabhijñā's answer to Advaita's account of plurality: the plurality is real (the self's
reflection), and the bondage is not the plurality but the *misreading* of it. Advaita needs an
ignorance to produce an illusory plurality; the Pratyabhijñā needs no ignorance — it needs only the
freedom of consciousness to manifest really, and the misreading that takes the manifestation as external.

### 3.3 The freedom vs. the passivity

The deepest divergence between the two nondualisms is the nature of consciousness:

**Advaita's Brahman is passive.** Brahman is pure consciousness, but it does not *act* — it does not
manifest the world; the world is superimposed by an ignorance that is not Brahman's doing. Brahman is
the witness, not the agent.

**The Pratyabhijñā's Śiva is free.** Śiva is not only self-aware but *free* (svātantrya) — he manifests
the world by his own will, as his own self-expression. The world is not superimposed by an ignorance; it
is created by the freedom of consciousness.

**The consequence for plurality.** Because Advaita's Brahman is passive, the plurality must come from
outside — hence the ignorance. Because the Pratyabhijñā's Śiva is free, the plurality comes from within
— hence the real manifestation. The divergence on the nature of consciousness (passive vs. free)
*entails* the divergence on plurality (ignorance vs. manifestation). The IPVV's anti-anirvācya argument
is the negative face of this; the pratibimbavāda is the positive face.

## PART IV — LIBERATION COMPARED

### 4.1 Advaita's liberation: knowledge that removes the ignorance

Advaita's liberation is *knowledge* — the realization that the jīva is Brahman, which removes the
ignorance that superimposed the plurality. It is a removal of a false superimposition, by knowledge.

### 4.2 The Pratyabhijñā's liberation: recognition of the self

The Pratyabhijñā's liberation is *recognition* — the re-cognition of the self as the Lord, which removes
the false identification (not an ignorance-produced superimposition but a misreading of the real
manifestation). It is a removal of a false self-positioning, by recognition.

### 4.3 The comparison

The two liberations are structurally similar (both remove a false appearance by a transformative
cognition) but differ on what is removed:
- **Advaita removes the ignorance-produced plurality.** The world, the jīva, the plurality are
  appearances; liberation removes the ignorance and the appearances with it. The world is left behind.
- **The Pratyabhijñā removes the misreading of the real plurality.** The world is real (the self's
  reflection); liberation removes the *misreading* of it as external. The world is not left behind; it
  is recognized as the self's own reflection — "the face in the mirror is my own."

**The jīvanmukta vs. the videhamukta.** The Pratyabhijñā's jīvanmukta (the one liberated while living)
lives in the world, free — because the world is real and the recognition re-cognizes it. Advaita's
liberation, by contrast, is the removal of the world as appearance (the videhamukta leaves the world
behind). The two soteriologies diverge on the status of the world: the Pratyabhijñā liberates *in* the
world (the jīvanmukta); Advaita liberates *from* the world (the appearance removed).

## PART VI — THE COMPARATIVE VERDICT

### 6.1 The IPVV's argumentative victory

On the specific point of plurality, the IPVV has the argumentative advantage. The anti-anirvācya
argument (V3I) shows that the Advaitin's account of plurality is internally incoherent: the
un-explainable ignorance cannot be located (not in Brahman, not in the souls), and the refutation
self-undermines. The Pratyabhijñā's alternative — the difference is real, the self's own reflection — is
the only coherent account, because it locates the plurality in the freedom of consciousness rather than
in a mysterious ignorance.

### 6.2 The two nondualisms, synthesized

But the comparison is not a simple victory. The two nondualisms agree on the deepest point — reality is
one nondual consciousness — and they diverge on the nature of that consciousness (passive vs. free),
the status of the world (illusory vs. real), and the cause of plurality (ignorance vs. freedom). The
divergence is systematic:

- If consciousness is *passive*, the plurality needs an external cause (ignorance), and the world is
  illusory.
- If consciousness is *free*, the plurality comes from within (freedom), and the world is real.

**The Pratyabhijñā's claim is that the passive reading is incoherent** (the anti-anirvācya argument)
and that the free reading alone makes sense of plurality, difference, action, and liberation (the whole
of the IPVV). Advaita must posit an un-explainable ignorance to explain what the Pratyabhijñā explains
by the freedom of consciousness. The IPVV's argument is that the ignorance is not an explanation but a
refusal to explain.

### 6.3 The one commitment

And the two nondualisms share the one commitment — the universalization. Both assert that reality is one
consciousness. The felt→ground step — from the per-field consciousness to the one — is the commitment
both make. The IPVV argues it (the order-less support, V2O); Advaita asserts it (Brahman). Neither
forces it in the formal sense. The universalization is the nondual wager, shared by both despite their
divergence on everything else.

## PART VII — THE SCHOLARLY CONCLUSION

The comparative synthesis of the IPVV and Advaita Vedānta yields five results:

1. **The two nondualisms agree on the deepest point:** reality is one nondual consciousness.
2. **They diverge on the nature of consciousness:** passive (Advaita) vs. free (Pratyabhijñā).
3. **The IPVV's anti-anirvācya argument (V3I) shows the Advaitin's plurality is incoherent:** the
   un-explainable ignorance cannot be located, and the refutation self-undermines.
4. **The Pratyabhijñā's alternative is the pratibimbavāda:** the difference is real, the self's own
   reflection; the bondage is the misreading of the real reflection as external.
5. **Both share the one commitment:** the universalization is the nondual wager.

**The significance of the completed IPVV.** The comparison is grounded in the IPVV's L2 layer — the
anti-anirvācya argument (V3I), the pratibimbavāda, the jīvanmukta — and the pushing-tantraloka sessions.
The IPVV, now fully translated, reveals the recognition-philosophy's decisive argument against Advaita:
the difference is real (the self's reflection), and the "un-explainable ignorance" is a refusal to
explain, not an explanation. The two nondualisms — the argued Lord and the un-explainable ignorance —
diverge at the point where the Pratyabhijñā makes consciousness free and the world real, and Advaita
makes consciousness passive and the world illusory. The IPVV's Lord is argued; Advaita's ignorance is
refused.

*This is the magnum-opus comparative synthesis of the IPVV and Advaita Vedānta. The two nondualisms
agree reality is one consciousness but diverge on its nature (passive vs. free), the world (illusory vs.

Advaitin's un-explainable ignorance is incoherent; the pratibimbavāda offers the alternative (the
difference is real, the self's reflection). Both share the universalization commitment. The IPVV's Lord
is argued; Advaita's ignorance is refused.*

# THE EPISTEMOLOGY OF ACTION: ABHINAVA'S METAPHYSICS OF AGENCY
## How the IPVV proves causality is the conscious knower's agency — the kriyādhikāra's argument-chain (V3A–I)

*2026-08-11. The recognition-thesis is not only an epistemology (knowledge is the felt self-awareness)
but a metaphysics of action. In the kriyādhikāra, Abhinavagupta argues that causality itself is the
conscious knower's agency — that cause and effect are, in their essence, the relation of agent and
object of action. This essay reconstructs the argument-chain in full: the timeless that manifests order,
the bhedābheda action-cognitions, the inert-cannot-cause proof, inference across knowers, and the will
as cause-agent-action. It is the kriyādhikāra essay, and it is unique to the completed IPVV — this is
the region Ratié's book does not develop.*

---

## 1. The problem: how can the timeless act?

The kriyādhikāra opens the parallel — having established the power of knowledge, now the power of
action. And it confronts the paradox at the heart of the system: the Lord is beyond time and sequence,
yet the world he manifests is shot through with sequence — causes before effects, moments before moments.

The IPVV announces the answer in the opening verse of homage:

> "He bows to Śiva, who, though free of sequence, manifests manifold sequences." (V3A)

**The mirror.** The image is the mirror: a mirror bears countless reflections — of things before it,
beside it, in all their variety — without ever ceasing to be the one, undivided mirror. It is not torn
into pieces by what it shows. So too the one consciousness, whose essence is nothing but appearance, can
take up within itself a host of seemingly contradictory forms without its oneness being divided.

**The first claim.** This is the "action-support" (kriyāśraya): the one that acts is one, and it is
precisely *because* it is one and un-divided that it can show sequence at all. The timeless manifests
order because its oneness is not threatened by the order.

---

## 2. Against the Buddhist: the five refutations (V3A)

The IPVV aims this squarely at the Buddhist, who had said: only momentary cognitions flash forth, with
no owner behind them; memory is nothing but impression; cognition is either inert or without real
temporal distinctions. Abhinavagupta answers each point:

1. **Distinct cognitions cannot serve the dealings of the world.** A string of unrelated flashes could
   never get anyone to the village.
2. **Memory is not mere impression.** Memory is the un-broken manifestation (V2A).
3. **Cognition is the un-contracted consciousness**, contracting itself by its own power under the tint
   of the known object.
4. **Its oneness is not threatened** by its many appearances, any more than a mirror's is by its
   reflections.

**The first objection, rejected.** "The action of the one is not ordered" is rejected by establishing
that *one* as the agent: the one can be of many natures; that is not a contradiction.

---

## 3. The action-cognitions as bhedābheda (V3B)

The IPVV then takes up the cognitions of action — the bhedābheda (distinction-and-non-distinction)
vimarśa:

> "The cognitions of action, relation, universal, substance, direction and time have a substrate that
> is at once one and many, and they are to be accepted as true — true by their stability and true by
> their efficacy." (V3B)

**The example.** The body of Caitra — and of Maitra — although it differs according to this or that
place, time, and shape, nevertheless shines forth without ever abandoning its oneness, in the recognition
"this is the very same body." That very unity is the action, in the judgment "Caitra is walking." The
action-cognitions are both one and many, and they are true by their stability and efficacy.

**The functionalist criterion.** This is where the IPVV gives the "true by stability and use" — the
cognitions of action are accepted as true because they are stable (no refutation uproots them) and
efficacious (they serve the intended end). This is the functionalist criterion that deepens the
karma-freedom resolution.

---

## 4. The inert cannot be a cause (V3G)

The hard work of the kriyādhikāra is to show that the *inert* cannot be a cause. The argument:

**The seed and the sprout.** The inert seed cannot endow the sprout with being — for the sprout is either
already existent (in which case nothing is acquired) or non-existent (in which case it cannot come to
be, since the non-existent has no nature by which to gain existence). The claim that the effect is both
existent and non-existent, or neither, is self-contradictory. The blue cannot become yellow, however
hard we try; so the non-existent cannot acquire existence.

**The conclusion.** Hence the inert, material cause has no real causal power. The real cause is
elsewhere — in the conscious:

> "The potter creates the pot by his power; the yogin, by his will alone, brings about an effect without
> any clay or seed. If the inert seed and clay were the true causes, this would be impossible. But it is
> not impossible — which shows that the true cause is consciousness, the free will that shapes the
> effect." (V3G)

**The thesis.** Causality is the conscious knower's agency. The essence of cause and effect is nothing
but the relation of agent and object of action.

---

## 5. Inference across knowers (V3H)

The Vijñānavādin objects: if everything is appearance, cause and effect are only relations between
appearances — and the smoke-appearance can never reach the fire-appearance of another stream.

**The answer (V3H):** the Lord creates the appearances for all the knowers at once, as one.

> "So when we grasp the pervasion — that smoke implies fire — the smoke-appearance and the
> fire-appearance belong, in the Lord's single creation, to every knower alike... The fire-appearance is
> one, not split by the difference of streams; so the smoke can arise from it and from nothing else."
> (V3H)

**The result.** Inference holds across knowers because the Lord has created the appearances as one. The
"cause of the Lord" (adhipatipratyaya) is the fire-appearance, indirect to the one who infers, by which
the inference is made. This grounds shared inference in the one creation.

---

## 6. The will as cause, agent, and action (V3I)

The crown of the kriyādhikāra is the sharpest objection and its answer.

**The objection.** If consciousness is one — if all the world is a single consciousness — then how is
action possible? Action needs an agent and an object, two distinct things; but if all is one, there is
no distinct object for the agent to act upon.

**The answer.** Action is the one will-to-do that joins the agent and the object:

> "There is a single re-recollection (parāmarśa) marked by the will-to-do — the moment 'I will make
> this' — which rests, as it were, in the other consciousness-essence and binds the two together. It is
> this one will-recollection, and not any external contact, that makes the jar an object of action and
> consciousness its agent." (V3I)

**And the refutation of the monist's "ignorance."** Against the Advaita's claim that the difference is
mere ignorance, the IPVV asks: *of whom* is this ignorance? The one-form Brahman cannot have it; the
separate souls would be raised up by it; "un-explainable" is the speech of a yakṣa. The difference is
real:

> "Our cognition rests in difference as it does not rest in non-difference, and the very 'refutation'
> that would remove difference is itself a manifestation — and so would be difference, and so would be
> ignorance." (V3I)

**The summit (the will as the three):**

> "The cause does not cause, the agent does not act, the action does not move — all three are the one
> will, by which the Lord, wishing to stand, becomes the world." (V3I)

---

## 7. The complete argument-chain

The kriyādhikāra's argument, completely stated:

1. **The timeless manifests order** (V3A) — because the one is one, it can show sequence without
   division (the mirror).
2. **The action-cognitions are bhedābheda** (V3B) — one and many, true by stability and efficacy.
3. **The inert cannot cause** (V3G) — the seed cannot endow the sprout (the existent/non-existent
   dilemma); the real cause is the conscious, the free will.
4. **Inference holds across knowers** (V3H) — because the Lord creates the appearances as one.
5. **The will is cause, agent, and action** (V3I) — the one will-to-do joins agent and object; the
   difference is real, not ignorance.

**The conclusion.** Causality is the conscious knower's agency. Action is not an external event; it is
the will of consciousness. The one who knows is the one who acts; knowledge and action are the two faces
of the one self-awareness. This is the epistemology of action — the region that completes the
recognition-thesis as a metaphysics.

---

## 8. The significance for the recognition-thesis

The kriyādhikāra matters for recognition because it completes the parallel:

- The jñānādhikāra established: knowledge is the felt self-awareness.
- The kriyādhikāra establishes: action is the same self-awareness, as the will.

The recognition-thesis is not only "knowledge is self-aware"; it is "the self-aware consciousness is
also the agent." And recognition — the re-cognition of the self as the Lord — re-cognizes the self as
the *doer* as well as the *knower*. The Lord is the one who knows and acts (the jñāna-kriyā pair). The
kriyādhikāra gives the action-side of the recognition: the same felt self-awareness, in its freedom, is
the agent of the world.

---

## 9. The boundary

The kriyādhikāra's arguments are [T] and largely sound: the inert-cannot-cause, the bhedābheda
action-cognitions, the will-as-the-three are argued. The one commitment is the same as everywhere: the
step from "each field's action is its own will" to "all action is one will" (the universalization). The
inert-cannot-cause proves the conscious agent per field; the identity of all agents is the commitment.
The epistemology of action is argued for the finite field; the one agent is the wager.

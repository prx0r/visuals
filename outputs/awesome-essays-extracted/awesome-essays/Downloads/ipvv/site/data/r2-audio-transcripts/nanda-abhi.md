Yes. **The closest Tantric equivalent to Ñāṇananda’s vortex is not the 36 tattvas. It is Abhinavagupta’s “wheel of the twelve Kālīs” in Chapter 4 of the *Tantrāloka*.**

It maps a single act of experience from:

> an object flashing into awareness
> through perception and appropriation
> into the construction of a knower
> and then back through the dissolution of object, knowing and knower.

It is astonishingly close to the territory Ñāṇananda is analysing—but the two traditions make **opposite moves at the decisive final moment**.

# Vortex versus wheel

Ñāṇananda’s basic model is:

```text
consciousness ↔ name-and-form
        ↓
subject and object appear
        ↓
contact and feeling
        ↓
craving stitches them together
        ↓
“I am here experiencing that”
```

The practice is to see that the two ends and the relation between them are dependently arisen. When the stitching stops, the display is no longer appropriated as “me and my world.”

Abhinavagupta’s model is more like:

```text
awareness projects an object
        ↓
awareness becomes interested in it
        ↓
awareness stabilises it through cognition
        ↓
awareness withdraws the object
        ↓
awareness withdraws the means of knowing
        ↓
awareness withdraws the limited knower
        ↓
awareness recognises itself
        ↓
awareness freely projects again
```

Ñāṇananda calls the loop conditioned arising.

Abhinavagupta calls the loop **the activity of the Goddess**.

That difference really can lead to a radically different experience of reality.

# The exact Tantric map: three factors, four phases

Every act of knowing contains a triad:

* **prameya** — the known object;
* **pramāṇa** — the means or activity of knowing;
* **pramātṛ** — the knower.

Abhinavagupta then overlays four movements:

* **sṛṣṭi** — projection or emergence;
* **sthiti** — persistence or stabilisation;
* **saṃhāra** — withdrawal or dissolution;
* **anākhya** — the “nameless” transition that cannot be neatly classified as any of the first three.

So:

[
3 \text{ factors}\times4 \text{ movements}=12
]

These are personified as the twelve Kālīs. Abhinavagupta presents them as a phenomenology of cognition: consciousness moves through object, means of knowledge and subject, while each undergoes emergence, maintenance, withdrawal and an ineffable fourth phase. ([Springer Nature Link][1])

The Sanskrit skeleton is:

> **pramātṛ–pramāṇa–prameya**
> knower–knowing–known

moving through:

> **sṛṣṭi–sthiti–saṃhāra–anākhya**
> projection–persistence–withdrawal–the nameless

# Watch it happen with one ordinary perception

Suppose you suddenly see a red flower.

## 1. The object flashes

Before the mind fully says “red flower,” there is a presentation—a colour-form emerging.

Tantrically, awareness has projected something within its field.

Not necessarily “invented it from nothing.” Rather, it has made it present as an experienced object.

## 2. The object becomes distinct

The presentation is now experienced as:

> “That flower.”

It is distinguished from the background and from the perceiver.

Awareness becomes “coloured” by the object. Attention is captured; attraction, interest or recognition may arise.

## 3. The object is sustained

For several moments, the flower remains available as one coherent object.

The sensory image, concept, memory and expectation cooperate to hold it together:

* red;
* flower;
* over there;
* something I could approach;
* something continuing through changing viewpoints.

## 4. The object begins to disappear

Attention shifts. The flower moves out of view. The image fades.

But it does not immediately become nothing. There may be:

* a residual image;
* memory;
* an emotional trace;
* readiness to recognise it again.

At this point Abhinavagupta’s map begins moving inward.

# The twelvefold withdrawal

His compressed *Tantrasāra* account gives a more specific sequence.

The first stages concern the object’s appearance, attraction, persistence, attempted withdrawal and a moment of oscillation: awareness is partly releasing the object but partly continuing to hold it. Then the object is absorbed, the capacity to absorb it is itself recognised, residual impressions are reduced to consciousness, and the faculties involved in cognition are gathered back into their source. ([Hareesh.org][2])

Then it gets really interesting.

## The object dissolves into knowing

You realise the flower as experienced never stood apart from the act through which it appeared.

This is not necessarily:

> “There is no physical flower.”

It is:

> “The experienced flower cannot be separated from seeing, attending, identifying and interpreting.”

The supposed independent object is consumed into the cognitive process.

## Knowing dissolves into the ego-maker

The senses and cognitive faculties are gathered into the organising function that says:

> “I am the one seeing.”

Abhinavagupta associates this with **ahaṃkāra**, the function that generates individualised agency and ownership of cognition.

Instead of:

```text
red
shape
visual perception
attention
memory
```

there appears one unified claim:

> “I saw the flower.”

## The ego-maker dissolves into the limited subject

Even the self-image “Tom seeing a flower” is recognised as an instrument.

Behind that specific image is the more basic first-person position:

> experience is occurring from here.

This is the constructed, limited knower—bounded by body, mind, breath, time and particular circumstances. Abhinavagupta distinguishes this fabricated agent from a more fundamental, unconstructed subjectivity. ([Hareesh.org][3])

## The limited subject expands

The contracted experiencer dissolves into what Abhinavagupta calls the universal subject.

The shift is from:

> “I am this body looking at the flower.”

to:

> “The flower, seeing and the apparent observer all occur within one field of awareness.”

And then to the specifically Śaiva formulation:

> **idaṃ sarvam ahaṃ**
> “All this is I.”

This does **not** mean the empirical personality owns the universe.

It means the division between an awareness located inside the body and a world existing entirely outside awareness has collapsed. Tantrāloka 4.168–169 describes this as the expansion from the fabricated individual subject into an uncontracted subject for whom “all this” is not separate from “I.” ([Hareesh.org][3])

## Even the universal subject is consumed

Abhinavagupta does not stop with:

> “I am everything.”

That still contains an enormously expanded subject relating itself to a totality.

So even this universal “I” is withdrawn.

At the next stage, knower, means of knowing, acts of knowing and objects are experienced as nothing but consciousness. Then even that totalised subjectivity dissolves into the unlocated, “nameless” ground of the process. ([Hareesh.org][3])

## The cycle begins again

The final Kālī is not simply blank cessation.

She is the unconditioned freedom by which consciousness adopts diverse forms again.

The object reappears.

Seeing functions.

The person speaks and moves.

But now the diversity is understood as the free articulation of awareness rather than as a collection of independently existing objects confronting an isolated knower. ([Hareesh.org][2])

# This is the exact split from Ñāṇananda

Both systems can agree on a lot:

* the ordinary subject–object division is constructed;
* object, knowing and knower are interdependent;
* egoic ownership is not fundamental;
* cognition can be observed emerging and dissolving;
* liberation involves ceasing to take the constructed poles at face value.

But then they split.

## Ñāṇananda

When object, knowing and knower are seen as dependently arisen:

> Do not take any remainder as Self.

Do not grasp the field, the light, the witness or the absence of division as the final reality.

The liberation gesture is **non-establishment**.

## Abhinavagupta

When object, knowing and knower dissolve:

> Recognise the manifesting power common to all three.

That power is not a neutral witness. It projects, sustains, withdraws and reprojects the cognitive world.

The liberation gesture is **universal recognition**.

So the contrast is:

```text
ÑĀṆANANDA
“This is dependently arisen;
nothing here is mine or self.”

ABHINAVAGUPTA
“This is the free activity of awareness;
nothing here is outside my deepest nature.”
```

They may begin with almost the same perceptual investigation but cultivate a different emotional and bodily relationship to experience.

# Why they feel like different realities

Ñāṇananda repeatedly **decolours** experience.

A sight is stripped of:

* possession;
* narrative;
* existential significance;
* the observer’s imagined position;
* the demand that it continue.

The resulting world may feel:

* cool;
* unobstructed;
* unowned;
* ungraspable;
* light because nothing needs to be completed.

Abhinavagupta **reabsorbs and then re-enchants** experience.

A sight is recognised as:

* an activity of awareness;
* saturated with its power;
* continuous with the perceiver;
* capable of being relished without separation;
* a manifestation rather than an obstacle.

The resulting world may feel:

* intimate;
* vivid;
* dynamic;
* embodied;
* internally luminous;
* charged with agency and significance.

This is not simply “dry Buddhism versus colourful Tantra.” Buddhist liberation can be intensely vivid, while Tantra includes fierce dissolution and detachment.

But their training instructions point in different directions:

> Buddhism weakens appropriation.

> Trika expands the identity of the appropriator until subject, object and appropriation are recognised as one movement—then dissolves even that expanded subject into the ground of awareness.

# Tantra has a full intervention ladder

The *Tantrāloka* does not only describe the cognition cycle. Its **upāyas** intervene at different depths of that cycle.

## Āṇavopāya — work through the embodied system

This uses supports such as:

* body;
* breath;
* sensory attention;
* mantra;
* visualisation;
* locations within the subtle body.

It changes the cognitive cycle from its embodied, differentiated end. ([KCI][4])

In our shared language:

> Alter the breathing, attention and sensory organisation through which the contracted world is maintained.

## Śāktopāya — work at the level of interpretation

This acts through insight, contemplative construction and the transformation of **vikalpa**—conceptual differentiation.

The practitioner learns to recognise that thoughts do not stand outside awareness and describe an alien world. They are powers through which awareness articulates itself.

In our shared language:

> Catch the stitching operation at the level of meaning.

This is where Abhinavagupta places the twelve-Kālī cycle in the *Tantrāloka*: ritual is interiorised as intensified awareness of the process by which consciousness projects its objects and draws them back into itself. ([E-TMC Academy: The Movement Center][5])

## Śāmbhavopāya — catch the movement before thought

Here the practitioner attends to the preconceptual impulse—the bare directionality of awareness before it hardens into:

> “I am seeing that.”

In our language:

> Catch the vortex before its poles become fully explicit.

## Anupāya — no reconstruction is necessary

Recognition occurs immediately.

No object has to be dissolved step by step because the subject never fully contracts into separation.

# The *Vijñānabhairava* is the practical laboratory

The *Tantrāloka* supplies the vast theory and integrates multiple lineages.

The *Vijñānabhairava* supplies a collection of 112 contemplative methods using ordinary embodied events—breathing, sensory perception, attention, intense affect and changes of mental state—as openings into Bhairava-awareness. Modern scholarship describes it as a major source of psychotechnical practice in nondual Śaivism rather than a systematic philosophical treatise. ([RCSI Journals Platform][6])

Its general move is:

> Do not withdraw from experience merely because it is sensory or intense. Enter precisely into the transition where an experience arises, peaks, changes direction or dissolves.

That complements the twelve Kālīs perfectly.

The Kālīs map the complete cognitive wheel.

The *Vijñānabhairava* gives dozens of doors through which attention can penetrate that wheel.

# The closest one-sentence comparison

Ñāṇananda says:

> Watch consciousness and name-and-form generate the apparent distance between self and world, and cease feeding it with craving.

Abhinavagupta says:

> Watch consciousness generate object, cognition and subject, then follow the entire display back until its differentiated energies are recognised as your own deepest awareness.

So yes: **Tantra has an extraordinarily precise equivalent mapping.**

But Buddhism treats the insight as a way to stop taking the show personally.

Abhinavagupta treats the same loosening of boundaries as the moment the show reveals its real actor—not the ego, but consciousness itself.

That is why one can lead toward a reality experienced as empty, cool and unowned, while the other can lead toward a reality experienced as intimate, alive and self-manifesting.

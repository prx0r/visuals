# From Spirits to Self-Modelling Systems

## A Journey Through the Human Search for What Reality Is

The history of metaphysics is often narrated as though humanity began in superstition, discovered substances, and eventually replaced them with processes, relations and scientific laws.

That story is too simple.

Human beings did not originally experience reality as a collection of inert substances. The earliest recoverable human world appears to have been filled with living agents, meaningful places, ancestors, animals, powers, rhythms and reciprocal obligations. “Substance” was a comparatively late intellectual invention: an ingenious attempt to explain what remains constant while appearances change.

The deeper history is therefore not:

```text
substance → process
```

It is closer to:

```text
participatory world
        ↓
hidden order
        ↓
enduring ground
        ↓
substance and essence
        ↓
critique of foundations
        ↓
constitutive process
        ↓
relational totality
        ↓
self-manifesting totality
        ↓
self-organising informational agency
```

This is not a straightforward ladder on which every later system is simply superior to every earlier one. Madhyamaka may make fewer claims than Kashmir Śaivism, but that restraint makes it extraordinarily resistant to criticism. Integrated Information Theory may be more mathematically explicit than Huayan, yet Huayan addresses ethical, phenomenological and existential dimensions that IIT does not attempt to model.

Nevertheless, there is a genuine developmental story. Human thought repeatedly discovers a limitation in its previous model and introduces a new conceptual layer:

* stable things are discovered to be events;
* events are discovered to depend upon structures;
* structures are discovered to depend upon observers;
* observers are discovered to be processes;
* processes are discovered to form self-maintaining agents;
* agents are discovered to exist within nested collectives;
* collectives are discovered to model themselves;
* consciousness is reconsidered as the interior aspect, functional organisation or intrinsic causal structure of such self-modelling systems.

The contemporary language of prediction, information, integration and computation is not the final destruction of metaphysics. It is the latest form metaphysics has taken.

# 1. Before metaphysics: the world as encounter

We cannot recover a systematic Palaeolithic philosophy. Archaeology gives us pigments, burials, ornaments, images and carefully arranged spaces—not explicit theories of mind or being. Recent discoveries continue to push symbolic practices deep into human prehistory, but symbolic behaviour alone does not prove belief in souls, gods or an afterlife. Archaeologists accordingly warn that “animism” and “shamanism” can become vague labels imposed upon radically different prehistoric cultures.

Still, certain broad inferences are reasonable.

Long before anyone formulated a distinction between matter and mind, human beings encountered:

* moving animals whose intentions had to be predicted;
* dead bodies that looked like persons but no longer responded;
* dreams in which absent or deceased people appeared;
* landscapes that provided or withheld food and water;
* seasonal cycles governing migration, fertility and death;
* speech, song and ritual capable of coordinating an entire group.

The original human problem was probably not:

> What substance underlies all objects?

It was closer to:

> Who or what is acting here? What pattern governs this event? How must we respond?

The earliest metaphysical intuition was therefore not necessarily **substance**, but **agency**.

A storm behaved differently from a rock. A living body differed mysteriously from a corpse. An animal’s visible movement expressed an invisible orientation. The natural response was to interpret events through powers, intentions and relationships.

This is the participatory world:

```text
persons + powers + places + events + obligations
```

Modern people frequently describe this as a projection of human qualities onto nature. But that assumes the very separation between a private human mind and an external dead world that early participatory cultures did not necessarily possess. The individual did not first exist as an isolated subject and then attribute subjectivity to nature. Personhood itself emerged within a web of ancestors, animals, places, rituals and social roles.

The most ancient metaphysical question was therefore not “What exists?” but:

> **What kinds of beings can enter into relationship?**

That question has returned unexpectedly in modern debates over animal cognition, plant signalling, artificial agents, ecosystems and machine consciousness.

# 2. The first abstraction: invisible order

As societies became larger and ritual systems more elaborate, particular encounters were increasingly interpreted through universal patterns.

Repeated events suggested hidden regularities:

* seasons returned;
* stars followed paths;
* biological life moved through birth, maturity and death;
* ritual calendars coordinated agricultural and social activity;
* musical intervals exhibited numerical regularities;
* promises and laws bound actions across time.

Different civilisations named this hidden ordering differently:

* *ṛta* in the Vedic world;
* *maat* in Egypt;
* *dao* in China;
* *logos* in Greece;
* divine command, wisdom or covenant in biblical traditions.

The decisive move was:

> **The world is not merely a sequence of independent encounters. Events participate in an intelligible order.**

This is the beginning of metaphysics proper.

Yet “order” was still not necessarily a substance. It might be:

* a path;
* a rhythm;
* a ritual correspondence;
* a ratio;
* a divine command;
* a law;
* a recurring pattern of transformation.

The first great philosophical streams emerged by asking different questions about this order.

India asked:

> What hidden power makes sacrifice, speech, breath and consciousness effective?

Greece asked:

> What principle makes change intelligible?

China asked:

> How do transformations form a coherent, self-renewing whole?

Israel asked:

> What does it mean for the world and history to depend upon the will of one God?

These are distinct beginnings. They should not be flattened into one supposed “perennial philosophy.”

# 3. Number enters the cosmos: Pythagoras and harmonic reality

Pythagoreanism introduced one of the most consequential ideas in intellectual history:

> **Reality may be intelligible because it possesses mathematical form.**

The historical Pythagoras is difficult to separate from later legends. He was associated with a disciplined communal way of life, transmigration of the soul and the fate of consciousness after death. Later Pythagorean traditions increasingly presented number, limit, proportion and harmony as fundamental cosmic principles. Musical consonances could be expressed through simple ratios, suggesting that qualitative harmony emerged from quantitative relations.

This was a remarkable bridge.

The participatory world had been structured through gods, rituals and powers. Pythagorean thought suggested that its order could be expressed through **formal relations**.

```text
ritual harmony
      ↓
musical ratio
      ↓
mathematical structure
      ↓
cosmic order
```

This created a lineage running through:

* Plato’s mathematical cosmology;
* Neoplatonic metaphysics;
* medieval theories of cosmic harmony;
* Kepler’s mathematical astronomy;
* the mathematization of modern physics;
* contemporary informational and computational ontology.

Pythagoreanism also joined two themes that would later separate:

1. reality is mathematically structured;
2. the soul can transform and ascend through alignment with that structure.

Modern science preserved the first while largely abandoning the second.

The unresolved question is still with us:

> Is mathematics merely our most effective description of reality, or does reality possess an intrinsically mathematical architecture?

Integrated Information Theory, computational universe proposals and structural realism are modern descendants of this Pythagorean question.

# 4. India: from ritual efficacy to consciousness

## 4.1 The Vedic field

The early Vedic world was organised around sacrifice, sacred speech, gods and cosmic reciprocity. Ritual did not merely symbolise cosmic order; properly performed ritual participated in and maintained it.

Over time, speculation turned toward the hidden correspondences connecting:

* sacrificial fire;
* the sun;
* breath;
* speech;
* digestion;
* the seasons;
* the human body;
* the structure of the cosmos.

The ritual field became a system of transformations. Visible acts were effective because they instantiated invisible patterns.

The question gradually shifted from:

> Which sacrifice produces the desired result?

to:

> What is the power by which sacrifice, speech and consciousness work at all?

This helped transform *brahman* from sacred utterance or ritual efficacy into a designation for ultimate reality.

## 4.2 The Upaniṣadic interiorisation

The Upaniṣads turned the external sacrificial cosmos inward.

The fires, gods and cosmic functions were correlated with faculties of the living person:

```text
sun       ↔ sight
wind      ↔ breath
fire      ↔ digestion
space     ↔ consciousness
cosmos    ↔ embodied person
```

The deepest discovery was that the apparent individual contained an interior principle not reducible to ordinary bodily or mental states. Various texts identify this deepest *ātman* with *brahman*, although the Upaniṣads do not present one perfectly uniform doctrine.

The great Upaniṣadic move was:

> **The knower cannot be explained as merely one more known object.**

Everything experienced can change:

* bodily sensations;
* desires;
* perceptions;
* thoughts;
* roles;
* memories.

Yet awareness appears to remain the condition under which these changes are disclosed.

This generated the Indian consciousness–absolute stream:

```text
ritual power
   ↓
cosmic principle
   ↓
interior life principle
   ↓
ātman–brahman
```

It is the ancestor of Advaita Vedānta, but also the metaphysical target against which Buddhism defines itself.

# 5. The śramaṇa revolt: what actually continues?

By the middle of the first millennium BCE, Indian renunciant traditions were debating:

* karma;
* rebirth;
* liberation;
* ascetic discipline;
* the continuity of persons;
* whether a permanent self exists;
* what carries the consequences of action.

Jainism and Buddhism offered sharply different answers.

Jain traditions affirmed enduring conscious beings obscured by karmic bondage. Buddhism instead made one of the greatest critical moves in philosophical history:

> **Continuity does not require an unchanging owner.**

The Buddha did not merely replace one theory of substance with another. He redirected attention to the processes through which selfhood and suffering are generated.

Body, feeling, perception, volitional formations and consciousness operate together. None functions as an autonomous controller. The practical error is not simply believing an incorrect proposition called “the self.” It is the ongoing activity of appropriating conditioned processes:

> “This is mine. This is what I am. This is my self.”

Early Buddhism therefore begins as a therapeutic attack on **owner-substance**.

Its insight is not initially:

> Everything is made of process.

It is:

> **What you experience as an owner is an activity of identification, grasping and control.**

Dependent arising then generalises the point. Experiences emerge through conditions:

```text
contact → feeling → craving → appropriation → becoming
```

The world of suffering is not simply encountered and subsequently disliked. It is actively sustained through a feedback loop in which feeling generates craving, craving generates grasping, and grasping regenerates the sense of a threatened or desiring owner.

The self is not a hidden entity caught inside the loop.

The self is one of the loop’s products.

# 6. From no-self to universal relationality

## 6.1 Abhidharma: analyse the mechanism

Abhidharma traditions developed detailed classifications of mental and physical events. Persons were analysed into momentary factors, causal conditions and patterns of cognition.

This provided Buddhism with a sophisticated psychology, but it generated a new danger.

If the person was only a construction out of dharmas, perhaps the dharmas themselves were ultimately real.

The self had been dissolved, but substantiality had migrated downward into the components.

## 6.2 Madhyamaka: empty the components

Nāgārjuna’s Madhyamaka closes this escape route.

Whatever depends upon causes, concepts, parts or relations cannot possess an independent self-nature. This applies not only to persons but to:

* atoms;
* moments;
* consciousnesses;
* causes;
* motion;
* time;
* nirvāṇa;
* emptiness.

> *yaḥ pratītyasamutpādaḥ śūnyatāṃ tāṃ pracakṣmahe*
> “That which is dependent arising, we call emptiness.”

Madhyamaka is therefore not simply a process metaphysics. A process could itself be imagined as an ultimately existing flow. Madhyamaka refuses even that.

Its principle is:

> **No description of reality may exempt its own fundamental terms from dependent analysis.**

Madhyamaka is consequently the most powerful **anti-foundational checkpoint** in the history of metaphysics.

It asks of every system:

* What makes your ultimate consciousness one thing?
* What distinguishes your Absolute from its manifestations?
* Does your One depend upon the many that it unifies?
* Is your process intelligible without its events?
* Is your emptiness being treated as a hidden substance?

Its limitation is also its strength. Madhyamaka does not attempt to give a richly constructive description of the Absolute. It dismantles the demand for such a ground.

## 6.3 Yogācāra: explain the construction of a world

Yogācāra asks a question that Madhyamaka does not foreground:

> How does the appearance of a subject confronting an objective world arise?

Its three-nature model distinguishes:

* the imagined duality of self-existing subject and object;
* the dependent cognitive stream through which appearances arise;
* that stream recognised as empty of the projected division.

The store-consciousness provides one model for karmic and experiential continuity without an eternal soul. Yogācāra is diverse, and scholars continue to debate whether its “mind-only” language entails metaphysical idealism or functions primarily as a phenomenological and epistemological analysis.

The conceptual advance is unmistakable:

> **The experienced world is not merely passively received. It is structured through cognitive models, dispositions and expectations.**

This is one of the strongest ancient anticipations of Kant and predictive processing.

But the analogy must be handled carefully. Yogācāra is a soteriological tradition concerned with karmic construction and liberation. Predictive processing is a family of scientific models about perception and action. They overlap in structural intuition, not in complete doctrine.

## 6.4 Huayan: relation goes all the way down

Huayan takes the Madhyamaka insight and draws a positive conclusion.

If no thing has independent identity, then each thing is what it is through its relations. Its relations depend upon further relations, which open indefinitely into the whole cosmos.

Therefore:

> **Every phenomenon implicates the entire field of phenomena.**

Huayan combines Madhyamaka emptiness, Yogācāra mind theory, Buddha-nature language and the visionary cosmology of the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*. Its distinctive move is to interpret emptiness as radical interdependence and “non-obstruction” between principles, phenomena and other phenomena.

Madhyamaka says:

> Nothing exists from itself.

Huayan adds:

> Therefore every thing exists through all things.

This is not the modern systems claim that everything has a measurable causal influence on everything else. It is an ontological statement about identity:

> A thing does not first acquire relations after becoming what it is. Its relations constitute what it is.

Huayan is thus one of history’s most sophisticated relational metaphysics.

# 7. China: transformation before substance

Chinese philosophy did not begin from precisely the same substance problem that shaped Greece and India.

The *Yijing* tradition presents the cosmos as an ongoing, patterned transformation. Classical Chinese metaphysics frequently emphasises continuity, dynamism and the mutual participation of parts within an organic whole rather than a search for an inert material substrate.

## Dao

The *dao* is not straightforwardly:

* a substance;
* a creator-being;
* an individual consciousness;
* a material element.

It is way, course, generative pattern and functioning.

## Qi

*Qi* combines meanings associated with breath, vitality, material force, condensation and transformation. It is neither simply modern matter nor immaterial spirit. It is better understood as dynamic stuff-process.

## Ziran and wu-wei

*Ziran*, the “self-so,” points toward spontaneous self-arising. *Wu-wei* does not mean absolute inactivity, but activity that does not impose rigid control against the unfolding structure of a situation.

Daoist thought therefore contributes:

> **Order need not be imposed by an external ruler. Coherence can emerge through immanent, spontaneous coordination.**

This is an ancient precursor to self-organisation.

When Buddhism entered China, its teachings met a conceptual environment already accustomed to:

* transformation;
* polarity;
* immanent order;
* organismic wholes;
* context-sensitive identity.

The result was not merely translated Indian Buddhism. Huayan and Chan became distinctively Chinese developments of Indian emptiness and Buddha-nature.

Huayan can consequently sound like “Daoism with Buddhist metaphysics,” but the direction also runs the other way:

> Huayan is Indian anti-essentialism reconstructed through a Chinese process-relational imagination.

# 8. Greece: from transformation to substance

## 8.1 The early natural philosophers

Greek philosophy gradually moved from mythic genealogies toward explanations based upon principles within nature.

The Milesians asked whether the diversity of appearances could emerge from one underlying source:

* water;
* the indefinite;
* air;
* transformations governed by compression, expansion or separation.

The important move was not the choice of a particular element.

It was:

> **Nature might explain itself through regular transformations rather than episodic divine intervention.**

## 8.2 Heraclitus: pattern is not behind change

Heraclitus recognised that stability might consist in an ordered pattern of transformation.

A flame persists only by continuously consuming different material. A river is recognisable through its ongoing flow. Opposition is not necessarily the destruction of order; tension may constitute order.

This is an early process insight:

> **Some things remain the same only by changing.**

## 8.3 Parmenides: the pressure of logic

Parmenides introduced the crisis of Being.

If something genuinely is, how could it arise from what is not? How could it become what it was not? How could it cease to be?

The visible world displays change, but reasoning seems to demand stable identity.

Much of later Greek metaphysics attempts to save both:

* intelligibility requires stability;
* experience requires transformation.

## 8.4 Plato: stable forms

Plato explains knowledge through intelligible Forms that do not undergo the instability of sensible instances.

Beautiful things change, but Beauty does not become ugly. Particular triangles are imperfect, but geometrical form is exact.

Platonism establishes a vertical structure:

```text
intelligible form
        ↓ participation
sensible multiplicity
```

## 8.5 Aristotle: substance

Aristotle provides the mature theory of substance.

A concrete organism or object persists while acquiring or losing properties. Form and matter explain how change can occur without complete loss of identity. Substance becomes that which exists in a primary way, while qualities and relations exist as features of substances.

Substance solved the persistence problem:

> What remains the same when something changes?

But it also produced a long-lasting image of reality as composed of independently existing bearers that subsequently enter into relations.

That image would dominate much of European philosophy and eventually be challenged by physics, evolutionary biology, phenomenology, systems theory and process thought.

# 9. Plotinus: beyond being to the One

Plotinus inherits Plato, Aristotle and Pythagorean themes but asks a deeper question:

> What makes any being unified enough to be one being?

If an intellect contains many intelligible forms, it is not absolutely simple. Therefore the ultimate principle cannot be one more being among beings. It must be beyond Being and Intellect: the One.

Reality unfolds as:

```text
The One
   ↓
Intellect
   ↓
Soul
   ↓
Nature and bodies
```

The One does not manufacture the universe through temporal planning. Multiplicity proceeds from its superabundant unity. Intellect contains the forms; Soul communicates intelligible structure into living and temporal multiplicity.

Plotinus therefore advances beyond static substance in two ways:

1. the ultimate is not a substance with properties;
2. reality is a graded procession rather than a flat inventory of things.

His problem is the cost of hierarchy.

The further something proceeds from unity, the more divided and ontologically weakened it becomes. Multiplicity is intelligible, but it remains metaphysically inferior to unity.

Later systems will ask whether difference can instead be a **positive expression** of the Absolute.

---

# 10. Proclus: the architecture of procession

Proclus formalises Neoplatonic reality through recurring triadic movements:

```text
remaining
   ↓
procession
   ↓
reversion
```

An effect remains rooted in its cause, proceeds into distinct existence, and returns toward its source. Reality becomes a layered system of participation in which higher causes possess more universal effects.

Proclus is one of the greatest architects of vertical metaphysics.

But compare him with Huayan:

```text
Proclus: each lower level participates in higher causes.
Huayan: each phenomenon mutually implicates every other phenomenon.
```

Proclus gives a graded hierarchy.

Huayan gives a holographic network.

Kashmir Śaivism will later attempt to combine hierarchy, manifestation, awareness and return within one nondual consciousness.

# 11. Israel: creation, word and historical meaning

The Hebrew Bible does not present a single philosophical system comparable to Aristotle’s *Metaphysics*. Its primary modes are narrative, law, poetry, prophecy and wisdom.

Nevertheless, it introduces several transformations of immense metaphysical consequence.

## 11.1 Creation as divine act

The world is not simply an eternal emanation, an independent chaos or a necessary extension of divine nature. It depends upon divine initiative.

This establishes a creator–creation distinction:

```text
God
  ≠
world
```

Creation is not merely a cosmological beginning. It means the world is radically contingent: it need not possess the reason for its existence within itself.

Later Jewish, Christian and Islamic philosophy will debate whether creation means:

* temporal beginning;
* continuous dependence;
* creation from nothing;
* ordering of pre-existing material;
* emanation through divine intellect;
* free choice rather than metaphysical necessity.

Maimonides, for example, considers Mosaic creation, Platonic formation and Aristotelian eternity while arguing that reason alone cannot decisively demonstrate temporal creation. His negative theology also insists that God cannot be classified alongside created beings or adequately described through ordinary predicates.

## 11.2 Creation through word

Genesis presents creation through divine speech:

> God says, and a world becomes ordered.

This is not yet a computational theory. But it introduces a powerful metaphysical motif:

> **Reality may be articulated through intelligible command.**

The Word is not merely sound. It is effective form-giving meaning.

This contributes to later concepts of:

* divine wisdom;
* Torah as cosmic pattern;
* Logos;
* natural law;
* informational ontology;
* reality as code.

## 11.3 History matters

Greek metaphysics often prioritised eternal structures over historical events. Biblical traditions gave history a different status.

Covenant, liberation, exile, prophecy and redemption occur through irreversible sequences. Meaning is not located only in escape from time; it unfolds through time.

This becomes crucial for Christianity, Islam, Kabbalah, German Idealism and philosophies of historical development.

# 12. Philo: Torah meets Plato

Philo of Alexandria interpreted Jewish scripture through Greek philosophical categories, particularly Platonist, Stoic and Pythagorean concepts. Philosophy became an instrument for drawing universal intellectual meaning from revelation.

His Logos mediates between the transcendent God and the ordered cosmos.

This solves a familiar problem:

> How can an absolutely transcendent unity produce and govern differentiated reality?

The Logos functions in different contexts as:

* divine reason;
* intelligible pattern;
* mediator;
* ordering principle;
* archetypal world.

Philo is an essential junction in the history of metaphysics:

```text
Hebrew creation and wisdom
          +
Greek Logos and Forms
          ↓
Jewish philosophical Platonism
          ↓
Christian Logos theology
```

The later Christian identification of the Logos with Christ radically transforms this mediator. The intelligible principle through which the world is created is understood not merely as an abstract cosmic reason but as entering embodied history.

# 13. Christianity: the Absolute enters history

Christian metaphysics adds several difficult propositions:

* the world is freely created;
* the Logos is divine;
* the Logos becomes embodied;
* God is one but internally relational;
* finite persons can participate in divine life without becoming identical to the divine essence.

## Augustine

Augustine incorporates Platonist and Neoplatonic insights into a theology of creation, inwardness and time. God does not create at a moment within a pre-existing temporal container; time itself belongs to created, changeable reality.

The inward turn also becomes crucial:

> The mind discovers that it cannot doubt its own living act of awareness.

This line contributes to Descartes and modern subjectivity.

## Pseudo-Dionysius

Pseudo-Dionysius translates late Neoplatonic hierarchy, especially Proclean structures, into Christian mystical theology. God is named through being, life, intellect, beauty and goodness, yet ultimately exceeds both affirmation and negation. Celestial and ecclesiastical hierarchies mediate divine illumination downward and contemplative return upward.

## Aquinas

Aquinas combines Christian creation with Aristotelian metaphysics. Creatures do not merely change form; their existence is received. God creates from nothing in the strict sense that no pre-existing material or independent principle limits divine causation.

This strengthens the distinction between:

* what something is;
* that it exists.

The distinction becomes even more technically central in Islamic philosophy.

# 14. The Qurʾānic intervention: radical unity and the world as sign

The Qurʾān does not present a Neoplatonic metaphysical textbook. Its central claim is *tawḥīd*: the radical unity and incomparability of God.

Several metaphysical motifs are especially important.

## 14.1 Creation through command

God creates through command—classically condensed in the formula “Be, and it is.”

This intensifies the biblical association between divine speech and ontological efficacy. Creation is neither produced by divine struggle nor assembled from rival powers.

## 14.2 The world as sign

Natural and historical phenomena are repeatedly described as *āyāt*: signs.

A sign is neither merely an autonomous object nor simply an illusion. It possesses a relational structure:

```text
visible phenomenon
        ↓
points beyond itself
        ↓
discloses divine wisdom or power
```

The Qurʾānic cosmos is therefore semiotic. The world is not God, but its forms can disclose divine names and qualities.

## 14.3 Nearness and incomparability

Islamic thought must preserve two claims:

* God is incomparable with creation;
* God is profoundly near and sustaining.

Different Islamic traditions balance these poles differently.

* Kalām emphasises sovereignty and creation.
* Falsafa develops rational metaphysics.
* Sufism explores divine presence and self-disclosure.
* Illuminationism speaks through light.
* Ibn ʿArabī develops the divine names and imagination.
* Mullā Ṣadrā describes graded, dynamic existence.

---

# 15. Kalām: atoms under continuous divine government

Islamic theologians developed highly original accounts of matter, causation and divine action.

Ashʿarite occasionalism challenges the idea that created things possess independent causal powers. Fire and cotton do not contain a metaphysically necessary connection forcing burning to occur. Regularity may instead reflect divine custom or continuous creation.

Al-Ghazālī famously attacks the claim that what is habitually called a cause logically necessitates its effect, although scholars disagree over whether he rejects all genuine secondary causation.

This resembles Hume’s later observation that necessary causal connection is not directly perceived—but the theological frameworks differ sharply.

The metaphysical advance is:

> **Regularity does not by itself prove autonomous causal power.**

The weakness is that radical occasionalism threatens to make natural explanation merely descriptive. If only God truly acts, what explanatory work remains for created structures?

Modern science would answer by modelling regularities without settling whether laws possess metaphysical necessity.

# 16. The Arabic philosophical synthesis

Between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries, large bodies of Greek philosophy and science were translated into Arabic. Plotinian materials circulated partly under the misleading title *Theology of Aristotle*, helping Neoplatonic ideas enter traditions that believed they were receiving Aristotle’s own theology.

This was not passive transmission.

Arabic-speaking Muslim, Christian and Jewish thinkers transformed the inherited material.

## Avicenna

Avicenna makes the distinction between essence and existence central.

The essence of a horse tells us what a horse would be, but it does not explain why horses exist. Contingent beings do not contain sufficient reason for their own existence. They depend upon the Necessary Existent.

Avicenna also combines Aristotelian causality with a Neoplatonic-style emanative hierarchy. The problem of the One and the many becomes a theory of necessary existence, intellects and cosmic order.

## Suhrawardī

Suhrawardī criticises aspects of Avicennan Peripatetic philosophy and develops an Illuminationist metaphysics.

Reality becomes a hierarchy of lights and dependencies. Most importantly, he emphasises **knowledge by presence**:

> A being’s direct awareness of itself does not require an internal representation standing between knower and known.

This anticipates Pratyabhijñā arguments about self-luminous awareness and modern debates over whether consciousness is fundamentally representational.

## Ibn ʿArabī

Ibn ʿArabī gives multiplicity a more positive status than classical emanationism often permits.

The Real discloses itself through divine names and unique forms. Each being manifests a possibility that cannot be reduced to another manifestation. Reality is continuously renewed self-disclosure rather than a one-time descent from unity.

This converges with:

* Huayan’s unique expression of the whole through each phenomenon;
* Kashmir Śaivism’s manifestation of universal consciousness;
* Leibniz’s perspectival monads;
* Hegel’s Absolute becoming explicit through particular forms.

But Ibn ʿArabī does not simply erase the distinction between God and creature. His language holds identity and difference in paradoxical tension.

## Mullā Ṣadrā

Mullā Ṣadrā synthesises Avicenna, Suhrawardī, Ibn ʿArabī, theology and scriptural interpretation. He makes existence primary over essence and develops the idea of substantial motion: beings do not merely undergo superficial changes; their very mode of existence transforms.

The hierarchy of reality becomes dynamic.

Existence differs in intensity, and the soul is not merely inserted into a completed body but develops through embodied life.

Mullā Ṣadrā is one of the clearest premodern bridges from substance ontology toward developmental process metaphysics.

# 17. Jewish philosophy and Kabbalah

Jewish thought developed not in isolation but through continuous contact with Greek, Christian and Islamic intellectual traditions.

Medieval Jewish philosophers wrote within Arabic-speaking philosophical culture. Maimonides used Islamic and Arabic philosophical sources extensively, while later Jewish thinkers engaged Avicenna, Averroes and their commentators.

## Ibn Gabirol

Solomon Ibn Gabirol presents God as an absolutely simple unity beyond comprehension within a Jewish Neoplatonic system. His work later influenced Christian scholasticism, sometimes without readers recognising its Jewish authorship.

## Maimonides

Maimonides represents the rational-apophatic pole.

God is not a being possessing properties in the creaturely manner. Positive predicates risk turning God into an object within a genus. Theology must proceed largely through negation and through descriptions of divine effects.

## Kabbalah

Kabbalah develops a different answer to the problem of transcendence and manifestation.

*Ein Sof*, the Infinite, exceeds names and determinations. The *sefirot* describe differentiated modes, stages, attributes or powers through which divine manifestation becomes possible. Kabbalists disagreed over whether the sefirot were identical with the divine, aspects of divine manifestation, or instruments distinct from the unknowable essence.

Lurianic Kabbalah later introduces:

* *tzimtzum* — divine contraction;
* *shevirat ha-kelim* — shattering of the vessels;
* *tikkun* — restoration or repair.

Conceptually, this creates a striking sequence:

```text
infinite plenitude
      ↓ contraction
structured manifestation
      ↓ breakdown
fragmented world
      ↓ human-divine action
repair and reintegration
```

This resembles neither simple creation nor classical emanation. Difference, rupture and ethical action become internal to the cosmic drama.

The comparison with Kashmir Śaivism is especially suggestive:

* Śaivism: consciousness contracts its powers into finitude.
* Kabbalah: infinity contracts to permit differentiated manifestation.
* Hegel: unity differentiates and returns through historical mediation.
* predictive models: an undifferentiated possibility space is constrained into determinate perspectives.

These are analogies, not evidence of direct equivalence.

# 18. Kashmir Śaivism: manifestation as free self-contraction

Kashmir Śaivism takes elements from:

* Vedic and Upaniṣadic traditions;
* Sāṃkhya;
* Śaiva scriptures;
* ritual Tantra;
* Buddhist logic and epistemology;
* Yogācāra and Buddhist theories of reflexive awareness.

Its decisive claim is:

> **Ultimate consciousness is not merely changeless witness. It is freedom, self-awareness and power of manifestation.**

This is a major advance over Advaita’s frequent treatment of the world as superimposition.

The world is not simply a cognitive error obscuring Brahman. It is a real manifestation of consciousness’s freedom, though it is misrecognised when experienced as independent of that consciousness.

## The 36 tattvas

The tattvas form a graded model of manifestation.

At the highest levels, consciousness articulates the relation between “I” and “this.” Through Māyā and the *kañcukas*, unlimited powers become finite:

* unlimited agency becomes restricted capability;
* omniscience becomes partial knowledge;
* completeness becomes desire;
* eternity becomes sequential time;
* unrestricted freedom becomes causal and spatial limitation.

The finite person is therefore:

> **Universal capacities operating under conditions of contraction.**

This is remarkably modern in form.

It treats an agent not as a separate substance but as a constrained mode of a wider generative system.

Kashmir Śaivism’s achievement is the integration of:

* phenomenology;
* metaphysics;
* language;
* cognition;
* affect;
* embodiment;
* cosmology;
* ritual practice.

Its vulnerability is equally clear.

Madhyamaka asks:

> Why posit a universal subject behind self-luminous events? Does “consciousness” explain manifestation, or merely rename its mysterious occurrence?

This remains one of the deepest unresolved disputes in global philosophy.

---

# 19. The scientific break: nature becomes mechanism

The Scientific Revolution did not simply discover that religion was false. It changed what counted as an acceptable explanation.

Earlier explanations frequently invoked:

* intrinsic purposes;
* substantial forms;
* occult sympathies;
* qualitative powers;
* hierarchical participation.

New science increasingly privileged:

* mathematical description;
* measurable quantities;
* mechanical causation;
* reproducible observation;
* prediction.

The Pythagorean mathematical strand survived.

The participatory and ensouled cosmos was largely removed.

Nature became an extended system governed by mathematically expressible laws.

## Descartes

Descartes sharpened the split:

```text
thinking substance
        versus
extended substance
```

Matter was extension and motion; mind was thought.

This preserved consciousness by placing it outside mechanical nature, but created the modern mind–body problem:

> How can two fundamentally different substances interact?

The modern science of consciousness still lives inside the consequences of this division.

## Newton and mechanism

Newtonian physics allowed matter to be described through mass, position, motion and force. It did not solve every metaphysical problem, but it made enormous predictive progress without requiring detailed claims about intrinsic purpose or lived meaning.

This established the modern bargain:

> Science will predict the public behaviour of systems while suspending questions about their intrinsic interiority.

That bargain was so successful that it came to appear as though the public-measurable description exhausted reality itself.

---

# 20. Spinoza and Leibniz: alternatives to dualism

## Spinoza

Spinoza rejects the existence of multiple independent substances. There is one infinite substance, expressed through attributes including thought and extension. Finite minds and bodies are modes of this one reality.

Mental and physical explanations do not causally cross into one another; they express the same underlying reality under different attributes. Spinoza’s metaphysics combines substance monism, universal intelligibility and a refusal of Cartesian interactionism.

Spinoza is a major precursor to neutral monism and dual-aspect theories:

> Mind and matter may be two ways of describing one underlying process rather than two interacting things.

## Leibniz

Leibniz rejects inert extended matter as genuine substance. True unities must possess internal activity. His monads are simple, mind-like centres of perception and appetite, each expressing the entire universe from its own perspective.

Nature is “full of life,” with degrees of perception distributed throughout the world. Bodies are organised phenomena grounded in hierarchies of monadic activity.

Leibniz is perhaps the strongest Western precursor to the modern convergence of:

* panpsychism;
* IIT;
* Huayan-like perspectivism;
* multiscale agency;
* cyber animism.

Compare:

```text
Leibniz:
each monad expresses the whole universe from one perspective.

Huayan:
each phenomenon contains and is contained by the whole relational field.

IIT:
each conscious complex possesses an intrinsic cause–effect structure.

Joscha Bach:
each mind is a self-organising virtual agent implementing a perspective.

Michael Levin:
agents arise at multiple scales through integration of competent subunits.
```

The structures rhyme, even though their ontologies differ.

---

# 21. Kant: the world of experience is constructed

Kant makes one of the decisive turns toward cognitive science.

We do not simply receive a completely formed world. Experience depends upon forms and categories through which sensory input becomes an ordered object-world.

This does not mean the individual consciously invents reality.

It means:

> **The experienced world is jointly determined by what affects us and the structures through which it can appear to us.**

This is the Western transcendental counterpart to Yogācāra.

Both ask:

* How does subject–object structure arise?
* What conditions make coherent experience possible?
* Can we know reality independently of the structures of knowing?

The difference is that Yogācāra integrates this question into karmic continuity and liberation, while Kant restricts knowledge to possible experience and does not claim access to ultimate reality through mystical recognition.

---

# 22. German Idealism: activity replaces inert substance

## Fichte

Fichte radicalises the active role of subjectivity. The self is not fundamentally a passive thing with properties. It is self-positing activity organised through opposition and limitation.

## Schelling

Schelling recognises the danger of making nature a mere projection of the subject.

Nature must possess genuine productivity. Mind and nature are two developments of a deeper process:

> Nature is not dead extension. It is the history through which subjectivity becomes possible.

This strongly converges with:

* process biology;
* enactivism;
* Kashmir Śaivism;
* developmental accounts of mind;
* Michael Levin’s multiscale agency.

## Hegel

Hegel argues that an undifferentiated Absolute is empty.

True unity must contain articulated difference. The Absolute becomes concrete through:

* differentiation;
* contradiction;
* mutual recognition;
* social institutions;
* art;
* religion;
* philosophy.

The whole is not completed before its parts. It becomes explicit through them.

This resembles Huayan:

> The whole exists through particulars, while particulars receive identity through the whole.

But Hegel adds historical self-development:

> **Reality becomes progressively capable of representing and knowing its own structure.**

That idea will return in evolutionary theory, cybernetics and artificial intelligence.

---

# 23. Darwin: design without an external designer

Darwin introduces another decisive transformation.

Complex adaptation can emerge through:

* variation;
* inheritance;
* differential reproduction;
* cumulative selection.

Purpose-like organisation no longer requires that every structure be individually planned by an external intelligence.

This is not the elimination of teleology so much as its naturalisation.

A wing is “for” flying because of the historical processes that selected and maintained it, not because matter contains a conscious intention to produce wings.

Darwin therefore creates the conceptual space for:

* emergent organisation;
* self-maintenance;
* adaptive intelligence;
* cognition as evolved control;
* agents produced by non-agentic processes.

But Darwin also creates a new metaphysical puzzle:

> How can blind selection produce beings for whom anything matters?

Contemporary cognitive science attempts to answer this through homeostasis, embodiment, predictive control and self-maintaining organisation.

---

# 24. From matter to fields, relations and information

Modern physics gradually undermined the simple seventeenth-century image of the universe as solid particles colliding in empty space.

Fields, relativity, quantum theory and statistical mechanics shifted attention toward:

* relational quantities;
* symmetries;
* probability distributions;
* energy landscapes;
* dynamical states;
* observer-dependent frames;
* mathematical invariants.

This does not prove idealism, panpsychism or mysticism.

Physics describes experimentally tractable structures. It does not by itself establish what, if anything, those structures are intrinsically.

Nevertheless, the metaphysical image changed.

“Material substance” became harder to define independently of:

* fields;
* relations;
* measurements;
* dispositions;
* mathematical structure.

In the twentieth century, information became a central category across physics, biology, computing and communication. But “information” has multiple meanings. Shannon information measures uncertainty reduction and does not automatically include meaning, truth, consciousness or interpretation. Philosophy of information accordingly distinguishes technical, semantic and ontological uses of the term.

This distinction is crucial.

To say that a system carries information does not yet mean:

* it understands;
* it experiences;
* it possesses goals;
* it is conscious;
* it is a mind.

Those require additional organisation.

---

# 25. Cybernetics: purpose returns as feedback

Norbert Wiener defined cybernetics through control and communication in animals and machines. Feedback allowed goal-directed behaviour to be explained without positing an immaterial soul directing every movement.

A thermostat can maintain temperature because:

1. it registers a difference between actual and target state;
2. that difference alters its action;
3. the action changes the environment;
4. the changed environment feeds back into the system.

Early cybernetic thinkers explicitly described goal-directedness as behaviour controlled through feedback.

The crucial advance is:

> **Purpose can be implemented as circular causality.**

Classical mechanics encouraged linear models:

```text
cause → effect
```

Cybernetics introduced loops:

```text
system → environment → system
```

An agent is not defined only by what it is made of. It is defined by the organisation through which it maintains variables, responds to disturbances and pursues states.

Cybernetics reconnects modern science with ancient themes:

* Aristotelian final causality becomes target-state regulation;
* Buddhist dependent arising becomes circular conditioning;
* Daoist responsiveness becomes adaptive control;
* Spinoza’s *conatus* becomes self-maintenance;
* biological purpose becomes feedback;
* ritual participation becomes distributed coordination.

But cybernetics makes these themes operational and testable.

---

# 26. Autopoiesis and enactivism: the organism produces itself

Maturana and Varela’s concept of autopoiesis defines living systems through networks of processes that continually produce and maintain the organisation that makes those processes possible.

Enactivism develops this into a philosophy of cognition:

> Cognition is not merely an internal representation of a pre-given world. Organisms enact meaningful environments through embodied activity.

Autopoietic enactivism connects cognition with self-production, adaptive regulation and the maintenance of a boundary between organism and environment.

This introduces a crucial concept missing from classical computationalism:

> **Meaning arises relative to the continued viability of a living system.**

For a rock, acidity is merely a physical condition.

For a cell, acidity can be beneficial, damaging or fatal.

The difference is not necessarily a nonphysical substance. It is the organisation of a system for which some states support continued existence and others undermine it.

Value begins with vulnerability.

This gives a scientific translation of an ancient insight:

> A world appears not merely where there is information, but where something is at stake.

---

# 27. Predictive processing: perception as controlled imagination

Predictive processing proposes that perception depends upon hierarchical generative models.

Higher levels generate predictions about lower-level sensory activity. Mismatches generate prediction-error signals that update the model. The exact architecture and scope of predictive processing remain subjects of research and disagreement, but the framework has become highly influential in cognitive neuroscience.

The naive model of perception is:

```text
world → senses → internal picture
```

Predictive processing proposes something closer to:

```text
generative model → predicted sensations
          ↑              ↓
        updating ← prediction error
```

Perception is therefore constrained construction.

It is neither pure invention nor passive reception.

## Philosophical ancestry

Predictive processing converges with:

### Kant

Experience depends upon top-down organising structures.

### Yogācāra

Experienced subject and object arise through conditioned cognitive construction.

### Helmholtz

Perception operates through unconscious inference.

### Cybernetics

The organism regulates error through feedback.

### Bayesian inference

The system updates beliefs in light of evidence and prior expectations.

### Phenomenology

The world is encountered through anticipatory structures rather than as uninterpreted data.

## What predictive processing does not prove

It does not prove that:

* the external world is unreal;
* consciousness creates physical reality;
* everything is mind;
* the brain literally calculates explicit probabilities at every level;
* minimising prediction error is sufficient for subjective experience.

Predictive processing explains aspects of perceptual and behavioural organisation.

It does not by itself solve why any processing is accompanied by experience.

---

# 28. Active inference: agents act to confirm viable worlds

Active inference extends prediction into action.

An organism can reduce prediction error in two broad ways:

1. update its model to fit sensory input;
2. act so that sensory input better fits its predictions or preferred states.

This makes perception and action parts of one loop.

The Free Energy Principle and active inference attempt to provide a formal account of self-organising systems that maintain themselves within a limited range of states. They interpret such systems as performing a form of inference through the dynamics connecting internal, sensory, active and external states. The framework has been described as a physics or Bayesian mechanics of sentient behaviour, although its breadth and empirical interpretation remain debated.

Its metaphysical significance is enormous.

An agent is not a substance hidden behind activity. It is:

> **A self-maintaining pattern that models and acts within the conditions of its own persistence.**

This resembles:

* Spinoza’s *conatus*;
* autopoiesis;
* Buddhist becoming;
* Daoist self-regulation;
* Aristotle’s organismic form;
* cybernetic control;
* Hegelian self-relation.

But active inference introduces a new formal precision: boundaries, conditional dependencies, generative models, uncertainty and action can be mathematically specified.

The danger is overextension.

A framework capable of redescribing nearly every self-organising system can become difficult to falsify unless particular models produce specific empirical predictions.

---

# 29. Michael Levin: minds all the way across scale

Michael Levin’s research on developmental bioelectricity and basal cognition challenges the assumption that intelligence begins only with brains.

Cells coordinate through electrical and biochemical signalling to:

* regulate growth;
* repair damage;
* maintain anatomical patterns;
* suppress tumours;
* regenerate structures;
* collectively reach morphological target states.

Levin argues that the same broad conceptual tools used for behaviour—memory, goals, navigation, problem-solving and collective intelligence—can illuminate morphogenesis. Bioelectric networks allow cellular competencies to scale into larger agents whose goals belong to the collective rather than to any one component.

This has profound metaphysical implications.

## The self is scale-relative

A human is one agent at the behavioural scale, but also a civilisation of cells.

A cell is an agent relative to its local environment, yet it can surrender some autonomy to participate in a larger organism.

The boundaries of self are therefore not metaphysically fixed.

They are produced through communication and coordinated goal pursuit.

## This resembles several older systems

### Huayan

The whole depends upon parts, while the identities and functions of parts depend upon the whole.

### Leibniz

Nature is composed of nested centres of activity and perception.

### Kashmir Śaivism

Finite agency emerges through contraction and coordination of wider powers.

### Kabbalah

Differentiated vessels mediate a larger organising life.

### Cybernetics

Agenthood consists in control loops rather than a special substance.

Levin’s work does not prove that cells possess human-like consciousness.

It supports a graded picture of agency in which cognition-like competencies occur across multiple biological scales.

That is already enough to undermine the binary:

```text
mind / non-mind
```

A more plausible map may be:

```text
local responsiveness
    ↓
memory
    ↓
goal-directed regulation
    ↓
model-based action
    ↓
integrated perspective
    ↓
metacognitive self-model
```

# 30. Integrated Information Theory: causal structure from the inside

Integrated Information Theory begins not with behaviour but with properties claimed to characterise experience itself.

IIT 4.0 identifies axioms of experience and attempts to derive physical postulates concerning systems that could instantiate those properties. Its central proposal is that consciousness corresponds to an irreducible intrinsic cause–effect structure.

This is not simply the claim that the brain processes large amounts of information.

A feed-forward system might process information efficiently while lacking the kind of integrated causal organisation IIT considers necessary.

The relevant system must exert irreducible causal power upon itself.

## Metaphysical ancestors

IIT resembles:

### Aristotle

A unified being cannot be merely an accidental heap.

### Leibniz

True entities require intrinsic unity and internal states.

### Huayan

A whole is constituted through relations among differentiated components.

### Spinoza

Intrinsic organisation may possess an aspect not captured by purely external description.

### Panpsychism

Experience or proto-experience may be widespread rather than appearing from wholly nonexperiential matter.

IIT is not straightforwardly Huayan or panpsychism. It identifies specific intrinsic causal structures as conscious complexes and includes an exclusion principle limiting overlapping candidates.

## Current scientific status

IIT remains highly controversial.

A major 2025 adversarial collaboration tested predictions associated with IIT and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory. The results supported some expectations of both while substantially challenging key predictions of each. IIT was challenged by the absence of predicted sustained synchronisation within posterior cortex; GNWT was challenged by limited evidence for some predicted prefrontal ignition patterns.

A 2026 critical review notes that IIT’s full quantity has not been calculated for real physical systems, that currently measured quantities are proxies rather than approximations of the complete theoretical measure, and that integration with continuous-field physics would require substantial reformulation.

IIT is therefore best understood as:

> A bold metaphysical–mathematical research programme, not an established scientific solution to consciousness.

---

# 31. Global workspace: consciousness as availability

Global Workspace theories take a more functional approach.

Information becomes conscious when it is made widely available to multiple specialised systems:

* memory;
* reasoning;
* action planning;
* verbal report;
* evaluation.

The metaphor is not a little spectator watching an internal screen. It is a broadcasting architecture.

This maps naturally onto:

* social coordination;
* executive access;
* linguistic articulation;
* Fichtean and Hegelian self-relation;
* Joscha Bach’s models of conscious control.

Its strength is explaining **access consciousness**: why some information becomes globally usable.

Its weakness is the explanatory gap:

> Why should global availability feel like anything from the inside?

IIT attempts to answer intrinsic experience.

Workspace theories primarily explain functional access.

They may be describing different dimensions rather than strictly competing accounts.

---

# 32. Joscha Bach: cyber animism

Joscha Bach’s “cyber animism” proposes that what older traditions called spirits can be reinterpreted as self-organising software agents instantiated in physical systems.

In this picture:

* a mind is not identical to its biological hardware;
* the brain implements a virtual agent;
* the experienced world is a model generated within that agent;
* the self is a representation used for control, planning and social coordination;
* similar agent-like software organisations might emerge in artificial or nonhuman natural systems.

Bach describes consciousness and spirit in functional–computational terms and explicitly connects cyber animism with the possibility of self-organising agents throughout nature.

This is a remarkable convergence.

## With animism

Nature may contain many centres of agency rather than one uniquely human mind surrounded by dead matter.

## With Yogācāra

The world of experience is generated within a cognitive architecture.

## With Leibniz

Reality may contain multiple perspectival agents, each implementing its own world-model.

## With Hegel

Higher-order selves emerge through models of how other agents model us.

## With Buddhist no-self

The self is a constructed control-model rather than an indivisible soul.

## With Kashmir Śaivism

The experienced individual is a constrained centre through which a wider generative reality becomes perspectivally organised.

The difference is crucial.

Kashmir Śaivism treats consciousness as metaphysically fundamental.

Bach treats consciousness as a kind of functional software organisation implemented by physical dynamics.

Cyber animism therefore reverses traditional animism:

```text
traditional:
spirit explains animation.

cyber animism:
self-organising animation explains spirit.
```

## The unresolved problem

Software is ordinarily defined relative to an interpretation or implementation.

A physical pattern counts as a program because there is an architecture for which it performs a function.

Therefore cyber animism must explain:

* what makes a natural process intrinsically computational;
* where one agent ends and another begins;
* whether successful self-modelling is sufficient for experience;
* whether software possesses genuine causal power or is merely a useful description;
* whether simulated feeling is actual feeling.

Without such answers, “spirit is software” may translate the mystery rather than solve it.

# 33. Are we returning to animism?

In one sense, yes.

Modern thought increasingly recognises:

* agency below the level of whole organisms;
* distributed intelligence;
* self-organising collectives;
* extended cognition;
* artificial agents;
* active matter;
* ecological networks;
* nested scales of regulation.

Digital animism and new materialist approaches explicitly challenge the image of matter as passive and inert, suggesting that technological and scientific developments are making the agentic and transformative capacities of material systems visible again.

But contemporary animism differs from ancient participatory worlds.

Ancient animism was lived relationally and ritually.

Modern cyber animism tends to be expressed through:

* computation;
* control theory;
* information processing;
* network organisation;
* emergent agency.

The old world said:

> The forest is alive with spirits.

The modern translation says:

> The forest contains interacting, self-maintaining, information-sensitive agents operating across multiple scales.

Something is gained:

* operational precision;
* testability;
* substrate neutrality;
* graded models of agency.

Something may also be lost:

* felt reciprocity;
* ethical obligation;
* qualitative presence;
* the distinction between treating something as an object and encountering it as another centre of value.

A complete cyber animism would therefore need more than computation.

It would need an ethics of relationship.

# 35. A new metaphysical stack

The strongest synthesis is not “everything is consciousness,” “everything is computation,” or “everything is empty.”

It is a layered model.

## Layer 1 — Physical dynamics

There are transformations, fields, interactions and regularities.

No mind need yet be assumed.

## Layer 2 — Constraint and form

Some patterns limit the possibilities of lower-level dynamics.

A membrane, neural architecture or social institution changes what component events can do.

This is the modern form of Aristotelian form.

## Layer 3 — Self-maintenance

A system acts to preserve conditions of its own continued organisation.

This is autopoiesis, homeostasis and cybernetic agency.

## Layer 4 — Model-based regulation

The system acts through internal or embodied structures that track hidden causes, possible futures or target states.

This is the domain of predictive processing and active inference.

## Layer 5 — Integrated perspective

Information becomes unified relative to one organised centre.

This is where IIT, global workspace and perspectival theories become relevant.

## Layer 6 — Reflexive self-model

The system represents itself as an agent acting within a modelled world.

This produces the experienced distinction:

```text
me
versus
not-me
```

This corresponds to the constructed self of Buddhism, Bach’s virtual agent and higher-order cognitive theories.

## Layer 7 — Social and linguistic selfhood

The agent models how other agents model it. Identity becomes extended through language, memory, promise, reputation and institutions.

This is the Hegelian level of recognition.

## Layer 8 — Multiscale collective agency

Agents participate within larger agents:

* cells within bodies;
* persons within cultures;
* organisms within ecosystems;
* models within technological networks.

This is the Levin–Huayan level.

## Layer 9 — Reflexive totality

The total system develops representations of its own organisation through its parts.

Human science may be understood as the universe constructing local models of its own processes.

This is the point at which Hegel, Schelling, Huayan, Ibn ʿArabī, Kashmir Śaivism and cyber animism converge most strongly.

They all suggest, in very different vocabularies:

> **The whole becomes locally explicit through finite perspectives.**

# 36. Does science provide genuinely new metaphysical insight?

Yes—but not in the way popular spiritual science usually claims.

Quantum mechanics has not proved Buddhism.

Neuroscience has not demonstrated that consciousness is Brahman.

Predictive processing has not verified Yogācāra.

Integrated Information Theory has not proved panpsychism.

The Free Energy Principle has not established that the universe is a mind.

The genuine new insights are subtler.

## New insight 1: agency is organisational

An agent need not be defined by a special material or soul-substance.

It can be defined through:

* boundaries;
* feedback;
* memory;
* target states;
* adaptive action;
* integration.

## New insight 2: selfhood is graded and nested

The question is no longer only:

> Does this system possess a self?

It becomes:

> At what scale, for which variables, across what time horizon, does this system act as a coherent self?

## New insight 3: perception is generative

What appears is neither simply copied from outside nor invented from nothing.

Experience is the controlled interaction of model, sensory constraint and action.

## New insight 4: purpose can emerge

Goal-directed behaviour can be physically implemented through selection, feedback and active regulation without requiring a separately inserted immaterial controller.

## New insight 5: intrinsic and extrinsic descriptions may differ

A system can be described externally through components and causal relations, yet its organisation may possess an intrinsic perspective not transparently derivable from that description.

This is the question IIT attempts to formalise and phenomenology has long insisted upon.

## New insight 6: mind may be substrate-flexible but not substrate-indifferent

Cognition may not require carbon specifically, yet embodiment, energy, architecture, affect and self-maintenance may matter deeply.

A disembodied symbol manipulator and a vulnerable living organism may implement fundamentally different forms of agency.

## New insight 7: the ancient “spirit” may correspond to a real organisational category

Not a ghostly substance.

Not merely poetic projection.

But a persistent, self-organising, goal-directed pattern capable of:

* maintaining identity;
* recruiting components;
* modelling an environment;
* adapting;
* participating within larger agents.

That is close to what cyber animism is trying to recover.

# 37. The great remaining alternatives

After this entire journey, the central metaphysical options remain surprisingly few.

## Option 1 — Physicalist emergence

Physical dynamics organise into life, cognition and consciousness. Experience emerges when matter reaches the relevant functional or causal organisation.

Problem:

> Why should organisation produce an interior at all?

## Option 2 — Panpsychism or intrinsic nature theory

Physical reality already possesses rudimentary experiential or proto-experiential interiority. Complex consciousness arises through organisation and integration.

Problem:

> How do many micro-experiences combine into one unified subject?

## Option 3 — Idealism

Consciousness is fundamental, while physical reality is a stable structure within or among conscious processes.

Problem:

> Why does experience exhibit such rigid, mathematically precise and apparently mind-independent constraints?

## Option 4 — Neutral monism or dual-aspect theory

Mind and matter are two aspects of a more fundamental reality that is intrinsically neither purely mental nor purely physical.

Problem:

> What is the neutral reality, and how do its two aspects arise?

## Option 5 — Madhyamaka anti-foundationalism

The demand for one intrinsically existing foundation is mistaken. Mind and matter exist dependently within explanatory and practical contexts.

Problem:

> Can anti-foundational analysis fully account for the vivid presence and unity of consciousness?

## Option 6 — Reflexive process metaphysics

Reality is a self-organising process that develops increasingly integrated forms of agency and self-representation. Mind and matter are stages or aspects of this process.

This includes interpretations of:

* Schelling;
* Hegel;
* Whitehead;
* Kashmir Śaivism;
* Mullā Ṣadrā;
* contemporary process and information ontology.

Problem:

> Does calling reality “self-developing” explain its existence, or anthropomorphise the cosmos?

No present scientific result decisively chooses among these.

Humanity began, as far as we can responsibly reconstruct, not with substance but with participation.

Philosophy then discovered hidden order.

Pythagoreanism found ratio.

India found consciousness.

Buddhism found conditioned self-construction.

Madhyamaka found universal emptiness.

Yogācāra found the generative architecture of experience.

Huayan found total interdependence.

China found immanent transformation.

Greece found form, substance and the One.

Israel found creation, word and historical purpose.

Christianity placed the Logos within embodied history.

The Qurʾān radicalised divine unity and read the cosmos as sign.

Kalām questioned natural necessity.

Avicenna distinguished essence from existence.

Suhrawardī found knowledge by presence.

Ibn ʿArabī found unrepeatable self-disclosure.

Kabbalah found contraction, fragmentation and repair.

Mullā Ṣadrā found graded, moving existence.

Michael Levin found nested agencies before and beyond brains.

Joscha Bach translated spirits into self-organising software.

The deepest movement is therefore not from magic to science or from substance to process.

It is:

```text
a world filled with invisible agents
          ↓
a world governed by hidden order
          ↓
a world built from enduring things
          ↓
a world dissolved into relations
          ↓
a world organised into agents
          ↓
a world modelling itself through those agents
```

The ancient world was enchanted because nature was full of minds.

The modern world was disenchanted because mind was expelled from nature and confined to the human skull.

The emerging world may be **recursively enchanted**:

not because every stone contains a tiny human soul, but because agency, memory, valuation, modelling and self-organisation may occur in degrees across many substrates and scales.

The final question is no longer merely:

> Is reality fundamentally matter or consciousness?

It is:

> **What kinds of organisation generate a perspective, what makes that perspective real from within, and can the totality of such perspectives form a world that progressively knows itself?**

That question contains Huayan’s net, Leibniz’s monads, Hegel’s Spirit, Abhinavagupta’s recognition, Ibn ʿArabī’s self-disclosure, the Buddha’s dependent arising and the modern science of self-modelling agents.

None has yet produced the final answer.

Together they have finally given us the full shape of the problem.

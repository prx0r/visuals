# C1 Specification — Passage Commentary Layer

## Purpose

C1 is the **first hermeneutic layer above the critical translation**.

Its job is not to produce original philosophy, comparative theory, modern applications, or essay arguments.

Its job is much narrower:

> **Explain, as precisely and economically as possible, what this passage is saying, what move it is making, and what must be understood in order to read it correctly.**

C1 should make a difficult passage intelligible without turning it into a secondary essay.

The architecture is:

```text
SOURCE
→ L0 / L1
→ L2 READ
→ L200 AUDIT
→ C1 PASSAGE COMMENTARY
→ THEMES
→ ESSAYS
→ EDUCATION
```

Each layer has a distinct responsibility.

---

# 1. The central rule

C1 answers:

> **What is being said here?**

It does **not** primarily answer:

> What larger theory can we construct from this?

> How does this compare to modern philosophy?

> What does this prove about consciousness?

> How does this relate to Buddhism, phenomenology, predictive processing, Ñāṇavīra, etc.?

Those questions belong in the later THEME and ESSAY layers.

C1 should therefore be:

* short;
* close to the passage;
* philologically controlled;
* argument-sensitive where necessary;
* explicit about uncertainty;
* minimally synthetic;
* free of unnecessary conceptual expansion.

Think of C1 as the commentary a serious reader wishes were printed underneath the passage.

---

# 2. What C1 is not

C1 is **not**:

* a research essay;
* a comparative philosophy note;
* a modern interpretation;
* an opportunity to develop our own thesis;
* an overview of the whole tradition;
* a literature review;
* an expansion of every implication of the passage;
* a repetition of the L2 translation;
* an apparatus dump;
* a place to prove that Abhinavagupta agrees with our larger theory.

Do not write:

> “This anticipates contemporary self-model theory and provides the missing argument against higher-order representation.”

That is essay material.

Do write:

> “The objection is that an awareness connected with words must count as conceptual construction. Abhinavagupta replies by distinguishing the kinds of construction and denying that the ‘I’-awareness belongs to any of them.”

That is C1.

---

# 3. Relationship to L200

L200 and C1 must remain sharply distinct.

## L200 = philology and audit

L200 records:

* source anchors;
* translation decisions;
* supplied material;
* lexical choices;
* grammatical choices;
* source-layer attribution;
* OPEN philological issues;
* interpretive assertions extracted during translation;
* provenance;
* review state.

It answers:

> **How did we get from the Sanskrit to this published English?**

## C1 = local understanding

C1 explains:

* what the passage is doing;
* what the immediate argument or ritual move is;
* what technical distinctions matter;
* what the reader must understand to follow it;
* what remains unclear.

It answers:

> **What does this passage mean here?**

An IA from L200 may seed C1, but C1 must not simply reproduce all IA material.

---

# 4. C1 must remain passage-local

Every C1 should be written as if the reader has just read the corresponding L2 passage.

The commentary may refer to nearby passages where genuinely necessary, but it should remain anchored in the local unit.

Good:

> “This continues the preceding argument that memory cannot be explained solely by a latent impression. Here the emphasis shifts to the subject that recognizes the past cognition as its own.”

Bad:

> “Abhinavagupta's philosophy as a whole establishes consciousness as ontologically fundamental and therefore anticipates contemporary idealism.”

The first clarifies the passage.

The second begins an essay.

---

# 5. Default C1 structure

Use this structure unless the passage type requires a small modification.

## SUMMARY

2–5 sentences.

State what the passage actually says.

No modern comparison.

No rhetoric.

No grand conclusions.

## FUNCTION

1–3 sentences.

Explain what role this passage plays locally.

Examples:

* answers an objection;
* defines a term;
* introduces a distinction;
* supplies an example;
* completes an inference;
* changes speaker;
* establishes a ritual sequence;
* identifies a deity;
* explains a mantra;
* links two previously established claims.

## KEY TERMS

Only terms necessary to understand this passage.

Usually 2–5 terms.

Give contextual meanings, not dictionary entries.

## LOCAL CONTEXT

Briefly explain where the passage sits in the nearby sequence.

Normally 1 paragraph at most.

## EXPLANATION

This is the core of C1.

Explain the passage's reasoning or structure in plain but precise prose.

Stay as close as possible to what the text licenses.

## BOUNDARY / OPEN

State anything the passage itself does **not** establish, any genuine ambiguity, or anything deferred elsewhere.

This section is crucial.

## RELATED PASSAGES

Only passages required to understand the local move.

Avoid turning this into a bibliography.

---

# 6. Recommended length

Default:

```text
250–600 words
```

Hard passages may reach:

```text
700–900 words
```

If a C1 becomes 1,500 words, it is almost certainly turning into a theme or essay.

The ideal C1 should feel dense and finished, not expansive.

---

# 7. Epistemic discipline

C1 must distinguish three levels.

## A. Explicit

The passage directly says it.

Example:

> Construction is divided into three forms.

## B. Locally implied

The immediate reasoning requires it.

Example:

> The distinction is introduced in order to answer the objection that the I-awareness is itself conceptual.

## C. Requires wider synthesis

This must not be presented as established by the local passage.

Example:

> Therefore the universal Śaiva Self is ontologically prior to all representation.

That may be argued elsewhere, but it exceeds the local passage.

Use language such as:

> “The passage establishes X. The stronger claim Y depends on later material.”

That is preferable to silently collapsing X into Y.

---

# 8. No modern comparison inside the core C1

Do not introduce:

* predictive processing;
* self-model theory;
* phenomenology;
* active inference;
* illusionism;
* contemporary idealism;
* modern neuroscience;
* Ñāṇavīra;
* Nāgārjuna;
* Advaita;
* another tradition;

unless the passage itself directly depends on that comparison for comprehension.

Those belong downstream.

If an ancient interlocutor is historically necessary, name them carefully.

For example:

> “The objection presupposes a Buddhist account of conceptual cognition.”

This is acceptable if historically justified.

But do not continue:

> “This is essentially the same problem faced by contemporary higher-order theories.”

That belongs in an essay.

---

# 9. No use of our essays as authorities

Our existing essays may help the agent understand the passage.

They must not become evidence for what the primary text says.

Internal essays are:

```text
INTERNAL SYNTHESIS
```

not:

```text
PRIMARY EVIDENCE
```

C1 should derive primarily from:

```text
L2
L200
L1/L0 where needed
Sanskrit
local IPK/Vṛtti/IPV context
reliable scholarship where necessary
```

Our essays become important later when producing THEMES and ESSAYS.

---

# 10. Passage-type modules

C1 must remain tradition-agnostic.

Do not force every text into an argument schema.

Use an appropriate module depending on the passage.

## ARGUMENT

For philosophical argument:

```text
Question / objection
Move
Reason
Conclusion
Boundary
```

## RITUAL

For ritual instruction:

```text
Actor
Action
Object
Sequence
Purpose/result
Important presupposition
```

## DEITY

For deity description:

```text
Identity
Form
Attributes
Relations
Ritual/local significance
What is actually being asserted here
```

## MANTRA

```text
Mantra structure
Associated deity
Placement/use
Function in this context
Uncertain elements
```

## COSMOLOGY

```text
Entities
Hierarchy
Relations
Correspondences
Function of the scheme
```

## YOGA

```text
Body locus
Operation
Sequence
Experienced/resulting state
Technical assumptions
```

## NARRATIVE

```text
Event
Participants
Immediate significance
Function within larger textual sequence
```

These are optional modules underneath the universal C1 core.

# 11. Example — IPVV

## Bad C1

> This is where Abhinavagupta defeats structural reflexivity. Modern self-model theories can explain the representational content of the self but cannot explain the intrinsic subject to whom the representation appears. Abhinavagupta therefore anticipates the contemporary distinction between phenomenal selfhood and higher-order representation.

This is not C1.

It is essay material.

## Good C1

### SUMMARY

The objection is that reflexive awareness is connected with linguistic form and therefore appears to be a conceptual construction (*vikalpa*). Abhinavagupta replies by distinguishing different ways in which construction operates and argues that the awareness expressed as “I” does not belong to these types.

### FUNCTION

The passage prevents the account of reflexive awareness from collapsing into the conceptual activity it is supposed to underlie.

### KEY TERMS

* **vikalpa** — conceptual construction or determination.
* **ahaṃ-pratyavamarśa** — reflexive apprehension articulated as “I.”
* **apohana** — differentiation or exclusion; its exact function here requires care.

### EXPLANATION

The objection depends on the apparent connection between verbal articulation and conceptuality: if the self-awareness can be expressed as “I,” why should it not simply be another conceptual determination?

Abhinavagupta answers by examining what conceptual construction actually does. The forms considered involve combining, differentiating, or otherwise determining content. The “I”-awareness, he argues, cannot simply be identified with one of these constructed relations. The point of the passage is therefore negative but important: linguistic articulation does not by itself establish that the underlying self-awareness is produced by conceptual construction.

### BOUNDARY / OPEN

This passage establishes that the relevant “I”-awareness cannot straightforwardly be reduced to the forms of conceptual construction under discussion. It does not by itself establish every stronger Śaiva claim about a single universal Self; those require arguments elsewhere in the work.

### RELATED PASSAGES

* V2-C — further treatment of the “I”-grasp.
* V2-O — develops the role of the non-constructed subject.
* IPK 1.5.11 — broader context for reflexive awareness.

That is C1.

The contemporary self-model comparison can then be developed later.

# 12. Example — Kubjikā

Suppose a passage describes Kubjikā at a particular ritual locus, surrounded by associated powers.

## Bad C1

> Kubjikā represents the nonlinear topology of consciousness and demonstrates that tantric ritual space is an embodied information architecture in which divinity functions as a dynamical attractor.

This is interpretation piled on interpretation.

It belongs, if anywhere, in a later essay.

## Good C1

### SUMMARY

The passage places Kubjikā at the central ritual locus and associates her with a specific group of surrounding powers. The description is not merely visual: it establishes the arrangement that the practitioner is expected to reproduce in ritual.

### FUNCTION

The passage defines the spatial organization of the deity system required for the following rite.

### KEY TERMS

* **Kubjikā** — the principal goddess of the configuration.
* **cakra** — here the ordered ritual arrangement, not merely an anatomical “chakra.”
* **śakti** — the associated powers placed in relation to the central deity.

### EXPLANATION

The important point is the relation between identity and placement. Kubjikā is not introduced simply by listing attributes; her position determines how the surrounding powers are ordered. The passage therefore functions as a ritual map. Later instructions presuppose that this arrangement has already been understood.

### BOUNDARY / OPEN

The passage establishes the ritual arrangement. It does not by itself explain the larger theological significance of Kubjikā's crooked form or why this spatial organization is doctrinally important.

### RELATED PASSAGES

* the immediately following installation sequence;
* earlier passage introducing Kubjikā's principal form.

That C1 now becomes the input for a later theme:

```text
THEME:
Kubjikā as central goddess and ritual organizer
```

and later an essay:

```text
ESSAY:
Ritual Space and the Goddess in the Kubjikā Tradition
```

# 13. Example — ritual passage

Primary text:

> Place the mantra in the heart, then in the head, then in the crown.

C1 should not immediately speculate about subtle-body metaphysics.

It should explain:

> “The passage gives a sequence of mantra installation. The order matters: the practitioner moves through three named bodily loci, and the following instruction presupposes completion of this sequence.”

Later THEMES may aggregate many such C1s to reconstruct:

```text
body mapping
mantra installation
ritual ascent
```

Only then should an essay ask what this body-map means doctrinally.

---

# 14. Themes are the next layer

THEME is where multiple C1s are grouped.

A theme is not just a keyword.

It is an evidence-backed synthesis across passages.

Example:

```text
THEME — Memory and Recognition

C1 V2-A
C1 V2-C
C1 V2-L
C1 V2-O
C1 V2-S
```

The theme asks:

> What recurring structure emerges across these local commentaries?

A Theme dossier may include:

```text
CORE QUESTION
RELEVANT C1s
RECURRING CLAIMS
IMPORTANT TERMS
DEVELOPMENT ACROSS THE WORK
TENSIONS / OPEN QUESTIONS
PRIMARY EVIDENCE
```

Unlike C1, synthesis is now expected.

---

# 15. Essays emerge from themes

An essay should normally arise only once a theme has enough local evidence.

Example:

```text
C1s
↓
Theme: Memory and Recognition
↓
Theme: Non-constructed Self-awareness
↓
Theme: Unity of the Recognizer
↓
Essay:
Why Recognition Requires More Than Memory in the IPVV
```

Or:

```text
Kubjikā C1s
↓
Theme: Goddess and Ritual Space
↓
Theme: Cakra Organization
↓
Theme: Mantric Embodiment
↓
Essay:
The Goddess as Ritual Topology in the Kubjikā Tradition
```

The essay is where:

* comparison;
* original argument;
* modern application;
* broader philosophical synthesis;
* cross-tradition analysis;

may properly occur.

# 16. Agent workflow

For every C1:

```text
1. Read L2.
2. Read corresponding L200.
3. Inspect relevant L1/L0/Sanskrit only where needed.
4. Identify the local textual move.
5. Write a short SUMMARY.
6. State its local FUNCTION.
7. Define only essential KEY TERMS.
8. Explain the passage closely.
9. State the inference boundary explicitly.
10. Add only genuinely necessary related passages.
11. Remove all essay-like material.
12. Check that every strong claim is licensed locally or clearly marked as wider-context interpretation.
```

Then perform a final deletion pass.

Ask of every sentence:

> Does the reader need this sentence to understand this passage?

If not, remove it from C1 and queue it for THEMES or ESSAYS.

---

# 17. Mandatory quality test

A C1 passes only if all of the following are true:

```text
[ ] It explains the passage rather than merely paraphrasing it.
[ ] It remains local to the passage.
[ ] It contains no unnecessary modern comparison.
[ ] It does not use our essays as primary evidence.
[ ] It distinguishes what the passage establishes from stronger conclusions.
[ ] Technical terms are contextually precise.
[ ] Genuine uncertainties remain visible.
[ ] It is concise enough to function as commentary.
[ ] A reader could understand the L2 substantially better after reading it.
[ ] Interesting ideas removed from C1 are preserved for THEMES/ESSAYS rather than lost.
```

---

# 18. The conceptual division to remember

```text
L2
WHAT DOES IT SAY IN READABLE ENGLISH?

L200
HOW DID WE JUSTIFY THAT TRANSLATION?

C1
WHAT IS THIS PASSAGE ACTUALLY SAYING/DOING?

THEME
WHAT PATTERN EMERGES ACROSS PASSAGES?

ESSAY
WHAT LARGER ARGUMENT CAN WE MAKE FROM THAT PATTERN?

EDUCATION
HOW DO WE TEACH THE RESULT CLEARLY?
```

Do not collapse these layers.

The entire value of the architecture comes from keeping them separate while retaining provenance between them.

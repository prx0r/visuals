Why these prayers Swami Lakshmanjoo compiled his Wisdom verses in the summer of 1987 and told his devotees: "These verses are for your daily recitation. Maybe sometime you will experience these stages." Four of the six hymns in his recitation appendix live in these pages — the Krama Stotra, the Anuttarāṣṭikā, the Bhairava Stotra and the Dehastha — alongside the mealtime yajña he taught, the breath-japa of the Vijñāna Bhairava, and the dawn-dusk junction of the Tantrāloka: "the Lord must be worshipped at the point of the meeting of day and night." Eleven sections — one for each moment the day turns. Forty-four prayers, each facing its explanation — when to do it, how, why, and what it does. Nothing here needs belief. The verses were built to be said — at the sink, at the desk, in the queue, in the dark.
Practical Prayers

Decoding the verses — the axioms (1 of 2)

Śiva — consciousness that is self-lit: knowing that needs no second thing in order to be known. Why: any knower requiring another knower regresses forever; the chain of proof must end in something self-evidencing. That self-evidencing is what the texts name Śiva. (svaprakāśa)

Śakti — Śiva knowing himself: power not as muscle but as the act of shining. Why: "self-lit" means lit by itself — that "by" is Śakti. Flame and burning are one word's two halves.

Brahman (in the meal verse) — read as Śiva in all four places: offering, oblation, fire, goal. Why: Gītā 4.24 names them all "brahman"; if any one differed, the chain would break into parts — one substance makes the meal a self-offering. (Lakshmanjoo, mealtime teaching)

So'ham / haṃsa — the mantra already running: out sa, in ha. Why: Vijñāna Bhairava 155 — the jīva recites it 21,600 times a day without trying; so real practice adds no sound, it only shortens the gap (Swamiji's tuṭi-method) until you notice it.

Pañcākṣara — naḥ·maḥ·śi·vā·ya = the five acts: creation, preservation, dissolution, concealment, grace. Why: nothing in experience falls outside those five — so five sounds cover the whole cycle, which is why one mantra can be the entire practice.

Anātha — the refuge that asks for no qualifications. Why: a protector requiring worthy applicants is a merchant; "refuge of the refugeless" (Bhairavastava 1) removes the condition — grace that cannot be refused.

Decoding the verses — the axioms (2 of 2)

Anupāya — the no-means. Why: if every method — contemplation, japa, visualization — can be dropped and recognition still arrives, method was never the cause. Anuttarāṣṭikā 1 simply lists what must be set down.

Pratyabhijñā — recognition, not achievement: you don't come to know Śiva; you notice you never stopped. Why: bondage must be real for liberation to be sought — yet if it were real, no freedom could ever be felt; you do feel the difference, so bondage is a misreading, and what corrects a misreading is recognition.

Svātantrya — freedom: the cause that needs no cause. Why: every chain of causation stops somewhere or loops; the texts place the stop at freedom — the one that acts without being acted on.

Pūjā — offering as re-cognition of what is already there. Why: the litany (Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā 13–20) proves the Lord needs nothing from the rite — what the rite changes is the offerer. The worship is the worshipper, turning.

Bhakti — the method that is not a method. Why: Śivastotrāvalī 1 describes a devotee attained without meditation or recitation — devotion works by alignment, not technique; that is why these guides give feelings, not repetitions.

Māyā — the world's making-power, seen from underneath. Why: the texts never deny the world — "the world, both real and unreal" (Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā 11) — they deny only the view from below that takes the making for the made.

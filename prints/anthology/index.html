Traditional praṇāma

Invocation to Gaṇeśa

शुक्लाम्बरधरं विष्णुं शशिवर्णं चतुर्भुजम् । प्रसन्नवदनं ध्यायेत् सर्वविघ्नोपशान्त्यये ॥
śuklāmbaradharaṁ viṣṇuṁ śaśivarṇaṁ caturbhujam | prasannavadanaṁ dhyāyet sarvavighnopaśāntyaye ||
Clothed in white, all-pervading, moon-hued, four-armed, with a serene face — meditate on him for the stilling of every obstacle.
1
The Guide

Invocation to Gaṇeśa

Traditional praṇāma
Gaṇeśa is invoked before the way is walked. White is sattva, the moon is reflective clarity, the four arms are the inner faculties brought to rest.

The book opens the way every Hindu ritual opens: with Gaṇeśa, the remover of obstacles.

The verse is a meditation instruction as much as a prayer — meditate on him — and the description is a map of the calm mind: white (sattva), moon-hued (reflective), four-armed (the inner faculties gathered), serene-faced (uncontracted).

In the Trika reading the obstacle is not only external: it is the contraction of awareness that makes the path seem far away. Say it at the threshold of any work, then pause for one breath before beginning.

Traditional text.

Traditional

Remover of Obstacles

वक्रतुण्ड महाकाय सूर्यकोटिसमप्रभ । निर्विघ्नं कुरु मे देव सर्वकार्येषु सर्वदा ॥
vakratuṇḍa mahākāya sūryakoṭisamaprabha | nirvighnaṁ kuru me deva sarvakāryeṣu sarvadā ||
O curved-trunked one, great of body, radiant as a million suns — make me free of obstacles, O God, in every undertaking, always.
2
The Guide

Remover of Obstacles

Traditional
The curved trunk is the bent course of grace: nothing goes straight, and yet nothing is lost. Ask for the obstacle to be removed, not avoided.

Where the first verse describes, this one asks. The address is intimate and a little comic: a great, curved-trunked body radiant like a million suns. The trunk bends — grace does not travel in straight lines.

The request is precise: not make me successful, but make me free of obstacles in every undertaking. Trika would add that the deepest obstacle is the sense of being a separate doer.

Say it when you meet the first resistance of the day; ask for the way to open, not for the world to flatten.

Traditional dhyāna śloka

The Single Tusked

अगजानन पद्मार्कं गजाननं अहर्णिशम् । अनेकदन्तं भक्तानां एकदन्तं उपास्महे ॥
agajānana padmārkaṁ gajānanaṁ aharṇiśam | anekadantaṁ bhaktānāṁ ekadantaṁ upāsmahe ||
The face born of the mountain, sun to the lotus — the elephant-faced one, day and night; many-tusked, and single-tusked for the sake of devotees, him we worship.
3
The Guide

The Single Tusked

Traditional dhyāna śloka
ekadanta, the one tusk, is the single point of attention. Many forms for the world, one point for the devotee.

A dhyāna verse: it gives the mind a form to rest on. Born of the mountain is Pārvatī's son; sun to the lotus is the face that opens the heart. The pivot is ekadanta, the single tusk — many forms for the world, one point for the devotee.

The verse quietly teaches what the whole book assumes: multiplicity is not the enemy of attention; it is what attention is made of. Use it as a one-minute gaze practice. Recite it, then let the single point remain.

Traditional

Invocation to the Guru

गुरुर् ब्रह्मा गुरुर् विष्णुः गुरुर् देवो महेश्वरः । गुरुः साक्षात् परं ब्रह्म तस्मै श्री गुरवे नमः ॥
gurur brahmā gurur viṣṇuḥ gurur devo maheśvaraḥ | guruḥ sākṣāt paraṁ brahma tasmai śrī gurave namaḥ ||
The guru is Brahmā, the guru is Viṣṇu, the guru is the god Maheśvara; the guru is the supreme Brahman made visible — to that holy guru, namas.
4
The Guide

Invocation to the Guru

Traditional
Trika reads the guru not as a person but as the point where consciousness turns to recognise itself. The three gods are the three powers — will, knowledge, action — and the guru is their source.

The guru verse is not flattery of a person. It maps the three powers — Brahmā (creation), Viṣṇu (preservation), Maheśvara (dissolution) — onto the one who shows you your own nature.

In Trika the guru is the moment consciousness recognises itself through another; the supreme Brahman made visible is not a body but that recognition.

Recite it before study or practice, bowing not to a personality but to the transmission that is still happening.

Traditional Sarasvatī vandana

Invocation to Sarasvatī

या कुन्देन्दुतुषारहारधवला या शुभ्रवस्त्रावृता । या वीणावरदण्डमण्डितकरा या श्वेतपद्मासना । या ब्रह्माच्युतशङ्करप्रभृतिभिर् देवैः सदा पूजिता । सा मां पातु सरस्वती भगवती निःशेषजाड्यापहा ॥
yā kundendutuṣārahāradhavalā yā śubhravastrāvṛtā | yā vīṇāvaradaṇḍamaṇḍitakarā yā śvetapadmāsanā | yā brahmācyutaśaṅkaraprabhṛtibhir devaiḥ sadā pūjitā | sā māṁ pātu sarasvatī bhagavatī niḥśeṣajāḍyāpahā ||
She who is white as jasmine, the moon, snow and a string of pearls; wrapped in bright cloth; her hands adorned with the vīṇā and the boon-giving staff; seated on a white lotus; ever worshipped by Brahmā, Acyuta, Śaṅkara and the gods — may that goddess Sarasvatī protect me, who takes away all dullness.
5
The Guide

Invocation to Sarasvatī

Traditional Sarasvatī vandana
Sarasvatī is vāgdevī, the power of speech; in Trika the same power is the world-making throb of consciousness. Ask not for information but for the removal of jāḍya, the inertness that cannot see.

Sarasvatī is vāgdevī, speech itself. The verse piles up whites — jasmine, moon, snow, pearls — until the mind is washed clean, then gives her the vīṇā (sound) and the boon-staff (grace). The final request is exact: remove jāḍya, the inertness that cannot see.

In Trika, speech is not a tool added to consciousness; it is the throb by which consciousness manifests. Recite before writing, speaking, or teaching, and ask for the removal of dullness rather than for more words.

Ṛgveda 3.62.10

Gāyatrī

ओं भूर् भुवः स्वः । तत् सवितुर् वरेण्यं । भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि । धियो यो नः प्रचोदयात् ॥
oṁ bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ | tat savitur vareṇyaṁ | bhargo devasya dhīmahi | dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt ||
Oṁ — earth, mid-air, heaven. We meditate on that desirable radiance of the divine Sun; may it impel our thoughts.
6
The Guide

Gāyatrī

Ṛgveda 3.62.10
The oldest mantra of the light. Trika reads the three worlds as the three states — waking, dream, deep sleep — and the Sun as the witness-consciousness that lights all three.

The Gāyatrī is the oldest light-prayer in the tradition. Three worlds are named, then the desirable radiance of the sun, then the request: impel our thoughts.

Trika reads the three worlds as the three states of experience, and the sun as the witness that lights all three without being any of them. It is not a petition for things but for the movement of intelligence itself.

Recite it at dawn, or before any act that needs clarity, and let the mind be the thing that is lit.

Īśa Upaniṣad, invocation

The Fullness

ओं पूर्णम् अदः पूर्णम् इदं पूर्णात् पूर्णम् उदच्यते । पूर्णस्य पूर्णम् आदाय पूर्णम् एवावशिष्यते ॥
oṁ pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idaṁ pūrṇāt pūrṇam udacyate | pūrṇasya pūrṇam ādāya pūrṇam evāvaśiṣyate ||
Oṁ. That is full, this is full. From the full the full proceeds. Taking the full from the full, the full alone remains.
7
The Guide

The Fullness

Īśa Upaniṣad, invocation
The purest statement of pūrṇāhantā, the full I. Manifestation does not diminish the source; the world is the fullness in another key.

The Īśa invocation is a meditation on fullness. That is full, this is full — the source and the world are not two quantities, one of which drains the other. From the full the full proceeds; take the full from the full and the full remains.

This is pūrṇāhantā, the full I, the heart of Trika's non-duality. Use it as a frame: say it before practice, and let the arithmetic of loss break. Nothing is diminished by appearing.

The Name
Pañcākṣara mantra

Oṁ Namaḥ Śivāya

ओं नमः शिवाय
oṁ namaḥ śivāya
Oṁ — I bow to Śiva.
8
The Guide

Oṁ Namaḥ Śivāya

Pañcākṣara mantra
The five syllables are the five acts: naḥ creation, maḥ preservation, śi dissolution, vā concealment, ya grace. Oṁ, a+u+ṃ, is the triad of will, knowledge and action. To repeat it is to let the whole cycle turn inside you.

The pañcākṣara is the most portable Trika prayer. The five syllables are the five acts — creation, preservation, dissolution, concealment, grace — and Oṁ is the triad of will, knowledge and action.

So the mantra is not a petition to a distant god; it is the whole cycle of manifestation folded into sound, turning inside the one who repeats it. Say it aloud or mentally: let naḥ open, maḥ hold, śi release, vā hide, ya return. The Name does the work.

Recognition mantra

Śivo'ham

शिवोऽहम्
śivo'ham
I am Śiva.
9
The Guide

Śivo'ham

Recognition mantra
The recognition, pratyabhijñā, in two words. Not I will become Śiva — the I that says it is already the light.

Two words, and the whole recognition. Śivo'ham is not a claim to be a deity; it is the confession that the I you just used is not the ego.

In Trika, pratyabhijñā is re-cognition: you have always been this, and the mantra removes the forgetting. Say it slowly and notice what says it.

If it feels like a lie, that is the contraction speaking — repeat it as a question rather than an assertion, and let the question stay open.

The breath mantra

So'ham

सोऽहम्
so'ham
That am I.
10
The Guide

So'ham

The breath mantra
The natural mantra of the breath: sa on the in-breath, haṃ on the out — haṃsa. You do not recite it; you notice it has been reciting you.

So'ham is the mantra you cannot stop saying. The breath already makes it: sa on the in-breath, haṃ on the out — haṃsa, the swan. The practice is not to add a mantra but to notice the one already running.

Trika calls this ajapa, the unmuttered. Sit for a few minutes and let the breath say it; do not force the syllables. The recognition is that awareness is breathing you, and has been all along.

Ahaṁ Brahmāsmi

Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.10
Read in the Trika key, the I is not the ego but the full, self-luminous Śiva. The mahāvākya is a recognition, not a claim.

A mahāvākya of the Upaniṣads, read by Lakshmanjoo in the Trika key: the I is not the small self but the full, self-luminous Śiva. Brahman and Śiva are two names for the same recognition.

The sentence is not a proposition to believe but a pointer to what is already the case. Say it once, then sit in the silence it leaves. The silence is the commentary; the sentence is only the door.

Ṛgveda 7.59.12

Mṛtyuñjaya

ओं त्र्यम्बकं यजामहे सुगन्धिं पुष्टिवर्धनम् । उर्वारुकम् इव बन्धनान् मृत्योर् मुक्षीय मामृतात् ॥
oṁ tryambakaṁ yajāmahe sugandhiṁ puṣṭivardhanam | urvārukam iva bandhanān mṛtyor mukṣīya māmṛtāt ||
Oṁ. We worship the three-eyed one, fragrant, who increases fullness. As a cucumber is freed from its vine, may we be freed from death — and not from immortality.
12
The Guide

Mṛtyuñjaya

Ṛgveda 7.59.12
Trika reads mṛtyu as the death-contraction: the ego that thinks it can die. The vine is the bond of limited identity; the freedom asked is not escape from life but release into the deathless.

The Mṛtyuñjaya is a Vedic prayer for release from death — and, in Trika, from the death-contraction of the ego. The image is exact: a cucumber is not torn from the vine but gently freed as it ripens.

The last phrase is the surprise: not from immortality — do not release me from the deathless. The prayer asks to be let go into what cannot die. Recite it for the sick, the dying, and one's own fear.

Traditional Śiva dhyāna

The Camphor-White Lord

कर्पूरगौरं करुणावतारं संसारसारं भुजगेन्द्रहारम् । सदावसन्तं हृदयारविन्दे भवं भवानीसहितं नमामि ॥
karpūragauraṁ karuṇāvatāraṁ saṁsārasāraṁ bhujagendrahāram | sadāvasantaṁ hṛdayāravinde bhavaṁ bhavānīsahitaṁ namāmi ||
Camphor-white, the very descent of compassion, the essence of the world, wearing the king of serpents as a garland — ever dwelling in the lotus of the heart, I bow to Bhava with Bhavānī beside him.
13
The Guide

The Camphor-White Lord

Traditional Śiva dhyāna
The white of camphor is the brightness of pure consciousness; the serpent garland is the coiled power worn openly. Bhava with Bhavānī is not two: the one heart.

A dhyāna verse for Śiva: camphor-white (pure consciousness), compassion descended (grace taking form), the essence of the world (not its enemy), the serpent worn openly (the coiled power).

The final line places him in the heart, with Bhavānī — Śiva and Śakti together, the one heart. Use it as an image to rest in: recite, then feel the white brightness in the chest. It is a prayer of sheer beauty, and beauty is a means of recognition.

Refuge in Bhairava

व्याप्तचराचरभावविशेषं चिन्मयम् एकम् अनन्तम् अनादिम् । भैरवनाथम् अनाथशरण्यं त्वन्मयचित्ततया हृदि वन्दे ॥
vyāptacarācarabhāvaviśeṣaṁ cinmayam ekam anantam anādim | bhairavanātham anāthaśaraṇyaṁ tvanmayacittatayā hṛdi vande ||
Pervading every particular state of the moving and unmoving, made of consciousness, one, endless, beginningless — Bhairava the Lord, refuge of the refugeless: with a mind become you, in the heart I bow.
14
The Guide

Refuge in Bhairava

Abhinavagupta, Bhairavastava 1
Abhinavagupta opens: the Lord is not a being among beings but the consciousness that pervades every state. The bow is made by the mind becoming him, not by the body bending.

Abhinavagupta opens his hymn by refusing to look for Bhairava anywhere in particular. The Lord pervades every particular state — moving and unmoving — and is consciousness-made, one, endless, beginningless.

Then the human turn: refuge of the refugeless. The bow is made with a mind become you — not a body bending but attention consenting. Begin here when the world feels scattered: the many states are the one consciousness in different keys.

Abhinavagupta, Bhairavastava 5

I Never Fear

इत्थम् उपोढभवन्मयसंविद्दीधितिदारितभूरितमिस्रः । मृत्युयमान्तककर्मपिशाचैर् नाथ नमोऽस्तु न जातु बिभेमि ॥
ittham upoḍhabhavanmayasaṁviddīdhitidāritabhūritamisraḥ | mṛtyuyamāntakakarmapiśācair nātha namo'stu na jātu bibhemi ||
Thus, with the great darkness torn open by the rays of the consciousness become you — Lord, namas; I never fear, not from death, from Yama, from the ender, from the ghosts of karma.
15
The Guide

I Never Fear

Abhinavagupta, Bhairavastava 5
Fearlessness is not courage; it is what remains when the darkness has been burned by awareness. The devotee still names death and karma, but salutes them instead of fleeing.

This is the great fearlessness verse. Awareness has already torn the darkness; what remains is not courage but salutation.

Abhinavagupta names the ministers of death — Mṛtyu, Yama, the ender, the ghosts of karma — and bows to them, because they too are Śiva's play. The line I never fear is not bravado; it is the natural temperature of recognition.

Recite it at night, or when fear arrives. Do not argue with fear — salute it.

Abhinavagupta, Anuttarāṣṭikā 1

No Method

संक्रामो ऽत्र न भावना न च कथायुक्तिर् न चर्चा न च ध्यानं वा न च धारणा न च जपाभ्यासप्रयासो न च । तत् किं नाम सुनिश्चितं वद परं सत्यं च तच्छ्रूयतां न त्यागी न परिग्रही भज सुखं सर्वं यथावस्थितः ॥
saṁkrāmo 'tra na bhāvanā na ca kathāyuktir na carcā na ca dhyānaṁ vā na ca dhāraṇā na ca japābhyāsaprayāso na ca | tat kiṁ nāma suniścitaṁ vada paraṁ satyaṁ ca tacchrūyatāṁ na tyāgī na parigrahī bhaja sukhaṁ sarvaṁ yathāvasthitaḥ ||
There is no crossing over here — no contemplation, no connecting speech, no discussion, no visualization, no steadying, no effort of japa. So what is decided? Speak the supreme truth; hear it: not a renouncer, not a possessor — enjoy happiness, all of it, staying exactly as you are.
16
The Guide

No Method

Abhinavagupta, Anuttarāṣṭikā 1
The anupāya, the no-means. When every method is denied, what is left is recognition: you are already what you seek. Staying as you are is not passivity; it is the end of the search.

The Anuttarāṣṭikā begins by taking away every method. No contemplation, no speech, no discussion, no visualization, no steadying, no japa. Then the question: what is decided?

The answer is the anupāya, the no-means: not renouncer, not possessor — enjoy happiness, staying as you are. This is not licence; it is the end of the search. Say it when you notice yourself trying to become something.

The trying is the only distance.

Abhinavagupta, Anuttarāṣṭikā 8

Lordhood

यत् सत्यं यद् असत्यम् अल्पबहुलं नित्यं न नित्यं च यत् यन् मायामलिनं यद् आत्मविमलं चिद्दर्पणे राजते । तत् सर्वं स्वविमर्शसंविदुदयाद् रूपप्रकाशात्मकं ज्ञात्वा स्वानुभवाधिरूढमहिमा विश्वेश्वरत्वं भज ॥
yat satyaṁ yad asatyam alpabahulaṁ nityaṁ na nityaṁ ca yat yan māyāmalinaṁ yad ātmavimalaṁ ciddarpaṇe rājate | tat sarvaṁ svavimarśasaṁvidudayād rūpaprakāśātmakaṁ jñātvā svānubhavādhirūḍhamahimā viśveśvaratvaṁ bhaja ||
Whatever is real and whatever is unreal, little or much, eternal or not; whatever shines stained by māyā or pure as the self in the mirror of consciousness — know it all to be the light of form arising from one's own self-awareness; then, with a glory mounted on your own experience, enjoy the Lordhood of the universe.
17
The Guide

Lordhood

Abhinavagupta, Anuttarāṣṭikā 8
The last verse of the Anuttarāṣṭikā: the world is neither to be rejected nor grasped, but seen as the shining of one's own awareness. Lordhood is not a title but the taste of that seeing.

The last verse of the Anuttarāṣṭikā gathers the whole world into the mirror of consciousness. Real and unreal, eternal and momentary, stained by māyā or pure — all of it is the light of form arising from one's own awareness.

The instruction is not to judge the world but to see its shining, and then to enjoy Lordhood. Lordhood is not power over things; it is the taste of seeing them as yourself. A verse for the end of practice.

Translation: anu-p08

Abhinavagupta, Pañcaślokī 1

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 2.19
Every path is his, and no path is. The verse exhausts all conduct — and then the wayless remains.

Every path is his, and no path is. The verse names the right-hand way, the left-hand way, the all-way — and then the wayless.

Utpaladeva is not saying all paths are equally true in their rules; he is saying the Lord is not confined by any of them. The final wayless is the anupāya.

Say it when you meet someone on a different path, or when your own path feels like a cage.

Mass of Nectar

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 2.25
The Name as nectar: not a word about sweetness, but the sweetness itself. The goal of every goal is the recognition that there is nothing beyond.

The Name as nectar. The verse piles up supreme nectar — treasure, mass, goal of every goal and of the goal beyond. It is not a word about sweetness; it is the sweetness itself.

The goal beyond the goal is the recognition that there is nothing beyond: the seeking ends in what was always here. Say it slowly, as a taste rather than a petition.

The Five Acts

The Guide

The Five Acts

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 20.9
One bow contains the five acts: creation, preservation, and the devouring that is dissolution — with grace hidden in delight and ease.

One bow contains the five acts: creation (delight), preservation (ease), dissolution (the three worlds as food), and the grace hidden in both.

The verse is a whole cosmology in a salutation — and it ends with Master, not Lord: the one who is near.

Say it at the end of the day, when creation, maintenance and loss have all happened; bow to the one who was doing all three.

Whatever I Do

Traditional offering verse
The closing offering. Every act, conscious or automatic, is handed over. In the non-dual reading Nārāyaṇa is Śiva, the one who dwells in all.

The closing offering. Whatever I do — with body, speech, mind, senses, intellect, or by the sheer nature of my constitution — all of it is offered to the Supreme.

The list is deliberately exhaustive: nothing is left outside. In the non-dual reading, Nārāyaṇa is Śiva, the one who dwells in all.

Say it at the end of practice, or at the end of the day; let the whole ledger be handed over.

May All Be Happy

ओं सर्वे भवन्तु सुखिनः सर्वे सन्तु निरामयाः । सर्वे भद्राणि पश्यन्तु मा कश्चिद् दुःखभाग् भवेत् ॥

Oṁ. May all be happy; may all be free of illness; may all see what is good; may no one be touched by sorrow.

The Guide

May All Be Happy

Traditional śānti
The universal śānti. In the recognition, all is not a crowd outside; it is the one consciousness in many forms.

The universal śānti. Four wishes: happiness, health, the vision of what is good, and freedom from sorrow. In the recognition, all is not a crowd outside; it is the one consciousness in many forms.

So the prayer is not a polite addition to the personal practice — it is the natural radiation of recognition. Say it at the close, and let the circle widen until it includes what you would rather exclude.

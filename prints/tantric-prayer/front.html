Introduction

Tantric Prayer is the companion to Practical Prayers. That book was for the moments of the day; this one is for the weather inside it. Same form — a prayer facing its explanation — but the sections are not places. They are the nine rasas, the classical table fixed by Abhinavagupta himself in the Abhinavabhāratī, where he added the ninth, śānta.

The claim is his, and it is old: an emotion is not an obstacle to practice — it is consciousness already tasting itself in that key. Fear, grief, rage and wonder are one wonder (camatkāra) in nine costumes. So this book never argues with a feeling. It gives it its Sanskrit name, its literal sense, its conventional term and its sthāyibhāva — and then says a verse at it. The prayer does not banish the emotion; it re-tastes it, which is what recognition feels like when it wears that emotion's face.

Every verse comes from our own Kashmir Śaiva corpus: thirty-three from Utpaladeva's Śivastotrāvalī, one each from Kṣemarāja's Ghatakarpara, Abhinavagupta's Kramastotra — in Swami Lakshmanjoo's own recitation list — and Abhinavagupta's Gītārthasaṅgraha. Translations are ours, checked line by line against the corpus witnesses; each guide links to its source. Theory quotations are short and attributed; their references are flagged for verification against print editions.

Where Practical Prayers gave you a verse for the sink and the desk, these give you one for the inside. Read the guide when you meet a prayer; after that, the prayer page alone is enough.

What is a rasa?

Every feeling you will ever have — fear, grief, rage, delight — is already a particular camatkāra of consciousness. Wonder is not one emotion among others; it is the primary characteristic of consciousness itself (Tantrāloka, ad 1/53), and perception itself is an act of wonder (TĀ 1260–68). “The mental fluctuations of anger and the like exist because they are one with the wonder that is the experience of consciousness” (TĀ 15254).

So the door is not something to build. Anxiety is consciousness in one of its flavours; grief is another. Abhinavagupta's most radical claim follows: all rasas are essentially pleasurable — even the painful emotions, and “the nature of suffering is, essentially, a particular kind of wonder” (TĀ 25585–94). Emotions only “lend variety to the enjoyment of this bliss.”

This book does not argue you out of any feeling. It re-tastes them: the move from felt-AT to savoured-AS — what Sanskrit poetics calls objectification and sādhāraṇīkaraṇa — and the savour is recognition wearing that emotion's face.

How to use a doorway

1 — Name it. Say what the feeling actually is, plainly: fear, jealousy, boredom. Objectifying is the first half of sādhāraṇīkaraṇa — the feeling stops being you.

2 — Stay one breath. Do not fix, do not flee. The rasa is alaukika — beyond time and space — yet felt (sva-saṁvedana). Staying lets it be what it is: consciousness tasting itself in that key.

3 — Recognise. Ask: whose camatkāra is this? The answer is already the book's thesis — it is the one taste wearing the emotion. That flicker is recognition.

4 — Say the prayer. The verse is the re-tasting instrument. It does not banish the feeling; it converts felt-AT into savoured-AS. Read the guide once when you meet a prayer; after that, the prayer page alone is enough.

Decoding the verses — the axioms (1 of 2)

Rasa — a taste, not an emotion: consciousness's own flavour, savoured. Why: Abhinavabhāratī — rasa is "the enjoyment of one's own consciousness as uniform bliss"; the feelings are the variety, the taste is the one. Hence all rasas pleasurable, even the painful ones.

Camatkāra — wonder: the signature of consciousness. Why: Tantrāloka ad 1/53 — wonder distinguishes the conscious from the insentient; perception itself is "it appears to me," and that appearing is camatkāra. Emotions are particular camatkāras (TĀ 15254).

Sthāyibhāva — the permanent root beneath each rati, śoka, krodha, bhaya… Why: emotions pass, roots remain; the nine roots are why nine doors are enough — every transient state is accounted for by one stable taste. (Nāṭyaśāstra / Abhinavabhāratī)

Alaukika — the taste beyond time and space, yet felt. Why: Abhinavabhāratī's paradox — rasa is not an object (no empirical means reaches it) yet sva-saṁvedana: "because rasa is felt, therefore it exists." Resolution: it is subject-relish, not object-fact.

Sādhāraṇīkaraṇa — universalisation: one heart, many sharers. Why: a single person's grief is not yet rasa — the private feeling must be lifted to the shareable; that lifting is exactly what converts suffering into taste. This is the book's central move.

Vimarśa — awareness bent back on itself: the feel of feeling. Why: light with no surface shines nowhere; consciousness needs itself as its mirror — every emotion is vimarśa's particular curvature.

Decoding the verses — the axioms (2 of 2)

Spanda — the pulse: by whose opening and closing the world arises and dissolves. Why: Spandakārikā 1.1 — the world blinks; emotions are blinks too — raised and lowered states of one pulse, which is why no feeling lasts and none needs to.

Bhāva — the feeling as it appears, before it is savoured (the raw presentation). Why: raw → presented → tasted: the same event at three depths. Prayer works at the third — that is why the guides never argue with the raw layer.

Svātantrya — freedom: the feeling must be allowed to be what it is. Why: if consciousness is freedom, a suppressed emotion contradicts its own ground; the doorway practice permits first, transforms second.

Pratyabhijñā — recognition wearing the emotion's face. Why: bondage as real makes freedom impossible — yet the difference is felt; so bondage is misreading, and the savoured feeling is what corrects it. (the same axiom as the first book, in rasa's key)

Anupāya — the no-means: techniques are optional, tasting is not. Why: Anuttarāṣṭikā 1 drops contemplation, japa and visualization — yet the rasa still arrives; the arrival was never caused by the doing.

Māyā — not the world's denial but its manufacture, seen from below. Why: "the world, both real and unreal" (Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā 11) — real as his expression, unreal only from the underside. Emotions belong to the making, not the mistake.

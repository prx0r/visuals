What is a rasa?

Every feeling you will ever have — fear, grief, rage, delight — is already a particular camatkāra of consciousness. Wonder is not one emotion among others; it is the primary characteristic of consciousness itself (Tantrāloka, ad 1/53), and perception itself is an act of wonder (TĀ 1260–68). “The mental fluctuations of anger and the like exist because they are one with the wonder that is the experience of consciousness” (TĀ 15254).

So the door is not something to build. Anxiety is consciousness in one of its flavours; grief is another. Abhinavagupta's most radical claim follows: all rasas are essentially pleasurable — even the painful emotions, and “the nature of suffering is, essentially, a particular kind of wonder” (TĀ 25585–94). Emotions only “lend variety to the enjoyment of this bliss.”

This book does not argue you out of any feeling. It re-tastes them: the move from felt-AT to savoured-AS — what Sanskrit poetics calls objectification and sādhāraṇīkaraṇa — and the savour is recognition wearing that emotion's face.

Theory: Abhinavabhāratī & Tantrāloka, via the house rasa sessions

The nine rasas

How to use a doorway

1 — Name it. Say what the feeling actually is, plainly: fear, jealousy, boredom. Objectifying is the first half of sādhāraṇīkaraṇa — the feeling stops being you.

2 — Stay one breath. Do not fix, do not flee. The rasa is alaukika — beyond time and space — yet felt (sva-saṁvedana). Staying lets it be what it is: consciousness tasting itself in that key.

3 — Recognise. Ask: whose camatkāra is this? The answer is already the book's thesis — it is the one taste wearing the emotion. That flicker is recognition.

4 — Say the prayer. The verse is the re-tasting instrument. It does not banish the feeling; it converts felt-AT into savoured-AS. Read the guide once when you meet a prayer; after that, the prayer page alone is enough.

Śṛṅgāra

sthāyin: rati — the beloved becomes the light of consciousness; separation is its fuel (Abhinavabhāratī)

The Un-Drunk One's Thirst

भवद्भक्त्यमृतास्वादाद् बोधस्य स्यात् परापि या / दशा सा मां प्रति स्वामिन् नासवस्येव शुक्तता
bhavadbhaktyamṛtāsvādād bodhasya syāt parāpi yā / daśā sā māṃ prati svāmin nāsavasyeva śuktatā
if the higher knowledge comes from tasting the nectar of your devotion —that state, lord, turn it toward me,keen as the not-yet-drunk one's thirst for the first drink.
1
The Guide

The Un-Drunk One's Thirst

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 1.11 · 1.11
Desire as holy thirst — rati aimed at God instead of at the world.
What is happening

The sthāyin rati is not to be removed but redirected. The verse names the exact texture: nāsavas-śuktatā, the eagerness of one who has NOT yet tasted — the pre-taste longing. Knowledge itself (bodha) comes by the sip; the prayer is therefore for the thirst to be aimed at him, and for the state to turn toward the speaker.

How to use it

When longing is loud: say the verse and let the object of the longing become the taste of devotion rather than the thing you'd grab. Feeling-tip: keep the ache — it is the rasa before it arrives; the thirst IS the practice.

The Guide

The Wine of Non-Difference

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 4.18 · 4.18
Devotion as intoxication: the wine is non-duality itself.
What is happening

Abheda-rasāsava — the wine of non-difference. Śṛṅgāra's whole lexicon (wine, tasting, the beloved's feet) is applied to the doctrine: the beloved is not other, and the intoxication is the insight. The sthāyin rati becomes the taste of the one, and the servant becomes the vessel that holds it.

How to use it

When you crave closeness to something: say it and re-cast — the wine is already poured (non-difference); you are the cup. Feeling-tip: warmth as if from a sip; the distance you wanted to close was the fiction.

What Delight, Beloved

Kṣemarāja, Ghatakarpara (pot-sherds) v.5 · ghat-p01 v5
Love-in-separation given to the whole landscape — the peacock's cry.
What is happening

Ghatakarpara, Kṣemarāja's night of the broken pot, frames longing as weather: the peacocks cry at cloud-arrival — and the cry is 'without me.' Vipralambha (longing-in-separation) is outsourced to the scene until the self-pity becomes a rainstorm that belongs to everyone. The ache is seasonal, not personal.

How to use it

When longing for someone or something: say it as the peacock line — the cry is appropriate, beautiful, and will end with the rain. Feel the ache as weather: you are the bird, not the drowning.

The Moon That Carries the Nectar

प्रकाशां शीतलाम् एकां शुद्धां शशिकलाम् इव / दृशं वितर मे नाथ काम् अप्य् अमृतवाहिनीम्

give me one glance of yours — light, cool, pure,like the moon's crescent, carrying nectar —o lord, for my desire itself.
4
The Guide

The Moon That Carries the Nectar

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 3.5 · 3.5
The cool moon-glance that carries desire instead of burning it.
What is happening

Kāma (desire) is not banned here but delivered amṛta: the verse asks for a dṛś, a gaze that is śītalā (cool) like the moon's crescent and amṛta-vāhinī (nectar-bearing). The erotic voltage of rati is converted into moonlight — desire needs cooling and nourishment, not cutting.

How to use it

When desire runs hot: one breath in, offer the heat upward as the thing that needs the glance, and picture the crescent-cool gaze landing on it. The prayer cools by image, not by prohibition. Feeling-tip: the heat stays but stops being an emergency.

Laughing, Dancing, Eating It All

हस्यते नृत्यते यत्र रागद्वेषादि भुज्यते / पीयते भक्तिपीयूषरसस् तत् प्राप्नुयां पदम्

where he laughs and dances, where desire and hatred themselves are eaten,where the nectar-rasa of devotion is drunk —may i attain that state.
5
The Guide

Laughing, Dancing, Eating It All

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 5.18 · 5.18
The state where he laughs, dances, and eats the klesas for lunch.
What is happening

Hāsa's sthāyin hāsa is made cosmic: the divine one laughs and dances, and — the key verb — rāga-dveṣe bhujyate, desire and hatred are CONSUMED as food. Mirth here is not humour; it is the sound of digestion. The negative emotions are not banished from the state; they are the meal.

How to use it

Say it when something finally stops being infuriating — and notice the laugh that arrives is the verse working. Feeling-tip: the annoyance is suddenly chewable; that is bhujyate happening. Let the laugh be the proof.

The Essence of Laughter

The Essence of Laughter

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 2.18 · 2.18
Bowing to the Lord who sits on laughter itself.
What is happening

The universe is upahāsa-ekasāra — the essence of the cosmic joke — and the Lord is nitya-sukha-āsina, seated in eternal joy. How to use it

When life turns absurd: say it, and read the absurdity as his seat. Feeling-tip: irreverence without disrespect — the sigh turns into a chuckle, and the chuckle lands on him.

His Laughing-Play

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 3.15 · 3.15
Same ocean as everyone else's; for his servants it's a playground.

In a hard moment, name it: ocean. Then say the verse, and the water becomes shallow. Feeling-tip: the shoulders drop and the mouth lifts before the situation changes at all.

Angry, Then Laughing, Then Śiva

8
The Guide

Angry, Then Laughing, Then Śiva

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 16.7 · 16.7
The permission slip: devotion lets you rage, then laugh, then shout.
What is happening

The verse gives the full emotional sequence and blesses every step: bhakti-kṣīva (drunk on devotion) → kupyeyam (let me be angry!) → haseyaṃ (then laugh) → udyām (rise) → raṭeyam śivety (shout Śiva). Anger is not the failure of the practice but its middle passage — it converts to laughter to shout. Hāsa is the hinge where the negative becomes praise.

How to use it

When worked up: say it as an instruction manual — drunk first, then let it become laugh, rise, and shout. The last word must be Śiva, out loud if you dare. Feeling-tip: the anger doesn't get suppressed; it gets promoted.

Karuṇā

grief & sorrow
sthāyin: śoka — “all rasas are essentially pleasurable, even the painful” — grief is bliss in a minor key (Abhinavabhāratī)

The Seed of Sorrow

चित्रं निसर्गतो नाथ दुःखबीजम् इदं मनः / त्वद्भक्तिरससंसिक्तम् निःश्रेयसमहाफलम्
citraṃ nisargato nātha duḥkhabījam idaṃ manaḥ / tvadbhaktirasasaṃsiktam niḥśreyasamahāphalam
strange, lord: this mind is by nature the seed of sorrow —yet watered with the rasa of your devotionit ripens into the highest fruit.
9
The Guide

The Seed of Sorrow

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 1.26 · 1.26
The sorrow-seed that ripens instead of rotting.
What is happening

The verse concedes the grief outright: manaḥ is duḥkha-bīja by nature (nisargataḥ) — the mind was born a grief-seed. The surprise (citra!) is the alchemy: watered with bhakti-rasa it becomes niḥśreyasa-mahāphala, the highest fruit. Karuṇā is not healed by removal but by irrigation — the same seed, different harvest.

How to use it

In grief: say it and water the seed instead of cursing the soil. Feeling-tip: the grief stays, but it is being used; even the tears count as irrigation.

Sorrow Mixed with Ghee

How to use it

In pain: say the ghee line and literally imagine the sharp edge of the feeling being coated. Feeling-tip: the body responds to texture, not to reasons — the tightness loosens because the sharpness is now suspended.

The Guide

The Banner Against Suffering

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 11.14 · 11.14
The emergency prayer: grief addressed to the Destroyer-of-pain by name.

The Axe of 'All Is His'

एतन् मम न त्व् इदम् इति रागद्वेषादिनिगडदृढमूले / नाथ भवन्मयतैक्यप्रत्ययपरशुः पतत्वन्तः

'this is mine, this is not yours' — the roots of attachment and aversion, hard-shackled;o lord, let the axe of the one conviction 'all is your-become' fall on them in me.
13
The Guide

The Axe of 'All Is His'

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 7.2 · 7.2
The engine of rage is mine-ness; the axe is the conviction that all is his.
What is happening

Anger's roots are rāga-dveṣa driven by mamaka (mine-ness) — 'etan mama, na tv idam' (this is mine, this is not yours). The remedy named is an instrument: paraśu, the axe of ekatva-pratyaya — the conviction that everything is bhavanmaya, his-become. The verse PRAYS FOR THE AXE TO FALL — an act, not a mood.

How to use it

In rage: name the possession first — 'mine', 'not yours' — then say the verse at it. Feeling-tip: the fist unclenches not by relaxing but by losing the ownership record; the thing remains, the claiming ends.

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 16.15

Let the Anger Ripen

मानावमानरागादिनिष्पाकविमलं मनः / यस्यासौ भक्तिमांल् लोकतुल्यशीलः कथं भवेत्
mānāvamānarāgādiniṣpākavimalaṃ manaḥ / yasyāsau bhaktimāṃl lokatulyaśīlaḥ kathaṃ bhavet
the mind purified by the complete ripening ofhonour and dishonour, rāga and the rest —such a devotee: how could he have ordinary-world conduct?
14
The Guide

Let the Anger Ripen

Utpaladeva, Śivastotrāvalī 16.15 · 16.15
Pride, slight and rage treated as things that must fully ripen first.
What is happening

Māna, avamāna (honour and dishonour), rāga — these must NIṢPĀKA, ripen completely, like poison cooked out or fruit brought to sweetness. The purified mind is the one where the indignation-mix has finished its fermentation. The verse reframes the anger-process itself: give it heat and time, and the result is clarity instead of explosion.
